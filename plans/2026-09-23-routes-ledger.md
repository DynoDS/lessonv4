## Decisions for Daniel

Five questions, each a real pull between two of your rules, or a rule I think is wrong. Each says what the plugin says now, what I think and what I suggest; "yes" takes the suggestion.

1. **Moving about in lessons.**
   - **What it says now.** Your activity list says four corners and "stand if" are not used, and that whole-class movement happens only when you ask for it. But the discussion lessons still offer four corners as a way to talk and a "four-corners vote" as an opening activity. And two places tell the designer to have children stand and turn to learn what a quarter turn is. (RT-G05, G08, J33, J35, J36, P04, B39)
   - **What I think.** Four corners is the move-to-show routine you ruled out. Standing to make a turn is different: the movement is what is being learned, not a way of showing an answer, but as written it needs you to ask for it every time.
   - **What I suggest.** Four corners comes out of the discussion lessons (a ranking at the table, a line on paper from agree to disagree, or a vote with a written reason instead). Moving is allowed without your asking only when the movement is the thing being learned, like a quarter turn. Role-play, hot-seating and a short debate stay, as now, only after every child has written or chosen a side.
   - **Question:** is that the line you want?

2. **A good example before a big written task.**
   - **What it says now.** In a knowledge lesson, before children write an explanation or comparison, the board shows a good one beside a weak one, unless they have already seen a good one earlier in this same lesson; you tightened this after the leisure lesson, where children had written one before but never seen a good one. A big-task lesson asks a different question: have children made this kind of work earlier in this lesson? One of its lines drops "in this lesson" and just says "familiar". The check looks only at knowledge lessons. (RT-E30, E31, F18, F19, F34, Q16)
   - **What I think.** "Made one" is the gap the leisure lesson fell through. But a big-task lesson is chosen because the class already knows the form, so one rule for both means a good and a weak example on the board in every big-task lesson whose product is an explanation, even one the class writes every week.
   - **What I suggest.** One rule everywhere: the example is skipped only when the class has seen a good one earlier in this lesson, and the check covers big-task lessons and a skills lesson's bigger practice task too.
   - **Question:** is a good-and-weak pair worth its slide in every big-task lesson whose product is an explanation?

3 and 4. **Teaching slides in every kind of lesson** (the first list's 3 and 4, one question).
   - **What it says now.** Knowledge lessons follow your four parts: the key sentence, the because, the example the class looks at, and what it does not mean. The other kinds of lesson describe their teaching slides in an older three-part shape with no "what it does not mean". A skills lesson's teaching slide must also be brief on the board, with a worked example of what is too wordy, and a discovery lesson keeps its key sentence to the end because children reach it themselves. Since 12 September a skills lesson may use a teaching slide and a bigger practice task, but their rules are written only in the knowledge-lesson instructions, which a skills lesson never reads, while the check already holds its teaching slide to those rules. (RT-E45 to E49, C28, D21, F05, F29, H07, C23, C27, C30, P06, Q14)
   - **What I think.** Your four parts suit every teaching slide, and the two exceptions are real ones worth keeping. The designer of a maths lesson is being checked against rules it never reads.
   - **What I suggest.** Your four parts and the good-example rules for a big task are each written once, under their own heading, and every kind of lesson reads that heading when it has a teaching slide or a big task, the way every lesson already reads the one success-criteria section it needs. The two exceptions are written beside the four parts: kept brief in a skills lesson, and the key sentence at the end in discovery.
   - **Question:** yes?
