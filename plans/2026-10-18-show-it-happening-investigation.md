# Why "show it happening" lands in some lessons and not others

Investigation only, 18 October 2026. Nothing in the plugin was changed.

The question: on 18 October you ruled that when a slide explains how something happens, the picture must show it happening. A rule was added that day. In the test lessons built since, it landed in some places and not in others. Why?

---

## The short answer

- **The rule worked on its own in only one lesson out of three.** The other two "successes" were lessons whose own answer had been written into the rule as its example.
- **The designer read the rule every time, before it wrote anything.** So it is not that the rule arrived too late. It was in front of the designer and lost.
- **It lost to older, stronger guidance that points the other way.** The plugin's own worked example for "how something works" is the food chain lesson with one big photo and a picture-less explaining slide. The main picture rule says "show the thing" and "keep the same picture while the class is on the same thing". The new paragraph sits fourth in that section, as an add-on.
- **Nothing checks it.** The only picture check looks at drawn diagrams. A photo on an explaining slide, or a "where is it" diagram on a "how does it work" slide, is never compared with the words.
- **Made pictures did not fail.** Every made process picture that was asked for came back first time. The two refusals on 18 October were body outlines, not process pictures. The lesson that lost its pictures (digestion, second run) never asked for them.

---

## 1. What the test runs really show

Five designer-only runs, all on 18 October, all with the rule in place. I traced every one from its full record: what it read, in what order, and what it wrote.

| Run | Explaining step | What the designer chose | Did the rule carry this lesson's answer? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sound, lesson 2 | How sound travels through the air | A made picture of air as dots bunching up as a push passes along | **Yes.** The rule's own example is this exact slide ("the air's tiny parts pushing one another along from a drum to an ear") |
| Food chains, run 1 | How grass makes its own food | The photo of sunlit grass, reused from the slide before | No |
| Food chains, run 2 | How grass makes its own food | A made picture of sunlight into the leaves and water up the roots | **Yes.** Between run 1 and run 2 the rule gained the sentence "a food chains deck explaining that grass makes its own food from sunlight showed a photograph of sunlit grass ... a made picture of the sun's rays going into the leaves and water rising up the roots was the one he liked". The run 2 designer even wrote "because the teacher wanted the making shown, not just sunlit grass" |
| Digestion, run 1 | How the oesophagus moves food; what the stomach does | Made pictures of the oesophagus squeezing a lump of food, and the stomach churning food to mush | No. **This is the one real, unaided success** |
| Digestion, run 2 | The same two steps | The same labelled diagram on every teaching slide, gaining one label each time. The squeeze and the churn are in the words only | No (the grass example was there, but it is about a different lesson) |

**So, unaided: one landed, two missed.** The sound and second food chain results are what a test looks like when the answer is in the question. They show the designer will copy a worked example; they do not show it can make the judgement on a new lesson.

Your older cases fit the same pattern, from before the rule existed:
- 17 October, Codex, sound lesson 1: "back and forth" arrows floating beside a ruler photo. The picture was planned from what the plugin could draw, and the check passed it because it matched its own list.
- 17 October, Claude, sound lesson 2, slide 6: drum photo, arrow, a box saying "the air", arrow, ear photo. The picture's stated purpose was "the vibration being passed from one thing to the next", but what it had to contain was just boxes and arrows, and the check only compared the contents.

## 2. When the designer decided each picture, and what it had just read

**Every designer read the "shows it happening" paragraph before writing a word of the lesson.** In these runs the designer does all its reading at the start, then writes the whole lesson in one go. For example, food chains run 1 read the paragraph three times (in its own instructions at 13:46, in the rules file at 13:50, in the picture guide at 13:52) and started writing at 13:58. Both digestion runs were the same.

**The picture is actually chosen while writing the slide-by-slide walk-through**, in the line "On the board: ...". The designer's instructions for that line say a Teach slide is the words "beside the actual object, text, diagram or working they look at". That wording points at the thing, not the happening.

**The instruction to apply the visual rule comes later**, at the step called "complete the picture contract", after the whole walk-through is written. By then each slide's picture is already settled, and that step's own question is "walk your own units and ask of each what it names that exists in the world": again, the thing.

**So your timing worry is half right.** The rule was read early, so it was not missing. But at the two moments the picture is decided, the words in front of the designer both ask about the thing, and nothing at either moment asks "does this slide explain how something happens, and what must the children see happening?"

One limit on this finding: the designers' private reasoning was not recorded, so I can see what they read and what they wrote, not what they weighed in between.

## 3. What pulls the designer away from it

Five pulls, ranked by how strongly the evidence supports them.

**3.1 The worked example for "how something works" teaches the opposite. Strong evidence.**
The plugin's guide to how you explain, read just before the designer writes the lesson, gives one example for "how something works". It is the food chain opening, from your 14 October ruling: "one big photograph of a rabbit eating grass", ask where the rabbit gets its food, ask where the grass gets its food, then "the next board explains that grass makes its own food using sunlight". The example names a photo for the opening and no picture at all for the explaining board.
Food chains run 1 followed it almost word for word, and wrote "with the photograph on both".
Your 14 October ruling was about the order of teaching (familiar thing, question, explanation, word last), and it was right. But the example as written also teaches "photo, then words", and a worked example is the strongest thing in the designer's instructions.

**3.2 The main picture rule says "show the thing" and "keep the same picture". Strong evidence.**
The section the new paragraph lives in opens with "does this name something that exists in the world?" and then "the picture is there whenever its thing is being talked about", with "it repeats while the class is still working on that one thing". Both came from your 12 October rulings and both are right for what they were about.
Digestion run 2 wrote: "one plain digestive system picture carries every Teach board, gaining a label each time, so the class watches one body fill up." That is the repeat rule, applied to the digestive system as "one thing". The new paragraph is fourth in the section, after two long paragraphs that point the other way.

**3.3 The rule's own limit was read for the whole lesson, not for each step. Supported, not proven.**
The rule ends: "a name or a told fact needs only its thing". The digestion lesson's objective is "To name parts of digestive system", and run 2 wrote "A naming lesson". But its slides do explain how: "Its walls squeeze behind the food and push it along, like squeezing toothpaste" and "your stomach squeezes and churns it". The limit was meant step by step; it was read as a label for the whole lesson.
Run 1, on the same objective, made the opposite call and pictured both steps. So the same words allow both readings.

**3.4 A live demonstration stood in for the picture. Supported.**
Digestion run 2 moved the squeeze into a tennis ball pushed down a pair of tights, "on a prepared day". On any other day the class sees the labelled diagram and hears the words. A good demo is worth having, but it is optional, so it cannot be the only place the how is shown.

**3.5 The picture budget. Plausible, not proven.**
The designer has 16 pictures for the whole lesson. Food chains run 1 used exactly 16 (most of them animals for the chains and the worksheet). A made picture of grass making food would have meant dropping one. Run 2 trimmed the chains and used 15. Without the recorded reasoning I cannot say the budget decided it, only that run 1 was at the limit.

**Ruled out in these runs:**
- The slide planner reusing a picture. In both misses the designer itself asked for the reuse.
- The picture search order. The misses never asked for a made process picture, so search order never came into it.
- The made-picture route failing. See section 4.
- A preference for a ready-made drawing (a "helper"). Neither miss used one for the explaining step. That pull is real in the 17 October cases, but in these runs the pull was toward a photo or a labelled diagram instead. The common thread is the same: the picture was chosen from what was already in the lesson, not from what the step explains.

## 4. What a made picture costs, and what happens when it fails

**Cost.**
- Three made process pictures were asked for across the runs (oesophagus squeezing, stomach churning, grass making food). **All three were made on the first try** and accepted.
- Pictures are made in parallel with the rest of the picture search. Each picture batch finished in roughly two to five minutes. A made picture adds little time.
- It does use one of the 16 picture places (section 3.5).

**The refusals on 18 October.**
- Two pictures in digestion run 2 were refused twice each by the image tool's safety filter: a plain child's body outline (for "find the stomach") and a second digestive system diagram (for the final labelling check).
- **Neither was a process picture.** The plan asked to see the rib line and belly button "on the bare tummy" of a child figure, and the filter read that as unsafe.
- In the test, I rescued them by hand with a mannequin-style figure. In a real run nothing would have rescued them: those two slides would have shown words with no picture.
- Body process pictures can work: the oesophagus and stomach pictures in digestion run 1 came through first time, because they show the organ, not a child.

**What a lesson is left with when a made process picture fails.**
- The plan marks a made process picture as "if this fails, leave it", so the slide shows its words with nothing in the picture's place. There is no stand-in (for example, a simpler drawn version or a labelled photo).
- This was already logged as an open risk on 18 October.
- One more small pull: in the picture guide, the sentence telling the designer a process picture is made goes straight into a warning that a made picture "costs the whole picture on a host with no generation route". A careful designer reading that might lean back to a photo.

## 5. Can the designer judge this from prose alone?

**On this evidence, not reliably.** One unaided success out of three, with the rule read in full every time.

**But it is a genuine judgement, not a rule you can count.** Section 6 shows why: many slides should not get a made picture. So a hard "every explaining slide must have a made picture" would be wrong.

**What is missing is that the judgement is never written down where anything can see it.**
- Each slide records its words, its purpose and its picture. Nothing records "this step explains how X happens, and the children must see Y happening".
- The only picture check (the helper check) looks at drawn diagrams. It now prints each diagram's purpose beside what it must show, which is good, but:
  - a photo on an explaining slide is never looked at (food chains run 1);
  - a diagram whose purpose is "where each part sits" is judged against that purpose and passes, even when the slide's words explain a squeeze (digestion run 2).
- The one reviewer did not run in these tests, so it is untested here. Its reading list includes the picture section, but nothing in its own instructions points it at this question.

**Your standing rulings fit this.** No caps, one reviewer, no new review stage, fix the cause upstream. The cause is upstream: the decision is made at a moment when nothing asks the question, and it leaves no trace a check could read.

## 6. Where an obvious fix would over-fire

These are the cases a fix must leave alone. The test runs got most of them right, and that must not change.

| Case | Right picture | Seen in the runs |
|---|---|---|
| A route or order is the point | A route diagram with arrows | Both food chain runs drew grass, rabbit, fox as photos joined by arrows. Right. "Is Zofia's chain right?" and the journey-fixing task in digestion run 2 are routes. Right |
| A name, or where something is | The thing, or a labelled diagram | "Where is your stomach?", "What's behind your belly button?", the labelling checks, the eardrum cutaway in sound. All right as diagrams |
| A told fact | The thing it is about | "Rabbits live in burrows" with a rabbit photo. Right |
| A real event, person or place | The photograph rules (search first; a made picture only when nothing is found, and called a picture) | The astronaut eating in space. Right |
| Scale or comparison | Whatever makes the comparison visible | The red and blue wool for the intestines. Right |
| **A step whose how the lesson explains** | **The thing happening** | Sound (air pushing along), grass making food, oesophagus squeezing, stomach churning. Only these |

**In numbers:** across these decks, the steps that genuinely explain a how are about one or two slides per lesson. A fix that made more than that into made pictures would be over-firing.

## 7. What I am confident about, and what I am not

**Confident (seen directly in the records):**
- Every designer read the rule before writing.
- Two of the three successes had their own answer in the rule.
- Digestion run 2 chose one repeated diagram on purpose and called itself a naming lesson.
- Food chains run 1 followed the worked example's "photo, then words" shape.
- All three made process pictures were made first time. The refusals were body outlines.
- No check compares a photo, or a diagram's purpose, with what the slide's words explain.

**Supported but not proven (reasoning was not recorded):**
- That the worked example and the repeat rule are what tipped each miss, rather than chance.
- That the picture budget mattered in food chains run 1.

**Not tested:**
- Whether the one reviewer would have caught the misses.
- Five runs is a small sample. A clean way to settle the main question: run the same new lesson (one the rule's examples do not mention, such as how a shadow forms or how water evaporates) a few times as it stands.

## 9. Daniel's answers (18 October 2026, talked through one at a time)

All six findings agreed. Nothing changed in the plugin yet.

1. **The tests flatter the rule.** Agreed: unaided it worked one time in three. When testing, he wants 3 out of 3, on lessons whose answer is not already written into the rule's examples.
2. **No reminder at the moment the picture is chosen.** Agreed: when it picks each slide's picture it should ask "this slide explains how X happens, so what must the children see?" rather than "this slide is about X, so show X".
3. **The worked example teaches "photo, then words".** Agreed: the example should show the picture of the grass making food, not just the rabbit photo.
4. **"Keep the same picture" beat "show it happening".** Agreed: when a slide explains how something happens, showing it happening wins over keeping the same picture.
5. **Made pictures are not the problem.** Agreed: they work and cost little, so ask for them; but a failed one should leave something useful on the slide, not just words.
6. **Nothing checks it.** Agreed: the choice is never written down, so nothing can catch it when it is missed. Not a blanket rule: only the one or two slides per lesson that explain a how.

Next: propose the fix, one decision at a time.

## 10. The fix Daniel agreed (18 October 2026, one decision at a time)

Already agreed in section 9 and included: the "how something works" worked example shows the grass-making-food picture, not only the rabbit photo; on a slide that explains a how, "show it happening" wins over "keep the same picture", judged step by step, never for the whole lesson.

1. **Write it down.** Every slide that explains how something happens gets one extra line when it is planned: what the children must see happening (grass: "They see: sunlight going into the leaves and water coming up the roots, so the grass makes its own food"). Slides that name, locate or show an order (a food chain) get no line, so it is one or two slides per lesson.
2. **Who checks it, with no new stage.** The automatic check confirms each slide with a "they see" line has its own picture for it, not one reused from another slide. The one reviewer reads the line beside the requested picture and asks whether the picture shows that happening, and also looks for a slide that explains a how with no line.
3. **Backup when the picture cannot be made.** Try once more as a plain drawing with arrows; if that fails, use the ordinary photo of the thing and tell the teacher in the report so he can draw the arrows on the board.
4. **Pictures of a child's body.** Keep the newer calm-wording rule (added after the test runs, in the picture request guide). Add one line: a child's body is shown clothed (plain T-shirt) or as a simple mannequin shape, parts drawn on top. Reason: on 18 October the image service made the picture and then blocked the finished image (output stage), so wording alone would not have helped.
5. **Testing.** Three "how" lessons in different subjects, all must pass (3 of 3): Year 3 science, how a shadow forms; Year 4 geography, how a volcano erupts (cut-away, not a mountain photo); Year 2 history, how the Great Fire of London spread (real event: an artist's picture, never a made picture passed off as real). One control that must not fire: RE or PSHE, e.g. why Christians celebrate Christmas (real photos only, no made "happening" pictures). Plugin plans and builds slides; decks shown to Daniel to judge.

Not built yet.

## 11. Built and tested (18 October 2026, evening, uncommitted)

**Built in the plugin's working copy (nothing committed or released)**
- Each slide plan can carry a "they see" line (`seesHappening`): what the class sees happening and which of the slide's own pictures shows it, with an optional plain stand-in picture. The scaffold writes an empty one on every slide.
- The design check refuses a "they see" picture already used on an earlier slide (a diagram counts as used in any of its labellings), and a picture request for it must begin "Shows it happening:".
- Rules file: the paragraph rewritten (three forms of the mistake, write it down, judged step by step, one or two slides a lesson); "keep the same picture" now gives way on a slide that explains how part of the thing works.
- The rabbit and grass worked example now shows the grass-making-food picture.
- The lesson designer writes "They see:" under the board when it plans an explaining slide, and checks it again at the picture contract.
- The reviewer reads each line against its picture and looks for an explaining slide with no line; its view now prints the line.
- The slide planner puts the happening picture on the slide that explains it.
- Backup: a happening picture's second try is a plain flat drawing with arrows; if it still fails, the slide shows the stand-in and the run report tells the teacher.
- A person's body is asked for clothed or as a mannequin shape.
- Tests: 14 new; 11 pinned paragraphs refreshed on purpose. Full suite 3,069 pass; builder 1,234 pass.

1. Every picture request says "photorealistic", even a drawing of something happening; it cost the shadows picture its first try.

**Daniel's rulings on the three side findings (18 October 2026), built untested at his word ("dont worry about tests or checks")**
1. "Photorealistic": a picture whose request begins "Shows it happening:" is now asked for as a clear, simple, flat-coloured textbook-style drawing from the first try; every other picture stays photorealistic (picture compiler).
2. Picture names: he preferred that the slides "just know" the name rather than a check catching it. Naming pictures by number was the first choice, but about 50 places across slides, sheets, wall and stick-ins read picture names, too big to change without tests, so the agreed fallback was built: every plan writes its picture names as one folder and a name (the design check refuses a bare name), the plan's guide says so, and the slide planner is told to copy names exactly, folder and all, whatever its examples show.
3. Great Fire slide 5: left as it is.
