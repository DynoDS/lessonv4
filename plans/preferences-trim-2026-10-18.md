# Preferences trim, 18 October 2026

The teacher agreed (18 October 2026) to trim `plugins/lesson-v4/references/preferences.md`: rules against generic AI writing cut back to pointers where `teacher-voice.md` already owns them, long incident stories shortened to a clause, and rules a check enforces said more briefly. Every ruling was kept. This file keeps each changed paragraph exactly as it read before, under its heading, so the full story behind a ruling is never lost. No agent reads this file.


## From: Core rules

**Write for this class, and for the child in it who understands least.** This teacher's classes work well below their age: a Year 4 class mostly understands like a much younger one (in his words, "the key stage two children are really key stage one"), many children have SEND or are learning English, and they meet each topic knowing nothing about it: assume no topic knowledge and no adult experience of work, money, laws, institutions or public life. Year 6 is the ceiling for primary language and ideas; work down from it for younger years and never above it, so a sentence that would sit comfortably in a secondary textbook is not yet teaching these children, however true it is. A child can read every word and still not understand the sentence, so write for understanding, not decodability, from the first whole-class explanation. Keep the intended curriculum learning and worthwhile thinking: the challenge goes in what children think, not in harder wording. That lowers the words, never what is taught: a Year 4 class still meets Year 4 science, history and geography, with real facts, numbers and things that surprise them, and it is spoken to as a class that can think hard about something it has simply never met. So read every board line as you read a sticky fact (`Would a child have needed this lesson to say it?`): a line that tells a Year 4 child what any Year 4 child already knows, as if it were new (`Every living thing needs food to live and grow.`, `You chewed it and swallowed it.`), or that only points at the picture (`Look at this rabbit.`), talks down to them, and what they already know is the way in as a question they answer. A science deck of 18 October 2026 built that way read to the teacher as fit for Year 2 or 3 ("there's something about it that still feels kind of babyish"), though every word in it was a Year 4 word. For children with SEND or learning English, access is not tiny sentences, which read as robotic and hide the links between ideas: it is naming the people and things, saying the cause and effect out loud, grounding each abstract word in something they can picture, and taking away anything they would have to decode before they can think. Children's own lives are the way in, and whatever today stands on from an earlier lesson is told again before it is needed, simply enough for a child who has forgotten it or was away.

**Sound like the real teacher, not like generated text.** The register itself - natural, direct, warm, professionally credible, and the "would this sound comfortable with the headteacher watching?" boundary - is calibrated in `teacher-voice.md` → Core voice; write to that. What survives any register: mix short, medium and, when the idea needs it, longer sentences, and do not turn everything into punchy fragments, a list of tiny statements or compressed headline language merely because that looks tidy on a slide. Write with the contractions natural speech uses (`don't`, `it's`, `we're`): full forms everywhere (`do not`, `cannot`, `it is`) are one of the quickest tells of generated text, so keep them for genuine emphasis (`Do not touch the wire.`) or a formal test register that truly calls for them.

**Definitions should be simple, accurate and useful rather than artificially dictionary-like.** One clear sentence is normal; two short connected sentences are fine when the second gives a genuinely helpful example or experience. `Friction is the force that slows things down when two surfaces rub together. You can feel it when you rub your hands together.` is acceptable when that extra example earns its space.

**Use examples and analogies that genuinely bridge understanding.** Familiar and concrete is good; stereotypical `child-friendly` is not. Do not automatically reach for sweets because the audience is children, football because the children are boys, or friendship/birthday-party scenarios because they sound youthful. Use a familiar comparison only when these children can reasonably understand the link. A strange analogy creates another concept to decode instead of removing one.

**Avoid recognisable AI habits when they are serving the writing rather than the lesson.** Repeated `Here's the tricky part`, `Let's explore`, `Your task is to`, forced enthusiasm, generic motivational language, perfectly symmetrical mini-lists, needless narration of the lesson sequence and repeated sentence templates should trigger a reread. None of those phrases is individually forbidden. Repetition, generic use and lack of purpose are the problem.

## From: Classroom Norms

**Time: a whole lesson runs about 45 to 50 minutes.** The teacher's rough picture of where they go (12 October 2026; every figure is approximate and none is a rule on its own): about 5 on books out, the date and objective, and moving to tables and back; about 15 to 20 of the teacher teaching; a quick task 2 to 3 minutes and a longer one in books or on a sheet 5 to 10, whichever the task needs; and about 15 for a worksheet when the lesson ends on one. When children are expected to work independently afterwards, the taught input is about 30. Every sequence is designed against that picture: the per-beat timings in this file (a starter five to eight minutes, a Do beat one to three, a Talk three to five) have to sum to the slot with those 5 minutes left over. One line of it is enforced: the beats' honest minutes and the 5 no beat plans may not pass 50, and the design check refuses a lesson that does. An arc can be excellent beat by beat and still not fit, because each component earns its place separately and nothing else pushes back; this budget is the pushback. The rest of the picture is for seeing where a lesson has spent its room, which the total hides. A Year 5 history lesson (7 October 2026) added up to 39 minutes: two partner source readings of 7 and 8 minutes, then a third new idea, then a worksheet timed at 9. The teacher's read was that the class would be lost by the third idea, and that the same idea might have worked after two quick tasks. The readings were right for the objective; they had used the room the third idea needed, and the 9 was the worksheet made to fit. `do-beats-in-a-row.py` prints these minutes beside the picture and marks both of those for a second look. So decide in this order. Choose each task for the thinking it gets from children, quick or long as that needs. Then read how full the lesson is. When it is too full, an idea gives way, never the tasks: one the objective can be met without is left out or kept for another lesson and named in the closing decisions. An idea the objective needs stays, because a lesson that no longer meets its objective has wasted its time; if every idea is needed and the honest sum still does not fit, that is the two-lesson judgement in "How Much Fits in One Lesson" below. This goes wrong in two directions, and the second is the one a full lesson invites: ideas kept by timing the worksheet short or shaving each beat, and tasks chosen quick because there is a lot to teach, which leaves thin tasks and more listening (`Make every child do the thinking`). When the honest sum doesn't fit, cut a beat rather than compressing them all: a squeezed beat teaches nothing and rushes every beat around it, and choosing what goes is part of the design.

**No number with a 6 straight before a 7.** The "6-7" playground meme sets the class off whenever the two digits sit side by side, so no number a child reads or hears holds them, with commas ignored: 67, 670, 167, 6,742 and 6,705 are all out. That covers every number the lesson writes: examples, answers, the working between them and the wrong answers a task offers, so check a calculation's answer and each exchanged digit, not only the numbers in the question (6,354 - 187 fails on its answer, 6,167). Choose numbers that keep clear of it from the start, because a set chosen for its exchanges has to be rebuilt when one of them is swapped late. A real year (1667) is a fact and stays, and so does a number inside a counting run such as a hundred square's rows. The design check and every builder refuse the rest.

**Routine classroom management belongs to the live teacher.** Generated slides, notes and task briefs state the learning action only, such as "Write...", "Discuss...", "Draw..." or "Choose...". They never say where children record it (a whiteboard, a mini-whiteboard, a book) and do not name a routine participation method: the teacher chooses books, whiteboards, devices, hands up, cold calling, wait time or another routine in the live classroom, and a board that says `on your whiteboard` has made that choice for a class the designer has never seen (the teacher's ruling, 5 October 2026: "It should never say whiteboards or books or anything like that. That's up to teacher"). The design validator refuses the wording on the board, in the script and in the teacher's orientation. What is still named is a thing the lesson itself puts in children's hands (the printed sheet, the cards, the map) and a device or response structure that is genuinely the task. Where every child has to commit an answer before talking, the board asks the question and its reason (`Who has painted more? Explain why.`), and the pencil sign beside it says that writing is one way to answer. Whether the class writes it or the teacher takes it aloud is then the teacher's call on the day. The word `Write` on the board takes that call away, because a class reads it as an order (the teacher, on a Year 5 deck whose task read `Write a name.`, 13 October 2026: "teacher would ask the question instead, and that means children don't have to write it ... If it says Write though then it does"). So `Write` opens a board line when writing is the task itself (a sentence to compose, a calculation to set out, a label to add), and a bare thing to put down (`Write a name.`, `Write one word.`) is written as the question it stands for. The script may still tell children to get an answer down before they talk. A board line that only says to write the answer (`Write your answer.`) is left out: the pencil sign on the question says it (`templates.md` → Signs), and the line costs the board a card.


## From: What a Lesson Is For

**Needed is not tidy.** The quickest way to make a final task visibly need the teaching is to sharpen every example into a clean contrast: Christians sing the carol for Jesus, everyone else for family. That makes the dependency obvious on the page and teaches a sort that is false, because the same person can hold the belief and love the singing, and the lesson's own point was that you cannot read one from the other. A rerun of a Year 4 RE lesson fixed its spine this way and lost the line its first version had, that one person can value both. So when the idea being taught is that one thing carries more than one meaning, or that one thing cannot be read from another, the set of examples includes a case that holds both, and the lesson says so out loud. The learning has to be needed by the task; it does not have to be made binary to be needed.

**Would a child have needed this lesson to say it?** The three questions can each be answered in full with something worthless, and every check will pass. A Year 4 history design named `Babies had rattles in Tudor times, and babies still have rattles today` as the learning children carry away, wrote `What is different about these two toys?` as the thought, and ended with a task that used both, and its reviewer approved it: a child could have said the fact before the lesson began and answered the question from the picture without any history. So the fourth test runs on the answers to the other three. For each fact the lesson claims children will carry away, and for the clause after `because` in the opening decision sentence: could a child in this class have said it before the lesson? If yes, it is not the learning, and naming it as sticky has turned the final task into recall of the slide, because a sticky fact that is the sentence the final task expects is the answer handed over early. For each `thinking` line: could a child answer it from what is on the board, or from what they knew walking in? If yes, the beat is reading or guessing, whatever it is called. Every subject has a doing that passes for thinking because the objective names it: using sources, using a map, recording results, calculating, writing a sentence with the feature in. The doing is the channel; the thinking is the decision or inference the doing needs, and each subject file names its own version, so read every thinking line against the subject file's test as well as against the slide. The limit: a retrieval starter is meant to be what children already know, and fluency practice repeats a known move on purpose. A quick check is not exempt: it is short, and it still has to need what the Teach just taught (`A quick check is a fresh case, not the last slide again`).

**When the learning is an idea, name it, and the evidence changes while the idea holds still.** The three questions have three kinds of answer. A fact (why the fire spread; what most Tudor children were doing at seven), a method (partition, then add), or an idea: a way of seeing that transfers to cases the lesson never showed. Continuity and change, cause, what a source can and cannot tell, a pattern with a reason, a fair test, the same practice carrying different meanings. Facts and methods have had slots in the lesson file from the start; ideas had one only in maths-style skill lessons. So a designer handed an idea-shaped objective put it into fact-shaped slots, and a Year 4 history lesson on continuity and change named `tiny pewter plates were made for play` as its learning, held one pair of plates for nine slides, and invented an age sum to make the plates "needed"; every rule above was obeyed and the idea was never taught. Now a knowledge lesson names its idea in `concepts`, in any route, and each beat that is an instance of it says so. An idea is learned across meaningful instances: the question stays the same while the evidence changes. For continuity and change, two pictures under one comparison question, then a new pair, then another, is the teacher's intended shape. Keep that movement across cases. Independent Practise may use an accessible new instance, or ask for a new comparison, selection of evidence or judgement across the instances already taught. Name what remains for children to work out: repeating an observation, finding the teacher's words or reproducing a supplied conclusion does not establish an independent judgement. Unseen facts are not compulsory. New factual context must be intelligible, and required curriculum content must be taught rather than held back to manufacture freshness. Guided immediate rehearsal remains useful before independent work: mark it as rehearsal and say what it secures (`output-template.md` → `use`). A lesson holds one say-it-back Do or quick check at most (the teacher, 6 October 2026: "two is too many, one at most"); `output-template.md` → `use` says what is not counted. The limit is the kind of learning, not a quota. A lesson whose learning is a fact keeps one well-developed case, and nothing here touches it. A lesson that has named an idea does not develop one case nine times, because an idea shown on one case is a fact about that case.

**Read the final task backwards as moves, not facts.** Each step of the final task that is a move (compare, explain a difference, justify a choice, weigh two reasons) names the beat where children first made that move with support, on the lesson's own material. A fact well taught does not prepare a move never made: producing two meanings for a star prepares producing meanings, and it does not prepare comparing your own meaning with someone else's. A move whose first performance is the final task is a stage the lesson is missing, and a prepared model paragraph is the product shown, not the move practised. The same RE rerun added a comparison to its writing and never had the class compare anything before it; a well-taught fact was carrying a step it could not carry.


## From: The Teach → Do → Teach → Do Rhythm

**A lesson holds two or three Dos, then its final task.** Every new idea is still used before the next one arrives (the rhythm above), but a Do comes in two sizes. A task is real work children are set to: cards to sort, a picture to label, a story to order, a prediction to test, a source to read, something to write, with its own setting-up and its answers gone through. A quick check is a minute in which every child commits one thing (a word, a letter, a yes or no, a prediction) with no setting-up, and the next board or the teacher's demonstration answers it. It is planned at two minutes or less, and a Do planned longer is a task however it is described: a four-minute rice test with its equipment was counted as quick on 30 September 2026, which put its lesson one task over. A beat with a printed sheet is a task too, however short: handing out a sheet, doing it and going through it is not a minute, and a Shaftesbury lesson that printed its one-minute check felt like four tasks to the teacher who taught it (1 October 2026). So a check that should stay quick stays on the board, and a beat worth a sheet takes one of the two or three. The two or three counts the tasks. After the starter, the ordinary content lesson runs Teach → task, Teach → task, then one beat that readies the final task, then the final task itself. The readying beat is whichever the task needs: for an objective that asks children to explain, the good and weak example with how to get it done and the partner rehearsal, which together are one beat, not three; otherwise a last short Teach that ties the parts together before the sheet. Teach → task three times, the third being the final task, is the most a lesson normally holds; going past it is the sign to look again at the amount, not a wall to hide things behind. Count the chunks of teaching as well as the tasks, because a chunk checked by a quick check is still a chunk: usually two pieces of teaching, each checked by a quick check or a task, then a short lead-in (here is the task, here is a good one) and the final task with independent time. Three chunks of teaching with a final task on top is usually too much (the teacher, 18 October 2026, of a sound lesson that taught the air, then the outer ear and eardrum, then the ear to the brain, each with its check, before its explanation task). It is the picture a judgement starts from, not a template, and the other routes keep their own shapes. A chunk is counted in ideas, so two is never a reason to pack more new things into one: each new thing the objective needs is still checked as it arrives, by a quick check when that is all it needs, and parts of one idea checked one by one (each part of a journey, each member of a set) stay one chunk. When the lesson still holds too much, the answer is fewer things, never bigger chunks: a digestion lesson of 18 October 2026 told the mouth, teeth, saliva, oesophagus and stomach before children did anything, and the teacher said "I feel like I've explained a lot and they haven't done anything". The tasks are where children stop listening and start using what they have been taught (the teacher, 30 September 2026: "a chance for that cognitive break from just listening and learning and to start applying"): an educational break, something different from sitting and listening while the teacher stops talking, most often working with a partner or a group on something (ordering, sorting, testing, building, deciding together), sometimes alone. A written answer or "tell your partner" after every Teach keeps children sitting and listening in all but name. So each task has to feel like doing something, and the lesson has room for only a few of them. Count what the class does, not what the plan calls it: the starter is not counted; the example, rehearsal and writing of an explanation task count once; an Apply after the final task counts; and a written piece children start once a sort or an order is done, with its own model or a switch from pairs to each child writing alone, is a second task inside the first and counts as a task of its own, because the teacher counts the writing as a task of its own (1 October 2026) and the class lives it as one: a renga lesson's main work put six stanza cards in order in pairs, then had each child write one join from a good and weak model, and read as long (2 October 2026). A short reason given while sorting, on the same go, is part of the sort. Quick checks are not counted as tasks, and they go wherever a new idea arrives with no task after it. A quick check checks what was just taught and teaches nothing new (bringing out what children wrongly think before the answer is taught, `Have you heard the sound yet?`, is still checking): a check that brings in the next idea (rice jumping on a stretched cling film, which the eardrum is then taught from) is the start of the next chunk, and counts with it. Quick checks still cost time, so a run of them is not free (the teacher, 18 October 2026: fifty quick checks could fill a whole lesson and still leave only three tasks counted, "and that doesn't make sense"), and they never make room for more ideas, because the amount is the chunks of teaching. A long teaching run is where one belongs: a Year 4 volcanoes lesson told the farmer's field, the eruption, the word magma and magma rising through a crack over five slides before its first task, and the teacher agreed that was too long (30 September 2026). A quick check still makes children think, a prediction the next board answers or a fresh case in one word with the answer off the board, which a child who missed the Teach could not settle by common sense, never the last slide's words said back, because quicker is not the aim (the teacher, 29 September 2026: "quicker doesnt always mean effective"). When the learning seems to need more tasks, the answer is fewer ideas, a quick check, teaching a thing through a task itself (as when children put the story's pictures in order and meet the story that way rather than hearing it told), or two lessons, with what is left named in the closing decisions (`How Much Fits in One Lesson`). Never tasks squeezed shorter, never a needed idea quietly dropped so the final task asks for something nobody taught, and never a second idea folded into one Teach to stay inside the count: that is the overload the count exists to stop, moved onto one slide (`One concept per Teach block, then back to the children`). Two Year 4 lessons of 29 and 30 September 2026 gave children seven things to do each (a naming lesson with five Do beats, its labelling and an Apply; the Nativity with four tasks, a good and weak example, partner talk and the writing), and the teacher lost the class in the second. Skill-based maths keeps its I do, we do, you do cycles (`subject-maths.md`); every other route carries this same amount in its own beats.

These name the thinking, not the format. `do-beats.md` holds the formats for each and is where you compare the plausible ones, and the subject file's `What a Do beat looks like` names the forms that thinking takes in that subject (a place marked on a map, a practice matched to its belief), so a beat in a content subject is not left to default to talk; this list is what you settle before you open either. Settling the kind is not yet settling the thought: `complete the because` is on this list, and picked after a slide that already stated the because, the thought a child actually has is where to copy the words from. So before opening the catalogue, write the beat's own `thinking` line: the question a child's mind is answering while they work, in that child's terms. Then read it against what the slide will show and against what the class knew walking in. If the line can be answered by reading the board, choose again, because copying is what will be remembered (`What a Lesson Is For`, `Three questions, in this order`). A line a child could have answered before the lesson is guessing, and gets the same answer. A chunk can be more than one of these, and the choice between them is a real decision about what today's learning most needs. **A quick check is a fresh case, not the last slide again.** A short check straight after teaching is a real Do beat when children apply what was just taught to something the Teach did not show: a new picture to place, a new card to sort, a new example to judge, a new situation to predict (the teacher's ruling). A case the class already knows, carried on with one thing changed, is a fresh case too when children have to work out what the change does: the same two cousins, twelve hours later. Set it with the scene said again on its own slide in short plain sentences, one fact each, and the question last, which is the wording the teacher asked for (12 October 2026): `Jun is in the UK. Noah is in New Zealand, on the other side of the world. It is midnight for Jun. Is it light or dark where Noah is?` The slide he was shown leaned on an earlier one (`the picture shows where they were at the first call`) and carried story added to make the task look different (`Jun wakes up and sends Noah a message`), and he found it confusing: a child was left to rebuild the set-up and had more to read with nothing more to think about. What makes such a task new is the thing that changed, so `use.new` names that, even when every word of it has been used; the design check then leaves it to the reviewer, and never needs detail added to the task. Choosing, completing or repeating the sentence the slide or the teacher has just said is finding it, not checking it: a child gets it right by remembering where the words were, the teacher learns only who was listening, and calling the beat a check does not change what the child does. That holds for an option bank whose right answer is the Teach's own sentence reworded, and for a wrong option no child this age would believe. **New is not enough: the case has to need today's teaching.** Before keeping any Do, quick or long, answer it as a child who missed the Teach, using only everyday sense and what is on the board. If that child gets it right, the beat is guessing or reading, however new its cards. A sort of things children use into Tudor, both, or today, with a games console, a wooden spoon and a ball among the cards, has every card new, and any child can place them all, because everyone knows a console is modern. A check asking which of two children, one before a law and one after, had the better life, over a timeline that already prints the law between them, is answered by the board. Beats of both kinds passed review as fresh cases and the teacher found them easy (1 October 2026). What needs the lesson is a case where the taught reason decides and common sense does not: cards a child can only place by asking the question the Teach answered; pairs where children name what stayed the same and what changed; a case whose answer turns on what the taught rule actually covered (a child just outside the law's age range); two records made visible side by side, such as two days shaded hour by hour and the difference counted. So when a candidate fails, change the cards or the case until the taught idea is what decides, or pick a different beat; do not keep it because it is quick. The subject's own work is not exempt either. Reading a source or comparing a picture from then with one from now is the channel, and the question asked of it is the beat: `find the word that says what the shoe is made of` is answered by a child who can read, and `what has changed between these two classrooms?` by a child with eyes, while `what does a child's shoe found in the soldiers' houses tell us about who lived at the fort?` or `why could a classroom today have this, when a Victorian one couldn't?` needs what was taught. A task that brings new evidence in, rather than checking a Teach, has no earlier teaching for a child to have missed, so the same test asks whether its question makes children work something out from the evidence that the lesson then uses, with nothing on the board already saying it. `Circle the shoes that belonged to children` is spotting; `children's shoes were found in the soldiers' houses: what does that tell us about who lived at the fort?` is the thinking, and it is lost again when the line above the picture says `soldiers' families lived at the fort too`. Finding the words in a real person's account that prove what their life was like is not spotting: it is history's own evidence work, and the teacher finds it a fine task (1 October 2026). What makes a case fresh depends on what it checks: for a procedure, fresh values can be enough when carrying out the procedure is itself the target; for reasoning, inference or explanation, a different name, picture or claim earns freshness only when children must examine or use its relevant features to reach an answer, and reusing the taught conclusion while ignoring the new material is weak evidence. The same conclusion can be valid in two cases when each requires that work; do not force different answers. A fact, a name or a definition may be recalled straight after teaching once the answer is no longer on show, because that makes every child produce it and tells the teacher who has it, and it claims recall, never understanding. A check that an idea, a relationship or a method's decision has landed is a fresh case. The difference is whether the answer is still in front of the child: after the enamel slide, `cover the board: what is the hard outer layer called?` is recall, `which of these says what enamel does?` with the Teach's own sentence as the right option is finding, and `which of these three teeth has lost its enamel?` is the fresh case. Saying what a beat is for is honest when it changes what the beat claims or what is done to the task (a check of unaided work takes its support away, a supported rehearsal says it is not assessing, a reused task says it is consolidation). It never lets a label stand in for the fresh case: after teaching the key on a map of Brazil, the check is reading the key on a map of Kenya, and calling a re-read of the Brazil map a quick check does not make it one. A retrieval starter is still meant to use what children already know, and practice that repeats a method on new numbers is still practice. Let the activity take the time its thinking genuinely needs, but do not let a beat intended as a quick check quietly expand until it crowds out the lesson. When a worthwhile sort, explanation, diagram or other activity becomes substantial, treat it as main practice rather than continuing to call it a tiny Do beat.

**Make every child do the thinking, and take away only the reading and writing that is not the learning.** A Do beat is where each child works out, remembers or uses the idea just taught, and children remember what they had to work at: quick is not the aim, and a smaller answer is not a better beat. Two separate questions decide its form. First, what does the child have to work out, and does this task make every child actually work it out? Producing something (finishing a sentence with the idea, placing six cards, labelling a new picture, completing a chain) pulls the idea from memory; picking one of two options is mostly recognising it, and a child can be right by luck half the time, so a choice between two earns its place only when the choice itself is the hard part (a near miss the misunderstanding would pick), and otherwise the beat gets more decisions (several cards, several cases) or the because. `Which end is the source, A or B?` becomes `Label the source, the mouth and a tributary on this new river`. Second, is anything in the way that is not the learning? A long passage read alone, many subject words to spell, a sentence to compose where the thought is a placement: in this class (well below its age, with many SEND and EAL children) the child who understood but writes slowly then looks the same as the child who did not. Take that load away and keep the thinking: short cards with a picture, read aloud first when the reading is only the route in; a word bank; a sentence to finish rather than compose. Reading and writing stay whenever they are the thinking or the subject's own work: finishing a sentence with the idea, sorting cards children read, a historical source searched for what it tells us, a results table, a map key, an RE extract, an explanation the objective asks for, a method children record (the teacher, 29 September 2026: `a good do beat like finish the sentence or sorting cards they read is still effective`). The test lessons of 28 September 2026 showed both failures in turn: first about half the Do beats asked for writing where the thought was a choice, then, once that was named, beats shrank to `write A or B`. In a quick check, each child commits their own answer (a letter, a mark, a placed card, a word, a finished sentence) before any talk: an idea only said to a partner lets one child think for two, the teacher cannot see who has it, and a lesson whose checks are all `tell your partner your idea` becomes the discussion over and over this teacher has turned down. Talk that generates ideas or rehearses a sentence before writing needs no commit first (`Partner talk is acceptable in any subject`). A quick beat asks for a spoken reason when the reason is what separates understanding from a guess; a written reason belongs to main practice and the final task. Varying how children answer matters as much here as anywhere: a lesson whose every Do is `decide, then tell your partner why` has swapped one habit for another. A final task with several cases keeps every case and sizes the writing: children talk each one through with a partner and write two (their choice, or one given and one chosen), so the thinking stays whole and a Year 4 page of four written reasons does not become the writing load that is not the learning (the teacher, 29 September 2026). How the teacher sees every answer stays the teacher's routine, and this is not a licence for stand-up or show-me routines, which he does not use.

**Use variety deliberately, without a quota.** Choose worthwhile pupil activity as carefully as the teaching example. Consider plausible alternatives for the particular learning before settling on a response: what will children inspect, handle, decide, rehearse, construct or explain, and why does that action help them learn this idea? Read the planned activities together from the child's side. A run of descriptions and explanations can be monotonous even when every prompt uses the right knowledge; changing the verb or moving the same explanation from writing to a partner does not by itself change the experience. Nor does changing the answer's format: write, sort, tick and write again is one experience when every task is answered alone, where the child sits, off the board, and several hand back what the teacher has just said. A Year 4 RE lesson on why the Nativity matters to Christians ran exactly that way (a starter, a true-or-false sort of story facts, one sentence whose expected answer was thirteen of the fourteen words just taught, a tick-two whose right answer was on the slide before, then the written explanation), and a deck and script the teacher called fantastic lost the class: `a lot of listening, a lot of sitting ... they came away listening, not learning` (30 September 2026). Children learn a lesson's meaning by working some of it out before the final task, so the repair is in what they do, not in telling less well: teach what one part means and let pairs work out the next with the cases in their hands (the shepherds taught, then the wise men and the star matched to what Christians say they show, with a wrong meaning among the cards); let them order or retell what they already know rather than hear it again; and give the beat that prepares the main work to cards at tables when handling them earns the time. A quick recall check with the answer off the board stays a good beat, and none of this asks children to stand up or move about. `Why`, `because` and `explain` are one form among many, not the default Do: a Year 4 lesson whose objective was only to name the parts of the digestive system asked for a because-sentence or a why in three of its five Dos and ended on `Explain how you know`, good discussion every one, and all the same kind. Let each Do do the subject (in science: predict, observe, sort, order, measure, label; the subject file's `What a Do beat looks like` is the menu), keep the written explanation for the final task of an objective that asks children to explain, and choose explaining elsewhere only where it is the best thing for that chunk. Use the relevant `do-beats.md` entries to find a better-fitting activity when this happens. Simple recall, a sentence stem or a sketch can be the best choice. Repeat a form when the repetition builds the learning, as in arithmetic, spelling or practising a new writing move. There is no register quota, compulsory movement or requirement to make each task harder; choose variety for its learning value, engagement and access, with manageable preparation and transitions.

**A hands-on task has a board version.** In other classrooms the teacher often teaches, then sends children back to their tables to do something practical with it, and a lesson that only ever answers on the board or in books loses that. So every beat where children do something is planned at the levels it sensibly has, and the teacher picks one on the day: the board version, always there, which runs from the slides with nothing prepared so a tired or cover teacher can still teach it; a printed sheet the pack makes, one whole page like a mini worksheet with the task written at the top, one between two (two to a page when the piece stays its size, for the teacher to trim); and real things, the wool, the stones, the magnets a teacher who reads the lesson the day before can go and get, planned whenever the best form of the task uses them and marked as that day's option, with the printed level showing pictures of the same things. The teacher asked for exactly this choice (1 October 2026: "all do beats ... it makes the board version, and makes a printout version, and also tells me if there could be actual physical things to get"), after a Year 4 history design decided "board only" for both its sorts and he never got it. **A printed level earns its paper by changing what children do or how they record it, not by reprinting the board.** On the board three children sorted by a law is a two-second read; on a sheet that prints the law as the clue and has children tick each child against it, it is the rule applied case by case. So ask what the sheet lets children do that the board does not: tick each case against a rule printed on it, write 1 to 4 on the order, underline in their own copy of a source, mark the map, answer a named child's claim on a task sheet with room to write. Where the honest answer is nothing, because the answer is said aloud or is one word (a quick recall, a partner talk, a confidence check), the beat is board only and says why in a sentence. A table or grid children would otherwise copy into their books before they can fill it is not that case: copying takes a class minutes and teaches nothing, so the sheet prints it ready to fill (a teacher who wants it copied can still ask for that). A worksheet later in the lesson with a similar table does not save the copying on this one; a Year 6 divisibility lesson of 2 October 2026 had children copy a four-row and then a twenty-box table "because the worksheet prints this kind of table". A sort or an order is always printed. The sheet is whole and nothing is cut wherever the task can be done that way: cutting can itself be a barrier for a child, cutting out a class set of cards is the teacher's evening, and a child changes their mind on a sheet by rubbing a letter out. Cards to cut out are for the task where moving the pieces is truly the best way to do the thinking (many pieces tried in different groupings, an arrangement built up and rebuilt), or for a request that asks for them, and then few and simple enough for the teacher to cut beforehand. The plan says in a few words what moving does that writing would not. "A child can change their mind by moving a card" is true of every sort, so it is not that reason: it chose cards whenever a plan wanted them, and two lessons in twenty cut cards out for a task a sheet does faster (the teacher, 11 October 2026; "if truly cutting is best, do it", 13 October 2026). For real things, the question is whether holding, moving or testing makes children think better than seeing it: yes when a quick test gives real evidence, when a model shows something hidden or too big to see, when the detail of an object is the evidence. The task is chosen for its thinking first; the props are how a prepared teacher runs that same task, never the reason it was chosen, and a practical task chosen to take a turn ("the first one wasn't practical, so this one should be") is the mistake the teacher named (30 September 2026). Each beat records the decision in its `levels` (`output-template.md` → Source-unit contract), its teacher note (`speakerNotes.teacherInfo`) says when a printed sheet goes out, and the orientation's preparation line names all three. `do-beats.md` §11 holds the hands-on forms.


## From: How Much Fits in One Lesson

**Scope comes from the objective, never from the list a plan offers or from a need the lesson made for itself.** What sets a lesson's size is what these children need, at their age, to do what the objective says, settled from the objective, the curriculum for the year group and how children learn best before the plan's content lists are read: the most able teacher reads the objective first and then takes from the scheme what serves it (the teacher, 18 October 2026: "the whole point of the scheme is to just give an idea or give ideas"). A plan's own priority marks, a scheme's core knowledge beside its possible or supporting facts or a first and second word list, say what the school thinks matters most, and they still bind only where the teacher marks them (`lesson-designer.md` → Your Role as Decision-Maker). Anything beyond what the objective needs earns its place by one test: does a child explain or understand better with it, or only name more? A word that helps children understand is good teaching, not load to strip, and a lesson never goes babyish to stay small: a food chains lesson keeps `producer`, because children explain a chain better with it. Depth follows the same test: each step children have to explain gets its how, and a step the explanation only passes through stays a plain sentence (`subject-science.md` → `Depth follows what the child must explain` has the case). Two growths pass every check that reads one beat at a time, because each part really is needed by another. A case, a lesson question or a final task brings in an idea the objective does not name, and the lesson then teaches that idea so the task can use it. And beats justify each other in a loop. A Year 4 lesson on how we hear did both: it opened on a string telephone, explaining a string telephone needs sound travelling through a solid, so a table test was added for the string's sake; the outer ear was taught so a cupped-hands test could use it, and the cupped-hands test was there so the outer ear was used. With five parts of the ear, rice on a cling-film bowl and the bell's whole journey on top, each piece needed by the next, the teacher found it "so much" (18 October 2026), when the air, the eardrum and the brain carried the objective. So follow every `needed by` to its end. It has to reach what the objective needs; a need the lesson created by its own choice of case or beat is a second strand with a cost, weighed against choosing a case that needs only what the objective does.

**The same measure counts stories, not only ideas.** A lesson follows one story: one place and time, one problem and the people in it, told from its scene to what changed, the way the Shaftesbury calibration follows Sarah Gooder to the new law (`Pride Lessons` → `A whole lesson at the right amount`). A second story arrives with its own scene, its own people, its own cause and its own ending, and it doubles what a child holds however well each half is told. A Year 4 lesson on how diseases spread told Tudor plague and Victorian cholera in full, each with its own town, its own route and its own treatments; every chunk earned its place, and the whole was two lessons (the teacher, 27 September 2026: "it's definitely too much, for sure"). When the plan or the objective offers two, tell one properly, and let the other come in as one board of comparison near the end or belong to another lesson, named in one line of the closing decisions. A plural in the objective (`how diseases spread`) is met by one story told properly and that comparison, not by two full stories. This is not the lesson whose objective is the comparison itself (`how children's leisure time changed`): there the two times are one story seen from both ends, each told lightly, never each told in full. The tell: a second scene-setting partway through the lesson.


## From: Lesson Designer content boundaries

**The fact is the destination. Teaching is the route. The slide carries the route.** Teaching is deliberately moving a child's thinking from where it is to something they can do without the teacher: connecting to what they already have, directing their attention to the thing that matters, making the relationship visible, giving them part of the thinking to do, and checking what they took from it. Telling is saying the destination: a fact, a definition, a star line, a question with nothing in front of it to think from. A Year 4 history deck (14 September 2026) put `A Tudor farm household`, `England, 1485 to 1603`, one fact and a question on its first Teach slide, with the whole route (what a home needs every day; none of it came from a shop or a switch; look at what she is carrying and what it is for) in the speaker notes, and the user, meeting it cold, said: "what am I exactly meant to do with that? The scene hasn't been set. There's nothing on the slide to guide me to know what to say." So a Teach slide carries the route, in whole sentences a teacher could say, by the ways in that fit the idea, often two or three in turn (a moment to notice, something familiar and its question, a scenario to discover through, a demonstration that makes a process or a hidden cause visible, or an idea explained), and when an idea has to be explained, usually in the order this teacher teaches: the takeaway, the because or so, the example the class looks at, and what it does not mean (`teaching-sequence-content-based.md` → `How this teacher explains` owns the ways in, the four parts and their exceptions). The script says the same route as spoken words. Neither carries teaching the other lacks; the difference between them is register and how many sentences, and the test of the board is a teacher who does not know this topic, on their first day, with the notes closed: could they teach this beat from what is on the screen, and could a child see what they are meant to look at and work out? The limit is amount, and it is the ceiling in `Pride Lessons` → `How much a Teach slide holds`: five things on the board at most, the picture included. When the route needs more, the beat takes two slides, split where the teaching turns; the words never shrink, and they are never clipped to fit. `Pride Lessons` → `What a Teach slide holds` carries the boards the user chose, as the calibration. The rule does not make every slide a Teach slide: a practice slide is still the question, the tool and the criteria, and a Do slide is still the case and what every child decides.


## From: Lesson Designer visual-need boundary

**Show the class the thing the slide is talking about.** The question to ask of every slide is `does this name something that exists in the world?` - a church, a carol service, a Bible, a food, a coin, a place, a building, an object, a person doing something. When it does, the class should be able to see it while the teacher explains. That picture is what a child looks at while you talk, what you point at, what they hook their own experience on to, and what they ask an unplanned question about. It earns its place on that alone, and nothing has to be decided from it. The lesson designer never sees the slides, so it asks the question of each group of board lines about one thing, because a paced Teach run gives each of those a slide of its own (`A Teach run is paced`, above): a beat whose lines move from the crowded houses to the yard and pump, to a person ill in bed, to the deaths, asks for a picture of each, not one for the whole beat. The Year 4 diseases lesson planned six pictures for seventeen slides, and the deck the teacher chose showed fourteen. The scene counts too: an everyday opening (`When it rains, the water runs along the ground, down any slope it can find`) names something in the world, rain running downhill, and asks for its picture like any other board. Being familiar is why that picture works, not a reason to leave it off: a Year 4 river lesson planned photographs for the whole Severn story and none for its rain-and-hills opening, and its first Teach slide was two cards of text (29 September 2026).

**The picture is there whenever its thing is being talked about, and it changes when the talk moves on.** Two rulings from the teacher on real decks (12 October 2026). A Year 3 RE deck first named Rama and Sita on two slides of words and small drawings: "that needs pictures". And of lessons built round a picture: "I still only see it on a few slides and not on slides where it should be"; a Year 5 history deck opened `Who ran Athens?` on a slide of words, with both its photographs of Athens on the slides after. So the first slide to name a person, place, animal or object shows it, and so does each later slide still about it; one picture used on several slides asks for nothing new. It matters most in Years 1 to 4, where a child who cannot yet read the name has only the picture. Then the other direction, also his: the same picture "over and over" gets boring. It repeats while the class is still working on that one thing (a Year 2 lesson describing a single night scene, noun by noun, wants that scene on every slide), and a slide that has moved on to another person, place or moment gets its own, so a story told over five boards asks for the picture of each moment. Two things this does not mean. The picture goes with the word, beside it or as its caption, never in its place: `sky` stays printed beside the photograph of the sky. And a small Educational SVG drawing added to a slide afterwards is decoration, which the teacher does not count as the picture, so it never answers whether the class can see the thing.

**A slide that explains how something happens shows it happening.** The question above asks whether the class can see the thing. A slide explaining how it works owes them more: the thing happening, the way a science teacher would show the air's tiny parts pushing one another along from a drum to an ear, where a Year 4 deck put a box saying `the air` between a drum photograph and an ear photograph and left the how to the words underneath (the teacher, 18 October 2026: children look at the picture first while the words are read, so the picture has to show what the words explain). So ask what the children must see to understand this step, then choose how it is made: a helper when one draws that, since helpers exist so common things are not rebuilt on every run, and otherwise a made picture of it happening (`lesson-designer-components.md` → Photograph acquisition, `controlled-ai`). Choosing the helper first and letting it decide what is shown is the mistake to avoid: boxes and arrows were to hand, so a route was drawn where the lesson was explaining a push. A photograph can make the same mistake: a food chains deck explaining that grass makes its own food from sunlight showed a photograph of sunlit grass, which shows grass, where the teacher wanted the making shown ("with arrows or something"), and a made picture of the sun's rays going into the leaves and water rising up the roots was the one he liked. The limit: this is for the step whose how the lesson actually explains, a process no camera can catch as it happens. A route diagram is right where the route is the point (a food chain's arrows, the order food travels), a name or a told fact needs only its thing, and a real event or person is shown by the photograph rules, never a made picture passed off as real (`lesson-designer-components.md` → Photograph acquisition).

**A person the lesson invents counts as something in the world, and is the hole in that question.** `Does this name something that exists in the world?` gets the answer no for Nadia and Grace, because they are made up, and the slide that quotes them ends up with nothing to look at. That is backwards: an invented person needs showing more than a real one does, not less, because showing them is the only thing that makes a child treat the words as somebody's rather than as an exercise. A Year 4 RE deck put two children's reasons for singing carols on the board as two text cards, with no faces and with the carol-singing photograph it had already sourced sitting unused on four other slides. So when a beat puts a person in front of the class, it owes the class that person: a portrait and a bubble (`slide-speech-and-characters.md` owns how), and the lesson's relevant photograph where one exists. The same test then does its ordinary work on what those people are talking about - carols, a nativity, a tree - and normally finds the picture already in the lesson.

**Giving a task its instructions is not launching it.** Before a substantial task, children may need to know why they are doing it now, what they are making, what a good one looks like and how the work will run, and only then the instruction. Those are beats on the board, not framing prose: the why is one line that gives the class something to hold while they look at the model, and **when the task is about a particular case, that line is the case and the question, not a recap of the teaching**. A child looking at a strong answer beside a weak one with no question in mind is looking at writing; a child who has just met Sam and his squash is looking for how to answer about Sam. So the case comes first, on a short slide of its own before the model, carrying who it is, what happened and what they are being asked - and the task slide keeps the case as well, because that is the board they write from. A recap of what the lesson established (`We've found what our agreement needs: joining in, passing, privacy, questions, help`) is the right line only when the product is the class's own work on what they have just built and there is no case to set; where the strong instance already says the chain, the recap is the same thing said worse and goes; what good looks like is an example beside a non-example with the difference named (`Be respectful` tells you nothing to do; `Listen while someone else is speaking` does); the how is the steps in the order children take them (make one, combine your group's, agree ours). **`steps` are stages of work, not the standard said again.** The teacher deleted a whole steps slide from the tooth deck on 18 September 2026 and the lesson lost nothing: `read what happened to Sam` came after two slides showing Sam, `use the four steps on the board, in order` pointed at the criteria panel on the next slide, `write it as sentences, not as a list` was what the two cards above had just shown, and `read it back: does each sentence say what caused the next one?` was the difference line word for word. The test on each step is whether the class would lose anything it cannot already see if that step were deleted. A task whose work is one sustained go, launched with a model and with the criteria visible while children work, usually leaves `steps` empty; stages earn the field when they really happen in an order and the later ones are invisible until the earlier ones are done. A slide that goes straight to `Write one rule` when none of that has been on the board has left the launch in the notes, and a teacher working from the slide sets the task without teaching it. Framing-in-notes still holds for the teacher's spoken orientation; it does not cover a model, a non-example or the steps, which a child needs to see. Slide count follows: one or two short slides before the task slide, each doing less, never the launch crammed on to the instruction. A task that runs in two parts is launched at each part, where children start it: the cards go out after the first part's how, and the model for the writing comes when the cards are done, just before the writing. A renga lesson of 2 October 2026 put the class checklist, a good and weak join and four steps before any child touched a card, about 300 words of talk for writing children would not start for ten minutes. A short beat children can start from its question alone, and a task whose product this lesson has already shown them a good one of, need none of this. Success criteria on the board that already show what a good one looks like (an actual good one, such as a model answer or a good paragraph, never a list of what a good one includes) stand in for the good instance, so the launch keeps its case and steps and needs no second model. **Model this very task by default: the good answer is to the question children are about to write about.** The teacher's ruling (1 October 2026, confirmed 18 October 2026): children should experience a good answer to the exact thing they are writing, because it shapes their own, and in his class even children told the answer still write it less well, so showing it does not hand the task over. Seeing a good one, having it taken off the board, saying their own to a partner and then writing it is how this class learns to explain (`explanation-tasks.md` → How this teacher teaches an explanation), and remembering the good one is part of that learning, not a sign the task is too easy. So `the model would hand over the answer`, or `the decision the task turns on would be given away`, is never a reason to model a different case: a good explanation of this case is what the launch is there to give. A parallel case is right in two places only: when the whole of a child's writing would be one sentence they could copy from the model (a one-line answer), or when the teacher asks for one. Whichever it is, say which in the design, and never claim a modelled answer as unaided evidence. A parallel case cost a Year 4 history lesson a second named child, a second claim and a model about a different idea from the task (games and electricity, when the task was about time and a law), and the ending ran long and felt like more telling. A Year 4 science lesson (17 October 2026) modelled Omar's triangle and then asked about Simran's bike bell, to keep the decision back for the children; the two were the same steps with the names swapped, so the class met two cases and gained nothing the bell itself would not have given. A Year 4 history launch showed `Sigurd came because there was not enough farmland at home for him, and the monastery had silver and nobody to fight back` as the strong answer, and the next slide asked `Why did Vikings like Sigurd sail to Britain?`: the first question was answered a minute before children met it, and the design still counted the writing as each child's own explanation. That is the defect - not the model.


## From: Slide Headings (Child-Facing Labels)

**My Turn, Our Turn, Your Turn** are familiar classroom labels and are kept as-is on skill-based maths and English slides. Children already know what these mean. The label always carries the turn word, because the slide title is the design's label and a title naming only the move leaves the deck with none of the three words in it: a Codex nearest-1,000 deck ran `The thousands either side`, `Beyond halfway` and `Round the whole set` with no turn named anywhere (19 September 2026). The validator holds the turn word on a skill-based turn. In maths the turn word is the whole title (below); in English the move follows it (`My Turn - Add the speech marks`).

**In maths, the plain words are what the teacher wants: `My Turn`, `Our Turn`, `Your Turn`, `Answers`, `Apply`.** The class reads the deck by them, and a maths slide's own question is already on the board under the title, so a longer title repeats it. A slide that teaches rather than turns takes the question it answers (`How do we find the thousands either side?`, `Which digit tells us?`). This holds even when a deck runs two different moves: the question on each slide shows which one it is (the teacher, 2 October 2026, on a divisibility deck titled `My Turn - Divisible by 6?`: "keep just my turn"). This is maths only (the teacher, 19 September 2026, on a nearest-1,000 deck whose turns were titled `The thousands either side`, `Beyond halfway` and `Round the whole set`); every other subject keeps the warm, move-naming titles above, because their slides carry no standing question the title would repeat.
