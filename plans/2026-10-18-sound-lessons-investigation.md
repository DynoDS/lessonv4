## 3. The big issue: too much in one lesson

### 3.1 What the lesson actually holds

**The lesson, start to finish**
- 26 slides, planned at 43 minutes, which the designer rounded to about 48.
- Everything the teacher says adds up to about 15 minutes of talk.
- Seven separate situations children meet: the string telephone, the drum, the table, the rice bowl, their own ears, the ear diagram, the school bell.
- Three word cards (vibration, eardrum, cochlea).
- What children do: a starter (four questions), "Is Ravi right?", the table test, the rice prediction, "Why is your outer ear curved?", cupped hands, "Have you heard it yet?", the bell chain, the good-and-weak launch, partner talk, the writing.

**The new things the designer listed**
- How the air passes a vibration along.
- That solids pass it along too.
- The outer ear catching it.
- The ear canal.
- The eardrum.
- The ear bones.
- The cochlea.
- The brain working out the sound.

The designer counted all eight, then wrote: "Every one of them is in the five lines and needed by the final explanation". Then it moved on.

**Codex did the same**
- The Codex lesson 2 also listed eight new things, with "medium" and "a signal travelling along a nerve" in place of the table and the brain.
- Codex had no table test and no string telephone, but taught the full ear anyway.
- So the depth of ear anatomy comes from rules both models share, not from one model's habits.

### 3.2 Why you want this (your past rulings)

This is not a new complaint. It's the same one, in a new subject.

- **31 August:** "every lesson tried to use everything he supplied". The repair then was about what in a plan binds. It worked for activities. It never touched facts.
- **10 September:** no caps. "Hard constraints can make lessons worse." What you accept is a judgement ("this is too much for this class in this time"), never a number.
- **9 September:** durable learning. Each part of a lesson exists because the next step needs it.
- **27 September:** the diseases lesson told two stories. "It's definitely too much, for sure."
- **28 September:** the diseases rebuild was "Year 6 to 8", "a LOT of concepts... what's the story?" The fault was concept load, not slide count. The retelling test came from this.
- **28 September:** "less is more" means pacy, not thin. Never fix "too much" by trimming the words.
- **30 September:** "It's not the slides as in the amount of slides. It's the amount in one lesson." The Nativity: "they came away listening, not learning".
- **16 October:** "it's not the size of your deck, it's how you use it... overloading teaching concepts is not" fine.

ChatGPT's "fewer ideas, not fewer slides" and "no caps" match all of this. So does its Teach, Do, Teach, Do, Apply shape, which is your 30 September shape.

### 3.3 Is it fair? Where I'd push back

**Fair**
- The lesson does hold two strands that each could carry a lesson: how sound travels through materials, and how the ear works.
- It does switch situation seven times.
- Your sense that it was "already long" by the rice fits the numbers. Starter, question, word card, two air slides, three table slides and the rice come to roughly 20 minutes before the ear starts.

**Push back 1: the ear anatomy is not simply "too much for Year 4"**
- ChatGPT said the National Curriculum doesn't require ear parts. True for the statutory wording.
- But White Rose Year 4 Sound gives the ear a whole step of its own: "It is important that children are introduced to the main parts of the inner ear". Its facts name the outer ear, ear canal, eardrum, ear bones, cochlea and brain.
- Your own scheme puts "vibration, ear drum, cochlea" in its top word list, and the designer carded exactly those three.
- So cutting the cochlea would go against your own scheme. That's your decision, not the plugin's (see section 7).

**Push back 2: a count of new things isn't the measure**
- Your Shaftesbury lesson, the one the plugin measures amount against, has around a dozen new things: Sarah, a trapper, the mine, law, Lord Shaftesbury, Parliament, a vote, the report, the new law, what it didn't fix, the fountain.
- Lesson 2 has eight. On a count, lesson 2 is lighter.
- What differs: Shaftesbury is one story, one scene (Sarah's mine), and every new thing moves that story on. Lesson 2 is two strands joined by the word "journey", told through seven different situations, with each ear part a separate name to learn.
- So a fix that just tells the designer to count harder, or to cut names, would miss it. The load came from three places at once:
  1. two strands in one lesson;
  2. lots of separate situations, each with its own set-up;
  3. a run of part names inside an explaining lesson.

**Push back 3: ChatGPT's own lesson is still two strands**
- It keeps the string telephone, the table and teaching through materials, plus a simpler ear.
- That's lighter on names but still "how sound travels through solids" and "how the ear works".
- White Rose splits it the other way: the ear is its own step, and travel through solids appears only as a misconception to watch for.
- Either can be right. Which is right for your scheme is a decision for you (section 7).

**Push back 4: "check the scheme"**
- ChatGPT said to check the scheme. I did. Your scheme for lesson 2 is itself broad:
  - the curriculum line is "travel through a medium to the ear";
  - the core facts are air and ear-to-brain;
  - the possible activities include string telephones, testing materials and labelling the ear.
- So the designer met a plan that offers two lessons' worth, and took most of it.

### 3.4 What caused it in the plugin

These causes stack on top of each other. Each one alone might not have done it.

**Cause 1: your scheme's two levels get flattened**
- Every one of the 30 science unit files on your website splits each lesson's facts into "Core Knowledge" and "Possible supporting sticky knowledge". Its word list has two tiers as well.
- For lesson 2, the core is just: sound travels through the air; vibrations entering the ear are passed to the brain as signals. The ear canal, ear bones and the cochlea's job are in the "possible" list.
- The designer reads the scheme file directly and sees those labels. But nothing in the plugin says what the split means. The plugin treats everything a plan lists as "available, judge it", and two of its rules lean towards including it:
  - the science rules say to add a later term "only when the approved objective, supplied sequence or curriculum for this lesson requires it". A plan listing something reads as the sequence requiring it.
  - the designer is told to note in a flag any content the plan lists that it left out. That makes leaving something out cost a flag, and including it cost nothing.
- Our own round five teaching checker praised the lesson because "every supporting fact and all the vocabulary are taught", and suggested adding water. So our own testing tool pushes towards covering everything too.

**Cause 2: the "is it needed?" test goes round in a circle**
- Every check on amount asks whether something is needed by a later part, or by the final task.
- But the designer writes the final task too. Once the cochlea is in the model answer, the cochlea is "needed".
- The walk-through shows it beat by beat:
  - the air teaching is there because the table test asks if a table does the same;
  - the table test is there because the string telephone needs a solid string;
  - the outer-ear teaching is there because cupped hands uses it;
  - cupped hands is there so children use the outer ear's job.
- Every reason points to another part the designer chose. None points back to what the objective needs. The last pair is a closed loop.
- The reviewer read the same chain and wrote that "every link the model answer uses was taught". That's true, and it's the circle again.

**Cause 3: the final case brought in a second strand**
- The designer picked the string telephone as the lesson question (it's in your scheme's activities).
- To explain a string telephone you need sound travelling through a solid, so the table test became necessary.
- A plugin rule then forced that: a final task can't rely on a scientific process the lesson never taught, so either teach it or change the task. The designer taught it, which added a whole chunk. Nothing asked it to weigh that against choosing a case that needs only the ear.
- This is the clearest example of a choice made early quietly setting the lesson's size.

**Cause 4: the triggers for "too much" are exactly the ones the circle defeats**
- The plugin's section on how much fits names two warning signs: an idea taught in the middle that nothing later uses, and a second scene-setting partway through.
- The reviewer is told to open that section only for those signs (or when a split looks needed).
- Here every idea is used later (cause 2), and the "journey" frame made seven situations read as one. Neither sign fired, so the section was never applied.

**Cause 5: the amount checks count activities, not ideas**
- The rule is two or three tasks, then the final task, with one-minute quick checks not counted.
- This lesson had three tasks and two quick checks, so it passed every count. The reviewer said so in those words.
- The plugin's own example of how quick checks help is this very rice test: "a lesson with four things to teach keeps each one used without four tasks". The exemption that kept Dos in check also gave ideas a way in.
- You agreed that exemption on 30 September ("if you think that's best then yes"), so this is a trade-off to revisit with you, not a mistake to quietly undo.

**Cause 6: "one new idea per chunk" means one idea, not one new name**
- The designer is told a chunk carries one new idea, and to read each chunk's new things.
- The designer treated "a vibration passed along" as the one idea and the ear parts as examples of it.
- So the ear's middle chunk held three new names (outer ear, ear canal, eardrum) and the last chunk three more (ear bones, cochlea, the brain's message), and each still counted as "one idea".
- The rule's wording switches between "idea" and "thing", which is what let this through.

**Cause 7: calling the learning "an idea" invited more examples**
- Since 10 September, an idea has to be met in at least two instances.
- The designer called the learning an idea and listed it as met "on the drum through air, the table, the cling film, the eardrum...". The extra situations then counted as good practice.
- The rule says at least two. It doesn't warn that more instances cost more set-up and more switching.

**Amplifier: the planned minutes look optimistic**
- Cupped hands is planned at 2 minutes. It involves predicting, writing, partner talk, two tries and a discussion.
- The Nativity investigation (30 September) already found the designer's own minutes run short. Nothing was changed for this, so the 48 minutes is probably low.
- I can't measure the real time without the lesson being taught.

### 3.5 What would still go wrong after an obvious fix

- **"Teach only core facts"** would break your food chains lesson. There, your own 14 October ruling taught "grass makes its own food using sunlight", which is a "possible" fact. A "possible" fact that explains the core stays; one that only adds a name is optional.
- **"Count new things and stop at N"** goes against no-caps, and Shaftesbury would fail it.
- **"Cut ear parts"** fixes this one lesson and misses causes 2, 3 and 4. The next lesson grows the same way through a different case.
- **"Tell the reviewer to judge amount harder"** has been tried in words before (27 and 30 September). Wording alone did not hold then.

### 3.6 What a real fix has to do, for any lesson

1. **Fix the scope before the final task is written.** Write the answer a child gives at the core level first, then let each extra earn its place by the test: does a child *explain* better with it, or just *name* more?
2. **Read a scheme's tiers as priority, not as a list to cover.** Core leads. A "possible" fact comes in when the core can't be understood without it. Remove the pull to cover everything (the "flag what you left out" cost, and "a plan listing it means the sequence requires it").
3. **Anchor every "why it's here" to the objective, not just to the next beat.** "Needed by a later beat" has to end at something the objective needs.
4. **Weigh what a chosen case brings in.** When a final case or lesson question needs an idea the objective doesn't, that idea is a second strand with a cost. Choose a different case or accept the cost on purpose; never let it come in unweighed.
5. **Let the reviewer see the whole-lesson load,** not just each beat: the core, the extras and why each extra is there, how many situations children switch between, and whether there are two strands. Change the trigger so it doesn't depend only on "an idea nobody uses later".
6. **Clear up "idea" versus "new name"** in the one-idea-per-chunk rule.
7. **Add a science "too much" example beside the diseases one,** with the honest note that the scheme lists the parts. Then the judgement has something to calibrate against in science.
8. **Fix our own testing brief** so the next stress test doesn't reward covering everything.

None of this is a cap.

### 3.7 How we'd know it worked

- **Lesson 2 rebuilt:** one strand, fewer situations. It still has to explain hearing well enough for your scheme's core and the words you choose to keep.
- **Food chains (the discrimination check):** "grass makes its own food" must survive.
- **Name the parts of the digestive system:** names are the objective there, so they must stay.
- **One non-science lesson from a broad plan** (history), to show the fix works beyond science.
- **One Codex run** of lesson 2, since Codex made the same anatomy choice.
- **A deliberate undo:** put the old wording back in a copy and check the rebuilt lesson grows again.

### What caused it

### What a real fix has to do
- Settle the 17 October decision, using both examples, as one fix.
- The fix has to cover planning (what must the child see at the step being taught), the check (purpose, not just contents) and the means to make it (a made teaching picture, or a helper).

## 5. "Inconsistent depth of scientific explanation"

### What was said
- ChatGPT called it one of "the two issues you've raised", to look at across the plugin. Daniel's own words about it aren't in the saved chat.

### Two readings, both supported by the slides

**Reading 1: depth changes from link to link in lesson 2**
- The air step gets a full how (each bit pushes the next, the line of people, "it's not that the air flies to you").
- The eardrum gets a how by comparison (stretched tight like the cling film).
- The table gets none ("the table passed the vibration along").
- The ear bones "pass it on". The cochlea "turns the vibration into a message", with no how at all.

**Reading 2: depth changes between the two lessons**
- Lesson 1 teaches one idea lightly (something vibrates; the part that vibrates makes the sound; the air in a recorder gets one line).
- Lesson 2 goes all the way to the cochlea and the brain.

### Is it fair? Where I'd push back
- The same depth everywhere would be wrong. Explaining how a cochlea works would overload Year 4 even more.
- The right rule is that depth follows the objective's core idea. The links a child must explain get a how; links that are only names stay names.
- By that rule, reading 1 is partly fair (the table, a link children must explain for the string telephone, got no how) and partly not (the cochlea as "a message" is the right Year 4 depth).

### What I need from you
- Which slides you meant, and which reading. I can't trace this further without your words or the screenshots.
- If it's reading 1, it is mostly the same root as section 3 (depth wasn't set from the core first).

## 6. The smaller issues

### 6.1 "Write yes or no" on the rice slide

**What I found**
- The designer wrote "Will the rice jump?" on the board.
- The reviewer changed it to "Will the rice jump? Write yes or no." It gave the reason "every child commits an answer the teacher could see".
- The slide designer then put "Write yes or no." in the blue task box with the pencil, and left the real question only in the title.

**Why you want this**
- It's already your rule, from 13 October: a bare thing to put down is written as the question it stands for, and a line that only says to write the answer is left out, because the pencil says it ("If it says Write though then it does").

**What caused it: a clash between three rules**
- Your 13 October rule says the above.
- An older line in the same rules file says "How every child commits is written into the task itself (`Write yes or no, then one reason`)". It still stands, and gives this exact example.
- The teaching-sequence rules repeat "with how they commit written into the task".
- Your Shaftesbury calibration lesson (27 September) also has "Is Sam right? Write yes or no, then one reason."
- The 13 October repair updated one place and left the older lines and example in force. The reviewer followed the older one.

**Push back**
- "Write yes or no, then one reason" isn't the silly bit. The reason is real thinking.
- The silly bit is a bare "Write yes or no." standing in for the question.
- So the fix is to make the older lines agree with 13 October (the question on the board, the pencil carries "write"), not to ban yes-or-no questions.

**Related, not raised by you**
- **No reason asked:** the science rules say a prediction needs a reason ("a blind guess is not useful prediction evidence"), but the rice prediction asks for none.
- **Four two-way choices:** the lesson has four ("Is Ravi right?", "Will the rice jump?", "louder or quieter?", "Have you heard it yet?"). On 28 to 29 September you said "A or B is fine, but could also be easily guessed", after beats shrank to "write A or B".
- Both are worth checking in the same fix.

**How we'd know**
- Rebuild the rice slide and the cupped-hands slide. Then search every saved deck for a task line that is only "Write ...".

### 6.2 The table test might not prove it

**What I found**
- Children put an ear on the table while a partner taps the far end, then lift their head and listen to the same tap through the air.
- So the test does have a comparison. The notes expect "louder through the table".
- But no slide or note says why the comparison is the proof: with your head up, the tap only comes through the air, so the extra loudness came through the table.
- The script also says the answer for the class ("Louder! So Ravi isn't right").

**Is it fair?**
- Half fair. ChatGPT's worry ("they can hear it through the air too") is answered by the head-up comparison. But children are never shown that reasoning, so for them it's a demonstration of a told answer.
- Covering the other ear would make it cleaner.
- Your scheme suggests this same kind of test, and it's standard in schools, so it shouldn't be dropped.

**What caused it**
- The science rules cover equipment that could break the rule being taught, and an "investigation" whose result was already told. Nothing asks: does this test rule out the other explanation, and do children see why?

**What a real fix has to do**
- One judgement in the science rules: when a test is the evidence for a claim, name the other explanation it rules out, and give children the reason the result counts.
- A one-line note is enough when that's all it needs. This must not turn every demo into a fair-test lesson.

**How we'd know**
- Rebuild lesson 2's table test. Then check one other science lesson with a demo (rice in lesson 1, or a magnets test) still reads naturally.

### 6.3 The rice "not joined up"

**What I found**
- The link is in the lesson. Slide 12 opens "When a sound reaches your ear, it makes your eardrum vibrate" with the rice result, and slide 14 says the eardrum is "stretched tight, like the cling film on the bowl".
- The script on the rice slide even starts with the purpose: "Now, what happens when a vibration reaches something?". But that line is only in the notes. The slide's title is just "Will the rice jump?".
- You said yourself, once you saw the eardrum slides, that you could see why the rice mattered.

**Is it fair?**
- Yes, in a narrower way than ChatGPT put it. The link is made, but only afterwards and only in speech. Reading the slides in order, the rice looks like a third separate experiment.
- That's your 14 September rule ("what lives on the slide") and the random-slide test.

**What caused it**
- Partly section 3: seven situations make any one of them look stuck on.
- Partly that the question the demo answers isn't on the board.
- The science rule "several activities are coherent when they attack the same sticking point... a list of science activities is not a sequence" exists, and the designer believed each one did attack it.

**What a real fix has to do**
- Mostly fixed by section 3 (fewer situations, one strand).
- One check to add: each demo's board says what it's finding out, in the lesson's own question, not just the demo's own question.

### 6.4 What worked, and must be kept

## 8. Decisions only you can make

These change what the fix does, so they come first.

1. **What is lesson 2 about?**
   - (a) The ear, all parts named, as White Rose does. No string telephone or table, and travel through solids moves to another lesson.
   - (b) Travel through materials, with a simple ear (eardrum and brain), as ChatGPT suggests.
   - (c) Both, but lighter.
   - Your scheme lists all three routes, so the plugin can't decide this from the scheme alone.
2. **The ear words.** Your scheme's top word list is vibration, eardrum, cochlea. Should the plugin treat a scheme's top word list as the words to teach, and the second list as optional? Or should it judge both lists?
3. **The quick-check exemption** (30 September). It kept tasks down but let ideas in. Keep it as it is, or count quick checks towards the amount when they bring a new idea?
4. **"Inconsistent depth":** which slides, and which reading in section 5?

### Daniel's answers (18 October 2026, talked through one at a time)

1. **What lesson 2 is about:** the objective. By the end, these Year 4 children can explain how we hear. The designer asks the best way to achieve that objective for this year group, using the curriculum and good teaching. A supplied scheme gives ideas; it is not followed exactly unless he marks it ("must include", "follow this plan"), which still binds even when the plan has flaws (a head teacher's plan). The most intelligent teacher reads the objective, then the scheme, and leaves out what the objective and the year group don't need.
2. **Which words stay:** a science word stays when it helps achieve the objective and is good Year 4 science teaching (curriculum, effective pedagogy), not because the scheme lists it. For lesson 2 that means the eardrum stays and the ear bones, ear canal and cochlea go, provided that judgement holds. But never babyish: vocabulary is part of teaching effectively, so a word that helps children understand stays ("producer" in food chains).
3. **Quick checks:** they check what was just taught and must never carry new teaching (bringing out a misconception is still checking). They don't count towards the two or three tasks, but they do count towards time. The shape, as context and never a fixed template: teach one thing, check it (quick check or bigger task); teach one more thing, check it; by then it feels like the end of the lesson, so the final task comes next with a short lead-in (here is your task, here is a good one, off you go), then independent time. About three chunks at most, the final task being the third. Three chunks of teaching and then a final task on top is too much; lesson 2 was exactly that, and the task count missed it because the middle chunk hid behind a quick check. Other lesson types keep their own shapes ("in a nutshell").
4. **Inconsistent depth:** the steps inside lesson 2 (his best memory; not the difference between the lessons). Rule: decide how deep to go from what the child must explain, then give each of those steps a how.
5. **The diagram:** yes. Children look at the picture first while the teacher reads, so it must show what the words explain. "The air" in a box came from picking a helper from the menu. A science teacher would show the air particles moving. Helpers exist for common things (a flowchart need not be rebuilt each run), but the designer must first ask what the children must see, then use a helper if it fits, or make the picture through Codex when none does. The check must test that. This settles the 17 October vibration-arrows decision in the same direction.

### Daniel's answers on the smaller issues (18 October 2026)

6. **"Write yes or no":** one yes-or-no on its own (a prediction, "Is Ravi right?") asks for the reason too: "Will the rice jump? Why do you think so?" (or "Explain your answer"), with the pencil. Several yes-or-no decisions together may stay bare yes or no. The older line and its example are corrected to say this, matching the 13 October ruling. Note: the rice was really a prediction that started the next teaching (the stretched skin), not a pure quick check; calling it quick is how it went uncounted (answer 3 applies).
7. **Tests as proof:** yes. When a test is the evidence for a claim, the lesson names what else could explain the result and gives children the reason the result counts (usually one line).
8. **Demo slides show what they find out:** "I guess so", so lukewarm. Treat it as light guidance for the designer (the slide carries the lesson's question the demo serves, and the link follows at once), not a hard rule or a check.
