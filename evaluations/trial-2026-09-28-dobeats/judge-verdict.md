# Do beat judgement: eight topics, A against B

Class: Year 4 working well below age, many SEND and EAL. Judged against the teacher's definition of a good Do beat, his dislikes, and the research failure tests (dobeat-research.md, Part 3). The last beat of each lesson is judged as the main task.

Summary of verdicts:

| Topic | Better | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Geography: rainforest people | B | slight |
| History: Anglo-Saxons | A | slight |
| History: cholera | B | clear |
| PSHE: being active | B | slight |
| RE: baptism | A | slight |
| RE: langar | A | clear |
| Science: conductors | A | slight |
| Science: water cycle | B | slight |

Neither version wins across the board. The differences come from particular beat choices, not from one version being built better overall.

---

## 1. Geography: rainforest people

**Verdict: B, slight.**

Reasons:
- **B's "Could they drive to the next village?"** is the best geography beat in either version. Children read a new photograph for evidence ("point to what in the photo tells you"). That is a geographer's move, it needs no writing, and it works for EAL children.
- **B's main task is a needs table with water done for you.** For this class that is far kinder than A's open "Describe how Indigenous peoples use the forest", which is close to a blank page and heavy on writing.
- **A's first two beats both ask for writing** ("Write three things", then "Write A or B... then write what the big river would give"). A's site choice is a good geography decision, but the written reason is heavy for a quick beat. A's sort of seven new things is its strongest beat, and B has nothing like it.

Remaining faults in B:
- "What would your family need?" is talk only. There is no individual mark before partners talk, so one child can answer for the pair.
- "Is Jack right?" needs knowledge the Teach headline did not give. The headline says they fish, hunt and collect, but the answer depends on knowing the garden is left to grow back. The hint helps, but only children who already know this can answer.
- There is no sort or matching beat on new cases, which the teacher likes. There is also no map or place work, even though this is a geography lesson.

## 2. History: Anglo-Saxons

**Verdict: A, slight.**

Reasons:
- **A builds one idea step by step.** Ava's half-right claim brings in "why they could come" against "why they wanted to". The Scots sort then practises it on new facts. The main task uses it again with sentence starters. Each child commits (writes yes/no/half right, writes W or C) before talking.
- **A's Scots sort is read aloud to the class ("Listen as each card is read")**, which lowers the reading load. A's main task gives four sentence starters, which is right for this class.
- **B's main task is heavier and risks untaught knowledge.** Oliver's claim must be judged twice with no starters. It also needs children to know there were no Roman soldiers where the Scots settled, which the headlines do not show was taught.

Remaining faults in A:
- "Why wait until the soldiers had gone?" is talk only after thinking time, with no mark.
- There is no work with evidence. B's "Why build a village on a hill?" (reading an archaeological find to infer flooding) is the best history beat in the pair, and A has nothing to match it. A would be stronger with that beat in place of its opening talk question.
- Three beats: talk, judge a claim, sort. That is fine, but nothing reaches back to earlier Roman lessons beyond the soldiers.

## 3. History: cholera

**Verdict: B, clear.**

Reasons:
- **A has only two quick beats and both are sorts** (would you try this, then can the smell idea explain this clue). That is the same format twice in a short lesson. The second sort also has pairs reading three clues, which is a lot of reading for this class.
- **B uses real historical evidence as the core.** "How do these two stories show it was the water?" gives each partner one true story (the brewery men, Susannah Eley) to explain. That is how a historian argues from cases. "Breathed in or swallowed?" is a quick decision that sets up John Snow's idea just before it is taught.
- **B's main task covers the whole enquiry** (how it spread and how people tried to stop it). It is rehearsed aloud first and has sentence starters. A's main task (Dylan's tar) tests only one narrow point.

Remaining faults in B:
- "How do these two stories show it was the water?" is talk only, and each child explains only one story. There is no individual commit, and the stories are long to read.
- Three of its four beats end with "tell your partner", so the lesson leans towards talk over and over.
- "Breathed in or swallowed?" is really a science inference (stomach against chest), not a historian's move. It is fine as a bridge, but not history thinking.

## 4. PSHE: being active

**Verdict: B, slight.**

Reasons:
- **B varies the thinking**: a quick sort, then judging Grace's partly right claim, then predicting how Mei will feel. A uses two sorts one after the other, then a sentence to finish.
- **B asks for less writing.** Its middle beats are "choose, then tell your partner why". A's Harry beat asks every child to write a two-part sentence mid-lesson.
- **A's thinking is richer in places.** "Straight away, or after lots of days?" is a strong idea sort, and A's main task (Arjun, two pieces of evidence) needs more reasoning than B's "pick your own activity". That is why B wins only slightly.

Remaining faults in B:
- The main task lets children pick any activity. That invites a stock answer ("football makes me happy") and does not test the "most days, not once" idea as sharply as A's Arjun case.
- Grace's claim and Mei's prediction both end with a reason told to a partner. Two talk steps in a row, with only the choice as the individual commit.
- The six-card sort has no named boundary case, unlike A's dancing game and car ride, so it may be too easy to show anything.

## 5. RE: baptism

**Verdict: A, slight.**

Reasons:
- **A varies its beats and each asks for a different RE move.** It reads the Bible story as a source, applies the promise idea to an older child (Arjun), and judges a claim about the water.
- **A's main task is built around the photographs** ("one part for each photo"). That gives this class a clear order to write in. B's main task is a long new case (Priya, age 15, full immersion) to read, which is a heavy reading load.
- **B's opening beat may need knowledge not yet taught.** It follows the Teach "because Jesus was baptised", but the answer depends on the grown-ups' promises.

Remaining faults in A:
- Priya's claim ("the vicar wouldn't need to pour water") is simply wrong. This is the "character who is always wrong" pattern the teacher dislikes. A partly right version would be better.
- The Bible story beat is long to read, and "find the sentence in the story" risks children copying rather than joining the ideas.
- B's two symbol beats (the white clothes, the candle lit from the Easter candle) are the best RE thinking in either version: children read a new symbol using the one they were just taught. A has nothing like this. The ideal lesson would take one of them.

## 6. RE: langar

**Verdict: A, clear.**

Reasons:
- **A's emperor beat asks children to predict before the reveal**, using the equality idea. B tells the whole story, including "sat on the floor, in a row with ordinary people", then asks why. So B's answer is almost on the slide, and the Teach headline gives the word "equal".
- **A's sewa sort has six helpers and a "hardest card" reason.** B's four-person sort is also good (place and reason point different ways), so this is close. But A's written letters give a record of what each child decided.
- **Both main tasks are good**, new scenes to read and interpret. A's is split into two short parts (equality, then sewa), which is easier for this class to organise.

Remaining faults in A:
- "Who could eat here?" has "all six" as the answer. Once a child sees the pattern, the task tells you little. One card that looks as if it might be refused (someone who was rude, someone who could easily pay) is still "yes", but the check would be sharper with a reason attached.
- The main task scene is long to read.
- All three quick beats ask children to write letters. That is a low writing load, but the format is similar each time.

## 7. Science: conductors

**Verdict: A, slight.**

Reasons:
- **A's testing has children physically place each object on the side they predict, then move any that were wrong.** That is a calm hands-on sort with no writing. B asks groups to write each object's name in a column, which adds writing to an enquiry.
- **A's final beat (Maya and Chloe on the same scissors)** gives two partly right claims about an everyday object, so children must say which part each clipped onto. B's single wire with two materials works the same way, but scissors are more familiar to this class.
- **A's "Conductor or insulator?" uses photographs of five untested objects** and asks for a reason, with a live test by the teacher if possible. B's version has four objects and no reveal step.

Remaining faults in A:
- The "What's the same?" discussion is talk only.
- Both versions are close. B's spoken prediction with a reason before each test is a stronger science habit than A's silent placing, and A could borrow it.
- There is no drawing (for example, a circuit with the gap filled) anywhere in the lesson.

## 8. Science: water cycle

**Verdict: B, slight.**

Reasons:
- **B has three application beats on new everyday cases** (the T-shirts, the misty mirror, breath on a cold day), and every child makes a written choice. A has two Do beats around two "tell your partner" observation hooks, so it is more talk, less commitment.
- **B's T-shirt beat is a fair comparison** (same wash, one thing different), which is how a scientist thinks. B's breath beat applies "you can't see water vapour" to a new case, with a partly wrong child against a right one.
- **A's evaporation sort is the best single beat in the pair.** It uses pictures, includes boundary cases (ice lolly melting, hair dryer without sun) and needs little reading. That is why B wins only slightly.

Remaining faults in B:
- All three quick beats ask for writing (a line, a sentence to finish, a name plus reason). For this class that is a steady writing load. A picture sort like A's would replace one of them well.
- There is no drawing. The water cycle is the obvious place for children to add an arrow or label to a diagram.
- Zara and Ethan: one is simply wrong. It is fine here, but it adds to the pattern noted below.

## Overall

### What separates better from worse Do beats

- **New evidence beats restatement.** The strongest beats give children something new to read as a historian, geographer or scientist would: the village photograph, the mound village, John Snow's two stories, the white clothes and the Easter candle, the boundary pictures in the evaporation sort. The weakest ask children to explain something the slide or story has just said (B's langar emperor).
- **An individual mark before talk.** Better beats have each child write a letter, a W/C, R/P/W or a choice first, then talk. Worse beats are "tell your partner" alone.
- **Sorts with boundary cases.** Sorting new cases, with one or two cards that could go either way, gave the clearest checks (A's active sort, A's evaporation sort, both sewa sorts).
- **Scaffolded main tasks.** Where the main task had a table, photos in order, or sentence starters, it suits this class. Open "describe" or "explain both" tasks without starters are too heavy.
- **Predict before the reveal** was better than "explain what you were just told" (A's emperor, B's "Breathed in or swallowed?").
- **Worse versions** tended to repeat the same format twice in a row, ask for writing where a letter would do, or assume knowledge the Teach had not given (Jack's garden, the Scots and the Romans, Leo's promises).

### What is still weak even in the better versions

- **"Tell your partner" ends almost every beat.** Each one is fine alone, but across a lesson it becomes the talk-question-talk loop the teacher dislikes. Some beats should end with the mark and a quick reveal, with no talk.
- **A named child making a claim appears in nearly every lesson** (Ava, Oliver, Jack, Grace, Priya, Leo, Dylan, Maya and Chloe, Isla, Zara and Ethan). Most are partly right, which is good, but it is becoming a formula. Several (Priya's water, Dylan's tar, Zara) are simply wrong.
- **There is no drawing and almost no marking on a picture.** No map in geography, no arrow on the water cycle, no circuit drawing, no timeline placing in history. Marking on a picture is the most obvious missing format.
- **Reading load is high in RE and history.** Stories, scenes and clue cards are long for a class working well below age. Reading a source is right for the subject, but the passages could be shorter or read aloud.
- **Little reaching back.** Almost every beat checks only the chunk just taught. Only a few (the cold can in the sky prediction, "What did the water show?") draw on an earlier part of the lesson, and none reach back to earlier lessons.
- **No plan for wrong answers.** No beat says what the teacher does if many children get it wrong.

### Would this teacher be happy with the better versions?

Mostly yes, but not fully.

- He would be pleased that there is no "stand if", no thumbs, no show-me routine, that the sorts use new cases, and that the classroom stays calm.
- He would likely notice, within two or three lessons, that nearly every beat ends with "tell your partner" and that a named child with a claim turns up every time. Both are near his "discussion over and over" and "always wrong" dislikes.
- He would also notice the missing drawing and map work, and that some RE and history beats ask this class to read a lot.
- The best beats (the photo, the mound village, the symbol beats, the boundary sorts) are what he is asking for. Neither version collects all of them, so the ideal lesson in most topics would take the best beats from each.
