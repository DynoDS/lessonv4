# Do beats: what the evidence says, formats that fit, and how they fail

Research brief, 28 September 2026. Written for a Year 4 class working well below age, with many SEND and EAL children, where each small chunk of teaching is followed by a short (1 to 3 minute) task every child does.

Evidence strength is labelled throughout: **strong** (many controlled studies, including with children), **moderate** (solid lab evidence, thinner classroom or child evidence), **practice wisdom** (credible teacher writing, not directly tested).

---

## Part 1. What the evidence says

### 1.1 A Do beat straight after teaching is mostly not "retrieval practice"

- **What the research found.** Roediger and Karpicke (2006): on a test 5 minutes later, rereading beat testing. Testing only won on tests 2 days and 1 week later. The retrieval benefit comes from pulling something back after some forgetting.
- **What that means here.** Straight after a teaching chunk, the idea is still in working memory. A task that asks children to repeat it is closer to rehearsal than retrieval. It is still useful, but for two other reasons: it lets the teacher **check understanding** (1.3) and it makes children **do something with the idea** (1.2).
- **How to get true retrieval into the lesson.** Have the second or third Do beat reach back to an earlier chunk as well as the new one (for example "Use what we learned about rivers at the start to explain this photo"). Spacing across days matters most. Rawson and Dunlosky's "successive relearning" (retrieve until correct, then again on later days) gave very large long-term gains. **Strong.**
- **Match the practice to the goal.** Agarwal (2019, in a real middle-school classroom): fact-only quizzes did not improve higher-order test performance. Higher-order or mixed quizzes did. If the lesson aim is "explain why", Do beats that only ask "name the..." will not build it. **Moderate to strong.**

### 1.2 Generative tasks: making children reorganise the idea

Fiorella and Mayer (2016) reviewed eight "generative" strategies: summarising, mapping, drawing, imagining, self-testing, self-explaining, teaching, enacting. Each has median effects of about d = 0.4 or higher, but each has boundary conditions that matter a lot for this class.

- **Drawing: strong, with support.** Learner drawing helps from primary age up. A 2020 meta-analysis of 53 studies found an overall effect of g = 0.69 (Cromley and colleagues). The key condition: it works when the mechanics of drawing do not take excessive attention. It works better when children are told which parts to include, or are given a **partly drawn picture to finish** (Schwamborn and colleagues; Van Meter and Garner 2005). For this class that means: never a blank page, always a frame.
- **Self-explaining: strong.** Bisra and colleagues (2018), 64 studies: g = 0.55. Prompting learners to explain why a step or fact is so helps. For low literacy, the explanation can be spoken to a partner, not written.
- **Enacting with objects: moderate.** Children who moved toys on a mat to act out what a text described understood it better. This is calm and seated: moving counters on a printed map, arranging a figure on a diagram.
- **Summarising and mapping: weak for this class.** Younger learners need extensive training to summarise. Concept mapping needs pretraining and works best when partly done for them.
- **Imagining: weak for this class.** It works better for learners with high prior knowledge. "Picture in your head..." is a poor Do beat for children who are below age.

### 1.3 Young children need guided retrieval, not blank recall

This is the single most relevant study for a below-age Year 4 class. Karpicke, Blunt, Smith and Karpicke (2014) worked with US fourth graders (UK Year 5) in real classrooms:

- Asked to "write everything you remember", children recalled about **10%** of a short text.
- Making a concept map **with the text in front of them**, they produced about **20%** of the ideas.
- Given a **partly completed concept map** to finish, they were very successful.
- A **"question map"** (five short questions in boxes arranged around the topic title) improved learning compared with rereading.

**What it means.** For children this age, especially below age, a Do beat must give structure: partial diagrams, question boxes, cues. "Tell me what you learned" and blank mind maps fail. **Moderate to strong.**

### 1.4 Checking for understanding

- **Rosenshine (2012).** Principle 6: check for understanding; ask many questions and check the responses of **all** students. The most effective teachers checked often and did not move on until most were successful. **Strong** (correlational studies of effective teachers plus cognitive science).
- **Lemov, Teach Like a Champion.** Three techniques fit this class:
  - *Reject Self-Report*: "Everyone got that?" tells you nothing. Yes/no self-report is especially unreliable.
  - *Standardise the Format*: design the page so the answer is always in the same place, so the teacher can scan it at a glance while walking.
  - *Active Observation*: circulate with a plan of what you are looking for, and track it (for example on a class list).
  
  These three give a calm, whole-class check with no show-me routine. **Practice wisdom, widely adopted.**
- **Wiliam's hinge questions.** One carefully built question at a turning point. Children answer in 1 to 2 minutes; the teacher reads the results in about 30 seconds; each wrong option matches a known misconception. The teacher then decides: move on, or reteach. The usual method is a show-me (letter cards), but the answer can equally go in a fixed box on a slip that the teacher reads while circulating. **Moderate** (sound logic, little direct trial evidence).
- **Christodoulou.** Multiple-choice questions are often bad because they are written badly, not because the form is weak. Good distractors are plausible and each one reveals a specific wrong idea. **Practice wisdom plus assessment theory.**
- **Sherrington.** Common failures: relying on volunteers, "Does everyone get that?", sampling two children and assuming the other 28 are fine, reteaching without re-checking. **Practice wisdom.**
- **Honest caveat.** The EEF trial of Wiliam's "Embedding Formative Assessment" found a small positive effect (about +0.09) on GCSE results. The ideas are sound. Whole-school programmes produce modest gains, and the gains depend on the teacher acting on what they see.

### 1.5 Worked examples and completion

- **Worked-example effect.** Novices learn more from studying worked examples than from solving problems unaided. Unaided problem solving uses working memory on searching, not on learning (Kirschner, Sweller and Clark 2006). **Strong**, mostly in maths and science.
- **Completion effect.** A partly worked example that the learner finishes sits between the two, and is ideal for a Do beat: the child does the key step while the rest is given.
- **Expertise reversal.** Supports that help novices can hinder more expert learners. This is the argument for a harder Greater Depth version of the same Do beat, not a different task.

### 1.6 Dual coding, and what it is not

- **What holds up.** People learn more from words with relevant pictures than from words alone, **if** the two are processed together (Mayer's multimedia research, building on Paivio). **Moderate to strong**, mostly from short lab studies.
- **What does not.** Pictures that are decorative, redundant, or placed away from the words they explain can add load and hurt (redundancy and split-attention effects). Didau's critique is fair: "dual coding" in schools has shrunk to "add pictures", losing the mechanism.
- **For Do beats.** The best use is a picture the child must **act on**: label it, circle the clue, add the arrow. Not a picture beside a written question.
- **Learning styles (VAK) are not supported.** Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer and Bjork (2008) looked for the key evidence: that teaching in a learner's preferred style improves results. They found virtually none. Dual coding is words plus pictures for **every** child. It is not matching children to a "visual" style.

### 1.7 Literacy load: construct-irrelevant demand

- **The idea.** If a task meant to check science knowledge also demands reading and writing, a child can fail for reasons that have nothing to do with science. Assessment researchers call this "construct-irrelevant variance" (Messick; Abedi on English learners).
- **Evidence.** Noble and colleagues (2020) tested ways of simplifying the language of science test items for English learners. **Adding pictures to the answer options** had the largest positive effect.
- **For Do beats.** Answer by mark, letter, arrow, circle, one word or a spoken sentence wherever the aim is not literacy itself. Keep instruction wording short and in words already taught. When reading is the subject skill (a history source, an English text), keep the reading and cut the other load: short extract, read aloud first, one question. **Moderate to strong.**
- **SEND fit.** The EEF's SEND guidance puts scaffolding and explicit instruction in its "five-a-day" for all pupils. Partial diagrams, sentence stems and picture options are exactly that.

### 1.8 The big caution: lab to classroom

- **EEF (2021).** The EEF's review of cognitive science in the classroom found strong lab results for retrieval, spacing, dual coding and elaboration. It found much less certainty about how well they transfer when teachers put them into everyday lessons.
- **Lethal mutations.** The known risk is the "lethal mutation": the routine survives, the active ingredient dies (Haertel's term, popularised by Wiliam). A five-question recall quiz at the start of every lesson is the classic example.
- **Memory is the residue of thought.** Willingham: children remember what they spent the minutes thinking about. The test for any Do beat is **what the child spends the minute thinking about**. **Strong** as a principle.

## Part 2. Do beat formats (not already in the plugin)

Key for literacy load: **none** (mark, point, draw), **low** (a word or letter), **medium** (a phrase or short sentence, or reading one short line), **high** (reading a passage or writing sentences).

"How the teacher sees" never means a show-me. Every answer lands on a slip, in the book, or in partner talk that the teacher listens to while walking.

Formats marked **(general)** fit most subjects. The rest are native to one subject.

### Drawing and annotation

**1. Finish the drawing** (general)
- **Child does:** Completes a partly drawn diagram, such as the last stage of the water cycle, the missing bulb in a circuit, or the second half of a symmetrical church plan.
- **Thought forced:** Which part is missing, and how it connects to the given parts (completion and organisation).
- **Suits:** Science, geography, RE, history.
- **Literacy:** None.
- **Seen by:** A printed slip with the partial frame in a fixed place. Scan while circulating.
- **Fails when:** The missing part is guessable from the shape of the gap, or drawing it takes skill children lack.

**2. Add the missing arrow**
- **Child does:** Draws the one arrow that is missing, or wrong, in a food chain, flow diagram, cause chain or map of trade routes.
- **Thought forced:** Direction of the relationship: what causes, feeds or moves to what. Children most often get direction wrong.
- **Suits:** Science, geography, history.
- **Literacy:** None.
- **Seen by:** Slip. The arrow is visible from a metre away.
- **Fails when:** There is only one place an arrow could go, so no thinking about direction is needed.

**3. Annotate the photo with three clues**
- **Child does:** Circles three things in a new photo and labels each with one word, for example "clues this is a hot, dry place" or "clues this is a Victorian street".
- **Thought forced:** Applying the taught idea to a fresh case, and choosing evidence.
- **Suits:** Geography, history, science, RE.
- **Literacy:** Low (one-word labels, or numbered circles matched to a word bank).
- **Seen by:** Slip or stick-in. Count the circles on the right features.
- **Fails when:** The photo is the one used in teaching (copying, not applying), or it has so much detail that children circle anything.

**4. Draw what happens next**
- **Child does:** In a three-box strip with the first two boxes drawn, sketches the third: melting ice, a seed growing, the next event in a story or historical sequence.
- **Thought forced:** Prediction from cause (a mental model of the process).
- **Suits:** Science, history, English, PSHE.
- **Literacy:** None.
- **Seen by:** Slip, scanned while circulating.
- **Fails when:** Children spend the minute drawing detail. Say "stick figures, 60 seconds, the one thing that changes".

**5. Redraw from memory** (general)
- **Child does:** The teacher's diagram is shown, then covered. Children redraw its key parts inside a given outline, for example the outline of a plant with the parts to be drawn in.
- **Thought forced:** Genuine retrieval, with drawing as the response.
- **Suits:** Science, geography.
- **Literacy:** None to low.
- **Seen by:** Book or slip.
- **Fails when:** There is no outline. Blank recall gives very little for this age (Karpicke 2014).

**6. Predict and sketch**
- **Child does:** Before the reveal, sketches what will happen: where the shadow falls, what the thermometer will show, which way the line on the graph goes.
- **Thought forced:** Committing to a prediction from the rule just taught. The reveal then confirms it or surprises.
- **Suits:** Science, geography.
- **Literacy:** None.
- **Seen by:** Slip with a fixed sketch box. Glance before revealing.
- **Fails when:** The reveal comes before children commit, or no one returns to explain the wrong predictions.

### Picture-response and hinge

**7. Which picture shows it?** (general)
- **Child does:** Chooses from three or four pictures the one that shows the idea (for example "which shows evaporation", "which is a physical feature", "which is a primary source"). Writes the letter in a box.
- **Thought forced:** Discriminating the concept from near misses. Each wrong picture is a known misconception.
- **Suits:** Science, geography, history, RE.
- **Literacy:** Low. Picture options reduce language load most (Noble 2020).
- **Seen by:** Letter in a fixed box on a slip, read while circulating. A class list tally shows who needs reteaching.
- **Fails when:** Wrong options are obviously silly, or the teacher does not decide what to do with the result.

**8. Hinge slip with a "because" tick**
- **Child does:** One multiple-choice question, then ticks one of two short reasons.
- **Thought forced:** Choosing an answer, then the reason. This separates lucky guesses from understanding.
- **Suits:** Science, geography, English grammar.
- **Literacy:** Medium (reading short options). Read them aloud.
- **Seen by:** Slip, standard layout.
- **Fails when:** Options are long sentences, so it becomes a reading test.

### Subject-native formats

**9. Where on the map?** (geography)
- **Child does:** Places a dot, letter or counter on a printed outline map: "Put A where the river meets the sea, B on the highest land".
- **Thought forced:** Locational knowledge, and reading the map as a map.
- **Suits:** Geography, history (where events happened).
- **Literacy:** None.
- **Seen by:** Slip. The teacher can see a wrong dot from across the table.
- **Fails when:** The map is a copy of the teaching map with labels still on it.

**10. Trace the journey** (geography, history)
- **Child does:** Draws the route on a printed map, for example the Thames from source to sea, or a Viking raid route, passing the named places in order.
- **Thought forced:** Sequence plus location plus direction together.
- **Suits:** Geography, history.
- **Literacy:** None.
- **Seen by:** Slip.
- **Fails when:** The route is drawn on the map already, so tracing copies it.

**11. Source detective: one question, one detail** (history)
- **Child does:** Looks at one short source (a picture, or two or three lines read aloud). Underlines or circles the single detail that answers one question, such as "What does this tell us about how poor children worked?".
- **Thought forced:** Using a source as evidence. This is the historical skill, not comprehension for its own sake.
- **Suits:** History; also RE (a sacred text extract) and English.
- **Literacy:** Medium, and appropriate, because reading the source is the history.
- **Seen by:** Stick-in or slip. Check which detail is circled.
- **Fails when:** The source is too long, or the question can be answered from general knowledge without looking.

**12. Place it on the timeline** (history)
- **Child does:** Marks an event, as a letter or small picture, on a printed, scaled timeline that already shows two known anchor events.
- **Thought forced:** Chronology relative to known anchors, not just order.
- **Suits:** History, RE (festivals across the year), science (life cycles).
- **Literacy:** None.
- **Seen by:** Slip.
- **Fails when:** The anchors are unfamiliar, so there is nothing to reason from.

**13. Which belief explains this?** (RE)
- **Child does:** Sees a photo of a practice (for example washing before prayer, or sharing food at Vaisakhi). Chooses which of three belief cards, each with a picture and a short line, explains it. Writes the letter.
- **Thought forced:** Linking practice to belief, the core RE move, rather than recalling facts about the practice.
- **Suits:** RE.
- **Literacy:** Low to medium. Read the cards aloud.
- **Seen by:** Slip.
- **Fails when:** Only one belief card belongs to the right religion, so the task is answerable by name-matching.

**14. Who might say this?** (RE, history, PSHE)
- **Child does:** A short quote or speech bubble is read aloud. Children match it to one of three pictured people: a factory owner, a child worker and a reformer; or a Christian, a Muslim and a Humanist.
- **Thought forced:** Perspective. What someone with that position or belief would think.
- **Suits:** History, RE, PSHE.
- **Literacy:** Low if read aloud.
- **Seen by:** Slip.
- **Fails when:** It depends on stereotype, not the taught content.

**15. What could they say?** (PSHE)
- **Child does:** A pictured scenario, such as a friend being left out. The child completes a speech bubble from a choice of two or three starters, then says it quietly to a partner.
- **Thought forced:** Rehearsing a realistic response, so the words are available later.
- **Suits:** PSHE, RSE.
- **Literacy:** Low (spoken; choose a starter).
- **Seen by:** Listening to pairs while circulating, plus the bubble on a slip.
- **Fails when:** There is only one "right" response, so children guess the teacher's answer. Evidence for rehearsal in PSHE is thin (practice wisdom).

**16. Change one thing** (science, geography)
- **Child does:** "What if the ramp was steeper? What if the town was on the hill, not by the river?" Draws or marks the new result.
- **Thought forced:** Seeing which variable matters (variation and cause).
- **Suits:** Science, geography, and English ("change one word, what changes in the meaning").
- **Literacy:** None to low.
- **Seen by:** Slip.
- **Fails when:** More than one thing changes, so the answer is ambiguous.

**17. Read the scale** (science, geography)
- **Child does:** Reads a pictured thermometer, measuring cylinder, rain gauge or map scale, and writes the number.
- **Thought forced:** A subject-native working skill.
- **Suits:** Science, geography, maths links.
- **Literacy:** None.
- **Seen by:** Slip. A number is quick to check.
- **Fails when:** It is used where the lesson aim is conceptual, not procedural.

### Generating and completing

**18. Make one of your own** (general)
- **Child does:** Produces a new example: "Draw a push you did this morning", "Write a noun phrase about the dragon using two adjectives", "Name a place near school that is human, not physical".
- **Thought forced:** Generation and transfer. The child cannot copy it from the slide.
- **Suits:** Science, English, geography, PSHE.
- **Literacy:** Varies. Keep it to a drawing or phrase unless writing is the aim.
- **Seen by:** Book or slip, while circulating.
- **Fails when:** Children reproduce the teacher's example with one word changed. Ask for something "from your house", "from our playground".

**19. Finish the worked example** (general)
- **Child does:** Sees a model with the last step blank: a sentence with the fronted adverbial missing its comma, a timeline missing the final event, a circuit missing the switch. Completes that step.
- **Thought forced:** The key step, with the rest carried (completion effect).
- **Suits:** English, science, history, maths-like work.
- **Literacy:** Low to medium.
- **Seen by:** Slip.
- **Fails when:** The completed step is identical to the one in the teaching example.

**20. Question boxes** (after Karpicke 2014; general)
- **Child does:** Four or five small boxes around a topic picture, each with a short question. Some are filled in already. The child fills the rest without looking back.
- **Thought forced:** Guided retrieval, and seeing how facts connect to one topic.
- **Suits:** Science, geography, history.
- **Literacy:** Low to medium (one-word answers; questions read aloud).
- **Seen by:** Slip or stick-in.
- **Fails when:** The questions are about wording on the slide, not the ideas.

**21. Which word does the work?** (English, general)
- **Child does:** Circles the one word in a sentence that shows the thing asked about: "which word tells you he is nervous", "which word shows it happened long ago", "which word is the verb".
- **Thought forced:** Close reading tied to one idea. This suits reading as the aim.
- **Suits:** English, history (sources), RE (texts).
- **Literacy:** Medium (reading one sentence, which is appropriate here).
- **Seen by:** Slip.
- **Fails when:** The sentence has two defensible answers that the teacher did not plan for.

**22. Caption it in five words** (general)
- **Child does:** Writes a caption of five words at most for a new photo, using a named new word, for example "The river erodes the bank".
- **Thought forced:** A constrained summary using the new vocabulary on a fresh case.
- **Suits:** Geography, science, history, RE.
- **Literacy:** Medium. Offer a word bank, or accept a spoken caption to a partner.
- **Seen by:** Slip, or listening to pairs.
- **Fails when:** The word bank gives the answer away, or it becomes a spelling task.

**23. Say it with the word, partner checks** (general)
- **Child does:** Explains the picture to a partner, and must use one named word (for example "evaporate"). The partner ticks a box on their slip when they hear it used correctly. Then they swap.
- **Thought forced:** Self-explanation in speech, with the vocabulary used, not just heard.
- **Suits:** All subjects.
- **Literacy:** None (oral).
- **Seen by:** Listening to pairs on a planned route, plus the ticks.
- **Fails when:** Only one partner talks. Build in the swap, and give each child a different picture.

**Mix for variety across a lesson.** One drawing or annotation beat, one picture-response beat, and one subject-native beat usually covers "variety" while keeping literacy low.

## Part 3. Why Do beats fail in practice, and how to spot it in a plan

Each failure below has a **test** you can run on a written plan.

**1. The answer is on the slide.**
- **What goes wrong:** Children copy or restate; nothing is retrieved or applied (Willingham; Coe's "poor proxies").
- **Test:** Is the expected answer visible, word for word, on the teaching slide still showing? Is the Do-beat case the same example used in teaching?

**2. Thinking about the wrong thing.**
- **What goes wrong:** The minute goes on cutting, colouring, neat drawing or decoding instructions (Willingham's biscuits for the Underground Railroad).
- **Test:** List what the child physically does. If most of the time is mechanics, it fails. Look for "colour", "decorate", "cut out", or no time limit on drawing.

**3. Literacy load swamps the concept.**
- **What goes wrong:** Children who understand the science fail because they cannot read the question or write the answer (construct-irrelevant demand).
- **Test:** Is a sentence required where a mark, letter or word would do? Are the instructions longer than the content? Are there untaught words in the instructions? Could an EAL child who understood the idea show it?

**4. Unguided recall for novices.**
- **What goes wrong:** "Write everything you remember" or a blank mind map gets about 10 to 20% from children this age (Karpicke 2014).
- **Test:** Any blank-page or free-recall task without cues, frames or partial answers.

**5. One child answers for the room.**
- **What goes wrong:** Volunteers or confident children answer; the teacher's view of the class is false (Rosenshine, Sherrington).
- **Test:** "Ask the class", "Who can tell me...", "Take answers". No step where every child commits to an answer before any is heard.

**6. Self-report instead of evidence.**
- **What goes wrong:** "Does that make sense?", thumbs, "tell me if you are stuck" (Lemov, Reject Self-Report).
- **Test:** The check asks how children feel about the idea rather than making them use it.

**7. The question gives itself away.**
- **What goes wrong:** Only one option is plausible, the right answer is longer or grammatically different, or the example is identical to the model (Christodoulou).
- **Test:** Can you get it right without the lesson's knowledge? Does each wrong option match a named misconception?

**8. The task needs knowledge not yet taught.**
- **What goes wrong:** It turns into unguided problem solving; working memory goes on searching (Kirschner, Sweller and Clark). Low attainers stall and the check shows nothing useful.
- **Test:** Can the task be done using only this chunk plus clearly secure prior knowledge? Name the knowledge each answer needs.

**9. The check leads nowhere.**
- **What goes wrong:** The teacher sees the answers, then carries on regardless (Wiliam's hinge exists to force a decision).
- **Test:** No line saying what the teacher does if many children choose the wrong option, and no quick reteach planned for the most likely error.

**10. Fact-only when the aim is reasoning.**
- **What goes wrong:** Recall tasks do not build "explain why" (Agarwal 2019).
- **Test:** Compare the lesson objective's verb with the Do beats. If the objective says explain, compare or infer, and every beat is "name", "label" or "match", there is a mismatch.

**11. Same format every time (lethal mutation).**
- **What goes wrong:** The routine survives but the thinking fades; children learn the format, not the content.
- **Test:** Three Do beats of the same type in one lesson, or the same slip layout used for different kinds of thinking.

**12. Busy and fun, but not learning.**
- **What goes wrong:** The task is justified by engagement, not by the thought it forces (Coe).
- **Test:** The plan's reason for the task is "engaging", "fun", "active" rather than a named thought ("forces them to decide the direction of the arrow").

**13. No right answer revealed.**
- **What goes wrong:** Children commit, then never find out, so errors stick.
- **Test:** There is no reveal, model answer or "check yours" step after the beat.

**14. Pair work with one thinker.**
- **What goes wrong:** One partner does it all.
- **Test:** No individual commit (a mark on a slip) before talk; roles not swapped.

**15. Timing overruns.**
- **What goes wrong:** Drawing and writing tasks swell to 5 to 10 minutes and eat the next Teach.
- **Test:** No stop rule ("60 seconds", "one arrow", "three circles"). An answer longer than a line for a 2-minute beat.

**16. Retrieval never spaced.**
- **What goes wrong:** Every Do beat is about the chunk just taught, so the lesson has checks but no real retrieval.
- **Test:** No beat that reaches back to an earlier chunk or an earlier lesson.

## Where the evidence is honest about its limits

- **Strong:** retrieval practice spaced over days, worked examples for novices, self-explanation, learner drawing with support, checking all pupils' responses.
- **Moderate:** dual coding in real classrooms (mostly short lab studies), hinge questions (strong logic, few trials), pictures to cut language load (good test-item evidence, less on classroom tasks).
- **Not supported:** learning styles and VAK matching.
- **Weak or untested:** most named classroom formats, including every one in Part 2. They are built from the principles above, not individually trialled. Treat them as reasonable designs to watch and refine, not proven techniques.
- **Children versus adults:** most retrieval and generative research used university students. The primary-age evidence (Karpicke 2014, the drawing studies) consistently says younger and lower-attaining children need **more structure**, not less.
