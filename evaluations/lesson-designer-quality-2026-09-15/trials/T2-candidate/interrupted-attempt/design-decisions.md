# Year 4 Science: To explain how sound travels from a source to our ears

## The journey, in one line

what vibrates → the rice that nothing touched → the air next to the drum wobbles → the wobble is passed on to your eardrum → explain it for a bell → no air on the Moon

## Teacher orientation

Teacher orientation: This lesson takes the class from "sounds are made when something vibrates" to how that vibration gets to an ear: the vibrating drum skin pushes the air next to it, the air passes the vibration on bit by bit, and the eardrum vibrates when it arrives. Children predict and then watch rice jump on a second drum that nothing touched, draw what the air is doing, put the chain in order for a guitar, then draw and write an explanation for a hand bell heard across the playground, and finish with the Moon question. The tricky move is the one you asked for: the sound doesn't fly across the room, the wobble is passed on through the air. It's taught on the slide headed "How does the vibration reach your ear?", with six children in a line passing a nudge from the back to the front without anyone moving from their spot. Have ready: a drum, a second drum or tambourine (or a bowl with cling film stretched tight over it) with a spoonful of rice on its skin, tried out before the lesson so the rice visibly jumps when the first drum is hit hard a few centimetres away. The main work is the bell explanation, in books, after the second Do.

## The lesson, slide by slide

### 1. Starter: Sounds and vibrations

**On the board.** Date and LO header. Three photographs, a drum being hit, a guitar string being plucked, a triangle being struck, with the questions:

(1) What is vibrating when this drum is hit?
(2) What is vibrating when this string is plucked?
(3) What is vibrating when this triangle is struck?
(4) True or false: a sound can be made without anything vibrating.

**The thought.** Which part of each instrument is actually the bit moving backwards and forwards?

**What happens.** You say: "Last time you made sounds with instruments and found out what every sound has in common. Look at these three. For each one, tell me the part that's vibrating, the bit that's moving backwards and forwards really fast. Then number four: true or false? Off you go." Every child writes four short answers. Then the check slide.

**What you land.** (1) the drum skin, (2) the string, (3) the metal of the triangle, (4) false, because every sound starts with something vibrating. A child who names the whole instrument for (1) or (2) is not yet pointing at the vibrating part, and that is the part the chain starts from.

**Why it is here.** By the end of it children can name the vibrating part of a source. That is the first link of today's chain and the second step of the success criteria, so the bell explanation later starts from something they already do.

### 2. Vocabulary: vibration, source

**On the board.** Two cards. "vibration: A vibration is when something moves backwards and forwards very quickly. A plucked guitar string does it so fast it looks blurry." beside the guitar photograph. "source: The source of a sound is the thing that's making it. Hit a drum, and the drum is the source." beside the drum photograph.

**What happens.** You say: "Two words we'll use all lesson. Vibration: that's the backwards-and-forwards movement you've just been naming. Look at the guitar string, it's a blur because it's moving so fast. Source: the thing that's making the sound. Hit a drum, and the drum is the source. Quick one. I clap my hands. What's the source? My hands. Good."

**Why it is here.** This sits beside the spine: both words are used on the very next slide, and "source" is in the objective the class copies into their books, so it gets one taught line rather than living only in the LO.

### 3. Observe: Will the rice move?

**On the board.** The prediction, and nothing that gives it away: "I'm going to hit this drum hard. The second drum has rice on its skin. Nothing is going to touch the second drum.

Will the rice move? Write yes or no, and one reason."

**The thought.** Nothing is going to touch the second drum, so is there any way its rice could move?

**What happens.** You say: "Here's a drum. Here's a second drum with some rice sitting on its skin. I'm going to hit this one, hard. I'm not going to touch the rice or the second drum at all. Will the rice move? Write yes or no, and give me one reason." Every child commits. Then: "Watch the rice." You hit the first drum hard, a few centimetres from the second. The rice jumps. "Again." It jumps again. "It moved. Nothing touched it. Keep that in your head."

**What you land, or look for.** The result itself: the rice jumped on a drum nothing touched. The reasons children wrote are the diagnostic: "no, because nothing touches it" is the everyday view that air is nothing; "yes, the sound will hit it" is the sound-just-goes idea in its rawest form. You don't correct either here; the next slide does.

**Why it is here.** It hands the class a puzzle they have watched happen: something invisible pushed that skin. The next slide can only explain it because they saw it, and the drawing beat after that is a drawing of this exact set-up.

### 4. Teach: What made the rice jump?

**On the board.** The photograph of rice bouncing on a drum skin, and the route beside it:

"The rice jumped, and nothing touched that drum. So something in between must have pushed its skin.

Between the drums there's air. You can't see it, but wave your hand in front of your face and you'll feel it: air is real stuff.

When the first drum's skin vibrated, it pushed the air next to it. That air vibrated too, and it pushed the second drum's skin."

Question band: "Nothing touched the second drum. So what pushed its skin?"

Star fact: **When something vibrates, it makes the air next to it vibrate too.**

**The thought.** Nothing touched the second drum, so what could have pushed its skin?

**What happens.** You say: "So the rice jumped, and I never touched that drum. Something must have pushed its skin. What's between the two drums? Nothing? Have a look. Wave your hand in front of your face. Feel that? That's air. You can't see it, but it's real stuff, and there's air packed all the way between these two drums. Now watch the first drum. When I hit it, the skin vibrates, backwards and forwards, really fast. Every time it moves forwards it gives the air next to it a push. So the air next to the drum starts vibrating too. And that vibrating air is what pushed the second drum's skin, and the rice jumped. Here's the bit to keep: when something vibrates, it makes the air next to it vibrate too."

**What you land.** The star fact, and the word "air" in every sentence, because the wrong idea is that the sound itself hops across and the air does nothing.

**Why it is here.** Children now hold that the air next to a vibrating source is real stuff that gets pushed and vibrates. The drawing beat asks them to picture exactly that, and the chain to the ear on the slide after starts from this bit of vibrating air.

### 5. Do: Draw what the air is doing

**On the board.** The task: "Draw the first drum, the second drum with its rice, and the air in between.

Show what the air does when the first drum is hit." The star fact from the last slide stays visible.

**The thought.** If I could see the air between the two drums, what would it be doing?

**What happens.** You say: "You can't see air, so you're going to draw it. Draw the first drum, the second drum with its rice, and then show me the air in between and what it's doing when I hit the first drum. Ninety seconds. Stick-figure drums are fine." Every child draws.

**What you look for.** The air between the two skins drawn as something that moves: wobble marks, little arrows or pushes starting at the first drum's skin and reaching the second. It doesn't need to be neat. Nothing drawn between the drums, or musical notes flying across, is the sound-just-goes idea, and the next Teach fixes it with the chain diagram: "some of you drew this, and it's nearly right".

**Why it is here.** Every child has now pictured the air as something that gets pushed and moves. The chain diagram on the next Teach is that picture extended all the way to an ear, so it lands on something they drew rather than arriving cold.

### 6. Vocabulary: eardrum

**On the board.** One card: "eardrum: Your eardrum is a thin skin stretched tight inside your ear. When a vibration reaches it, it vibrates too, and that's how you hear." beside a cutaway diagram of the ear showing the ear canal ending at the eardrum.

**What happens.** You say: "One more word before the next bit. Eardrum. Inside your ear, at the end of the tunnel, there's a thin skin stretched tight, a bit like the skin on our drum. That's your eardrum. Look at the picture: it's the thin wall at the end of the ear canal. When a vibration reaches it, it vibrates too, and that's how you hear."

**Why it is here.** Beside the spine. The next slide ends the chain at the eardrum, and children can't follow "it pushes your eardrum" without knowing there is a skin in there to push.

### 7. Teach: How does the vibration reach your ear?

**On the board.** The chain diagram: the drum on the left with its skin vibrating, a row of small circles for bits of air across the middle with each one pushing the next, and an ear cutaway on the right with the eardrum marked and vibrating. Beside it, the route:

"The air next to the drum is vibrating. But that air doesn't fly across the room to you. It pushes the air next to it, and that pushes the next bit, all the way across the room.

It's like a line of children. Nudge the one at the back and the nudge travels to the front, but nobody moves from their spot.

When the last bit of air reaches your ear, it pushes your eardrum, and your eardrum vibrates too. That's how you hear the drum."

Question band: "When I nudge the back of the line, nobody walks to the front. So what gets to the front?"

Star fact: **Sound doesn't fly to your ear. The vibration is passed on through the air, all the way to your eardrum.**

This is more than one board holds; it splits where the teaching turns, the line of children on one slide and the ear on the next, with the diagram on both.

**The thought.** If the air next to the drum stays where it is, how does the wobble get all the way to my ear?

**What happens.** Six children stand in a line facing the front, each with hands resting on the shoulders of the child in front. You say: "So the air next to the drum is wobbling. Here's the question: does that bit of air fly across the room and into your ear? No. Watch this. You six are the air. This end is the drum, and the far end is somebody's ear. Stand still, that's the point. I'm the drum. I nudge the back of the line. Watch it travel." One gentle push forward on the back child's shoulders; the lean passes down the line and the front child rocks forward. "It got to the front, didn't it? Did anybody walk? No. Everyone's still where they started. The nudge was passed along. That's exactly what the air does. The air next to the drum pushes the air next to it, and that pushes the next bit, and the next, all the way across the room. The air stays where it is. The vibration travels. And when the last bit of air reaches your ear, it pushes your eardrum, and your eardrum vibrates too. That's you hearing the drum. So the sound doesn't fly to you. The vibration is passed on through the air, all the way to your eardrum." A slinky does the same job as the line if you'd rather: push one end and the pulse travels along while the coils stay put.

**What you land.** The star fact. The second wrong idea to catch here is that the air itself blows across the room like wind; the line shows it doesn't, because nobody walked.

**Why it is here.** Children can now say how the vibration gets from the air beside the source to the eardrum. The ordering beat asks for exactly that chain on a new source, and the bell explanation asks for it in their own words.

### 8. Do: Put the chain in order

**On the board.** The scenario, then five cards in a muddle:

"Someone across the room plucks a guitar string, and you hear it.

Put the cards in order to show how the sound gets from the string to your ear. One card is wrong and belongs nowhere."

Cards, as shown: "Your eardrum vibrates." / "The air passes the vibration on, bit by bit, across the room." / "The string vibrates." / "The sound flies across the room to your ear." / "The air next to the string vibrates too."

Instruction: "Write the cards in the right order. Leave out the wrong one."

The star facts stay off this slide on purpose: this is the check that the chain is theirs.

**The thought.** Which step passes the vibration across the room, and which card would mean the sound just goes by itself?

**What happens.** You say: "New source. Someone over there plucks a guitar string and you hear it. Five cards; four of them are the chain from the string to your ear, in a muddle. One of them is wrong, and it's wrong in the way we've been talking about. Put the four in order and leave the wrong one out. If you're stuck, start with what vibrates first." Every child writes an order on a whiteboard. Two minutes, then the order read out from the notes.

**What you look for.** In order: the string vibrates → the air next to the string vibrates too → the air passes the vibration on, bit by bit, across the room → your eardrum vibrates. The wrong card is "The sound flies across the room to your ear". A child who keeps the "flies" card and drops the "passes it on" card still has the sound going by itself; go back to the line: nothing flew, the nudge was passed along. The eardrum put first is a child who has the parts but not the direction.

**Why it is here.** Children can lay out the whole chain for a new source in the right order and reject the flying-sound version. That is the shape of the bell explanation, with the sentences given; the Practise takes the sentences away.

### 9. Practise: How does the bell reach you?

**Launch slide first.** What we've established: "A vibrating source makes the air next to it vibrate, and the air passes the vibration on to your eardrum." What a good explanation looks like, on the drum the class knows, beside a weak one:

Weak: "The drum makes a sound and the sound goes to my ears."
Strong: "The drum skin vibrates. It makes the air next to it vibrate too. The air passes the vibration on, bit by bit, until it reaches my eardrum, and my eardrum vibrates."
The difference, named: the weak one says the sound just goes. The strong one says what the air does at every step.

Then the steps, in order: draw the source, the air in between and your ear; name the part of the source that is vibrating; say what it does to the air next to it; say how the vibration gets across to your ear; say what your eardrum does.

**Task slide.** A photograph of a brass hand bell, the task and the success criteria beside it:

"The hand bell rings at the far end of the playground, and you hear it.

Draw the bell, the air in between and your ear.

Explain how the sound gets from the bell to your ear."

Success criteria:
1. Draw the source, the air in between and your ear.
2. Name the part of the source that is vibrating.
3. Say what it does to the air next to it.
4. Say how the vibration gets across to your ear.
5. Say what your eardrum does.

The star facts stay off the task slide on purpose: the explanation is the evidence for the objective, and with the second star fact on the board step 4 is copied rather than written.

**The thought.** What is the air doing at every step between the bell and my ear?

**What happens.** You say: "Here's a bell you know. It rings at the far end of the playground and you hear it, all the way over by the fence. Draw the bell, the air in between and your ear. Then explain how the sound gets from the bell to your ear. Use the steps: what's vibrating, what it does to the air next to it, how the vibration gets across, and what your eardrum does. Think back to the line: nothing flies across." About ten minutes in books, then the model on the check slide.

**What you land.** The model: "The bell's metal vibrates when it's rung. It makes the air next to it vibrate too. That air pushes the air next to it, and the vibration is passed on, bit by bit, all the way across the playground. When it reaches my ear, it pushes my eardrum, and my eardrum vibrates. That's how I hear the bell." Accept any wording that has those four parts and the air doing the passing-on. Look for "the sound travels" with no air in the sentence, a straight arrow labelled "sound", and the eardrum missing.

**Why it is here.** This is the objective performed: the chain explained in their own words for a source the lesson never explained. The Moon question then takes the air away and asks what happens.

### 10. Apply: No air on the Moon

**On the board.** A photograph of an astronaut on the Moon's surface. "On the Moon there is no air.

Two astronauts stand next to each other. One hits a drum.

Does the other astronaut hear it? Write yes or no, and explain why." The second star fact is visible: the vibration is passed on through the air.

**The thought.** With no air between the drum and her ear, is there anything to pass the vibration on?

**What happens.** You say: "Last one, and it's a strange one. On the Moon there's no air at all. None. Two astronauts are standing right next to each other, and one of them hits a drum. Does the other one hear it? Yes or no, and the reason is the bit I want. Use what you know about what the air does." Every child writes, then the model.

**What you land.** "No. The drum skin still vibrates, but there's no air next to it to push. With no air, there's nothing to pass the vibration on to her eardrum, so she doesn't hear it." A child who says "yes, if the drum is touching her helmet" has understood it better than the question, because the vibration then has something solid to pass through: that's a strong answer, not a wrong one. Astronauts really do talk by radio for this reason. A plain "yes, sound just travels" is the idea the lesson set out to replace, still standing.

**Why it is here.** The idea has to go somewhere the lesson never showed it. Taking the air away is the one case that separates "the air passes the vibration on" from "the sound goes", and it is the unaided retest of the note's misconception.

---

## Read-back

By the end, children will explain how the sound of a bell reaches their ear from across the playground, because the lesson gives them the air as real stuff that a vibrating source pushes and that passes the vibration on bit by bit to the eardrum, replacing the idea that the sound just flies across, evidenced by the written bell explanation and the unaided Moon question.

Read from the other end: a child who still thinks the sound just goes writes "the bell makes a sound and it goes to my ear" for the bell and "yes" for the Moon, and both checks catch it.

## The decisions the walk-through does not show

- **The learning.** Children will understand that a sound reaches them because a vibration is passed on: the source vibrates, it makes the air next to it vibrate, each bit of air pushes the next, and the eardrum vibrates when the vibration arrives. It is an idea, not a fact about one drum: the same question ("how does the vibration get there?") is asked over changing evidence, the untouched second drum, the class's own ears, a guitar across the room, a bell across the playground, and a drum with no air round it. It is recorded as the lesson's one concept and the Teach, Do, Practise and Apply beats are its instances.
- **The objective and boundary.** The LO is used exactly as written and displayed whole. Today's boundary is vibrations travelling through the air to the ear (the Year 4 statement, in the teacher's words). Deliberately out: the word "medium", other materials (water, wood), sounds getting fainter with distance, pitch and volume, the word "particle" (the air is drawn as "bits of air"), and "sound waves", which is later learning. The Moon answer's helmet case is accepted as a strong answer without being taught.
- **Prior knowledge and structure.** The brief says children know sounds are made when something vibrates and have named vibrating parts; the starter makes that visible without reteaching it. New today: the air as real stuff that is pushed, the passing-on, the eardrum. Content-based, because the learning is an explanation to build and use; Discovery was the nearest alternative and was rejected because the mechanism (air being pushed and passing it on) is invisible and cannot be found by observing, so the demonstration is a bounded observe beat that gives the class something to explain, followed by direct teaching. Independence: predict → drawing of the class case → chain ordered with the sentences given → chain written for a new source → transfer with the air removed.
- **The dominant sticking point.** "The sound just goes": the sound leaves the source and gets to the ear by itself, and the air does nothing. Exposed in the Observe predictions and the Do 1 drawings; replaced on Teach 1 (the air pushed the skin) and Teach 2 (the line of children); checked once on Do 2 (the "flies across" card); retested unaided on the Apply (no air). Secondary, one touch only: "the air itself blows across like wind", answered by the line where nobody walks, plus a teacher line on Teach 2.
- **Independent evidence.** The bell explanation, with the star facts off the slide so step 4 has to be written rather than copied, and the Moon question, where a child holding the wrong idea answers "yes" and a child holding the right one has to connect "no air" to "nothing to pass it on". Neither can be answered from a surface cue: the bell slide shows a bell, not a chain, and the Moon slide shows an astronaut.
- **Success criteria and worksheet.** Five how-to steps in the child's words, because the task is carrying out an explanation in a fixed order; not marked draw-live, because it is a one-lesson method. The worksheet is a fresh alternative with its own source: a cymbal crashed at the front of the hall (drawing plus explanation, the steps printed), then the retest in a new form, two explanations of hearing a dog bark, one "flies through the air like a ball being thrown", one with the air passing the wobble on, and "which is better, and why". The steps are the one thing pre-authorised to come off the sheet if it will not fit, because the board carries them.
- **Load-bearing visuals and equipment.** The two-drum rice demonstration is the evidence the first Teach explains; the photograph of rice bouncing on a drum skin is its record on the board and the fallback if the equipment fails. The chain diagram (source, bits of air pushing each other, ear with eardrum) is the representation for Teach 2 and the thing a working wall would keep. The ear cutaway sits on the eardrum card. Starter and vocabulary photographs show the vibrating parts. The line of children is teaching, not a Do: one gentle push, hands on shoulders, and a slinky does the same job.
- **Reasoning.** The Apply is the reasoning shape: the taught relationship with one condition removed (change one thing). The Do 2 wrong card and the worksheet comparison are the same judgement in smaller forms.
- **Light line.** The line of children handed one over, and it is in the Teach 2 script: "You six are the air. Stand still, that's the point."
- **Time, and what went.** Starter and check about seven minutes, the two vocabulary moments three, predict-and-watch three, Teach 1 four, the drawing three, Teach 2 with the line five, the ordering three, launch and bell explanation twelve, the Moon four: about forty-four with transitions. An "ear on the desk" beat (the vibration through wood) was designed and cut: it is a second material, which is beyond today's boundary, and its three minutes were the bell explanation's. Sticky facts stay off the Practise and the Do 2 slides on purpose, and are on the Apply because connecting "no air" to the fact is the thinking there.
- **Deliberate omissions.** No separate stick-in piece: every drawing is freehand of things a child can draw, and the ordering is written on whiteboards. No pitch, loudness or distance. No named character on the Apply, because a direct question carries the same thinking with less reading.
- **flagsForTeacher.** One: the lesson turns on a two-drum rice demonstration (a drum, a second drum or tambourine or cling-film bowl, a spoonful of rice, tested beforehand). If none of it is available, the Teach 1 photograph of rice bouncing on a drum skin carries the point, but the class then sees the result rather than watches it, and the prediction beat becomes a shorter "what do you think happened?".
