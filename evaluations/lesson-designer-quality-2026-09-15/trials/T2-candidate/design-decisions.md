# Year 4 Science: To explain how sound travels from a source to our ears

## The journey, in one line

The drum you can see shaking → the air right next to it is shaking too → each bit of air passes the shake on → your eardrum shakes → take the air away and nothing arrives → trace a brand new sound the whole way.

## Teacher orientation

Teacher orientation: Children already know that sounds are made when something vibrates. Today they learn what happens next: the vibrating object shakes the air right next to it, each bit of air passes the shaking on to the bit beside it, and the shaking arrives at the eardrum. They record a prediction about a speaker and a piece of tissue paper, a yes or no about shouting in space, the order of a sound's journey from a bank of cards, and then a written explanation of how a clap at the front of the room reached them at the back. The tricky move is that nothing flies across the room: each bit of air only shakes on the spot and hands the shake on, and that is taught on the path diagram in "How does the shaking reach you?". Have a speaker or tablet and a strip of tissue paper ready for the demonstration. The main written work sits near the end, at "Trace the clap to your ear".

## Slide by slide

### 1. Sounds we made: starter

**On the board.** A photograph of classroom percussion (a drum, a triangle, a tambourine) and three numbered questions.

> (1) What has to happen to make a sound?
> (2) You press your hand flat on a drum skin that is still ringing. Why does the sound stop?
> (3) A triangle is hanging still. Is it making a sound? How do you know?

**The thought.** What is going on in something that is making a sound?

**What happens.** The teacher reads the three questions and children answer in books or on whiteboards. Then the answer slide: something has to vibrate; pressing the skin stops it vibrating, so the sound stops; the still triangle makes no sound because nothing is vibrating.

**What you land.** Sound starts with something vibrating - which is where today picks up.

**Why it is here.** It puts the vibrating object back in children's hands, and every beat after it starts from that vibrating object.

### 2. Key words: sound source, vibration

**On the board.** Two cards.

> **sound source** - the thing that is making the sound. It is the thing that starts vibrating.
> **vibration** - a very fast shaking, backwards and forwards, over and over.

**The thought.** None - the teacher introduces the two words.

**What happens.** The teacher reads both cards, pointing at the drum photograph for the source and the jumping rice for the vibration, then asks quickly: is the beater the source, or the drum? (The drum: it is the thing that vibrates.)

**Why it is here.** Beside the spine. Children need both words before the first Teach can be followed, and they use them in every beat after it.

### 3. What is the air doing?: Teach 1

**On the board.** A photograph of grains of rice jumping on a drum skin as it is struck, with the route in whole sentences:

> **A drum skin, and the air touching it.**
> You already know this bit. The drum is our sound source, and its skin vibrates when we hit it. That's what is throwing the rice up.
> Here's the new bit. A sound source doesn't vibrate in an empty space. There's air pressed right up against that skin, and every time the skin moves, it pushes the air.
> So look at the rice, and then think about the air right beside it.
> **The sound source is shaking. What do you think the air right next to it is doing?**
> ★ A vibrating object makes the air right next to it vibrate too.

**The thought.** If the skin is pushing the air next to it, what is that air doing?

**What happens.** The teacher walks the route aloud, pointing at the rice, and lands the star fact. Children answer the question to the room while looking at the photograph.

**What you land.** A vibrating object makes the air right next to it vibrate too.

**Why it is here.** It puts the air into the story for the first time, and the prediction that follows cannot be made without it.

### 4. Will the paper move?: Do 1

**On the board.** A photograph of a loudspeaker with its cone visible, and the case.

> This speaker is about to play some music. The front of it will vibrate, in and out, very fast.
>
> The tissue paper isn't touching it. What will the paper do? Write what you think, and one reason why.

**The thought.** If the speaker is shaking the air, what will that air do to a piece of paper hanging in it?

**What happens.** Every child writes a prediction and a reason before anything plays. Then the teacher holds a strip of tissue paper about 10 cm in front of the speaker and plays music. The paper flutters hard.

**What you look for.** Children who wrote that it will shake because the vibrating air is pushing it have the idea. Children who wrote "nothing, because sound is invisible" are holding the wrong rule, and that is worth knowing before the next Teach.

**Why it is here.** They have seen that vibrating air pushes whatever is sitting in it, which is exactly what the eardrum beat needs.

### 5. How does the shaking reach you?: Teach 2

**On the board.** The path diagram: the drum on the left, a row of separate bits of air across the middle, an ear on the right, and no arrows yet. The teacher draws the passing-on arrows one at a time as they talk.

> **From the drum to your ear.**
> So the air right next to the drum is vibrating. Now watch what that air does to the air beside it.
> Each bit of air gives the next bit a tiny push, and then that bit pushes the one after it. The vibration gets handed on, bit by bit, all the way across the room.
> Here's the part that catches people out. The air doesn't fly across the room to you. Each bit of air just shakes where it is and passes the shake on.
> **Follow my arrows. What is passing the vibration along to your ear?**
> ★ Each bit of air passes the vibration on to the next bit, all the way to your ear.

**The thought.** If each bit of air only shakes on the spot, how does the vibration get all the way to me?

**What happens.** The teacher draws each arrow and narrates it, so the class watches the vibration move without the air moving. By the end, every gap between neighbouring bits of air has an arrow in it and the last arrow reaches the ear.

**What you land.** Each bit of air passes the vibration on to the next bit, all the way to your ear.

**Why it is here.** This is the lesson. Everything after it (the space case, the ordering, the written explanation) is children using this chain.

*(If this will not fit one board at a readable size, it splits where the teaching turns: the handing-on on one, the "the air doesn't fly across the room" on the next, with the diagram on both.)*

### 6. Could you hear it in space?: Do 2

**On the board.** A photograph of an astronaut on a spacewalk, and the case.

> Out in space there is no air at all. None.
>
> Two astronauts are floating right next to each other. One of them shouts as loudly as they can.
>
> Would the other one hear it? Write yes or no, and say why.

**The thought.** If there is no air out there, what is left to pass the shaking along?

**What happens.** Every child commits to yes or no with a reason before anything is said. Then the teacher takes it: no. The shouting astronaut's throat still vibrates, but there is nothing between them to pass the vibration on, so nothing arrives at the other one's ear. That is why astronauts talk to each other on the radio - they can be floating an arm's length apart and still have to call each other up.

**What you look for.** "No, because there's no air to pass it on." A child who writes "yes, because the sound would still go" has the wrong rule and has just met the case where it fails.

**Why it is here.** They have decided that with nothing to pass the vibration on there is no sound, which is what makes the air a real part of the explanation rather than the gap in between.

### 7. Key word: eardrum

**On the board.** One card beside the ear diagram.

> **eardrum** - a thin piece of skin stretched tight just inside your ear, like the skin on a drum.

**The thought.** None - the teacher introduces the word.

**What happens.** The teacher reads the card and points to the eardrum on the diagram.

**Why it is here.** Beside the spine. Children need the word to follow the Teach that comes next, and they use it in the success criteria and in their written explanation.

### 8. What happens when it reaches your ear?: Teach 3

**On the board.** A labelled diagram of an ear: the outer ear, the canal running inwards, and the eardrum stretched across the end of it.

> **An ear, from the outside in.**
> The vibration has travelled all the way across the room, and it has just arrived at your ear.
> Just inside, at the end of the little tunnel, is your eardrum: a thin piece of skin pulled tight, exactly like the skin on the drum we started with.
> The vibrating air arrives and pushes on it, so the eardrum starts vibrating too. Your brain turns that into the sound you hear.
> **Your eardrum is like the drum skin we started with. What do you think the vibrating air does to it?**
> ★ The vibrating air makes your eardrum vibrate, and that is when you hear the sound.

**The thought.** What does the vibrating air do to something as thin as an eardrum?

**What happens.** The teacher points along the canal to the eardrum while talking, so the class can see where the vibration arrives. Children answer the question looking at the diagram.

**What you land.** The vibrating air makes your eardrum vibrate, and that is when you hear the sound.

**Why it is here.** It closes the chain. The written explanation has a last step to write because of this beat.

### 9. Put the journey in order: Do 3

**On the board.** Five cards, scrambled, and one instruction.

> A. Your eardrum starts to vibrate.
> B. Each bit of air passes the vibration on to the next bit.
> C. The school bell is struck and starts to vibrate.
> D. The sound flies across the room on its own.
> E. The air right next to the bell starts to vibrate.
>
> Four of these five things happen when you hear the school bell. One of them never happens at all.
>
> Write the four letters in the order they happen, then the odd letter out and why.

**The thought.** Which of these actually happens, and in what order?

**What happens.** Every child writes the order and the rejected letter. Then it is taken as a class: C, E, B, A, and D is out because a sound is not a thing that can fly anywhere on its own - it is a vibration being passed on through the air.

**What you look for.** Children who keep D, or who put B before E, are still holding the sound as a travelling object.

**Why it is here.** They can lay a sound's journey out in the right order with the wording supplied, which is exactly what the written explanation asks them to do without it.

### 10. Trace the clap to your ear: launch and practice

**On the launch board.**

> We know a sound starts with a sound source vibrating, that the air right next to it vibrates too, that each bit of air passes the vibration on, and that your eardrum vibrates at the end.
>
> Two answers to a different question: how you heard a whistle blown across the playground.
>
> A weak one: "The whistle made a noise and the noise went over to my ear." It never says what the air is doing.
>
> A strong one: "The whistle vibrated when the teacher blew it. The air right next to the whistle started vibrating. Each bit of air passed the vibration on to the next, all the way over to me. The vibrating air made my eardrum vibrate, and that's when I heard it."
>
> The difference: the strong one says what the air is doing at every step.

The worked example is deliberately a whistle rather than the clap, so the launch shows the standard without printing the answer to the task the class is about to write.

**On the task board.** The success criteria panel, and the task.

> Your teacher claps their hands at the front of the room. You're sitting right at the back, and you hear it straight away.
>
> Explain how the sound of that clap got all the way to your ear.

Success criteria, visible while they write:

> 1. Name the sound source, the thing that vibrated.
> 2. Say what the air right next to it did.
> 3. Say how the vibration got all the way across the room.
> 4. Say what happened at your eardrum.

**The thought.** What was the air doing between their hands and my ear?

**What happens.** Children write four sentences in their books, one for each step. The teacher's clap is a real event in the room, so the case needs no picture. The answer slide afterwards carries the strong answer as a model.

**What you look for.** Whether the air is doing something in every sentence, or whether the middle of the journey has gone missing.

**Why it is here.** It is the lesson's evidence: a fresh sound source, a real distance, and the whole chain written by each child on their own.

### 11. You heard it, so what must have happened?: Apply

**On the board.** A photograph of a house's back door, seen from the garden, and the case.

> You are in the garden with your back to the house. You hear the back door close behind you. You didn't see anything at all.
>
> Write two things that must have happened, even though you couldn't see them.

**The thought.** If I heard it and saw nothing, what had to be going on that I couldn't see?

**What happens.** Two minutes, written. Then the model: something must have vibrated, the door as it hit the frame, and the air between the door and my ear must have passed that vibration on, bit by bit, until it reached my eardrum.

**Why it is here.** It is the lesson's own question turned round. Every beat so far ran forwards from a source; this one starts from the hearing and reasons backwards to the vibration and the air, which is a move the practice never asked for.

---

## The read-back

By the end, children will explain how a sound gets from a source to their ear, because the lesson replaces "the sound just goes" with a vibration that is passed on from one bit of air to the next until it reaches the eardrum, evidenced by each child's own written explanation of a clap they only heard.

Read the other way: would the clap explanation catch a child who does not hold the passing-on? Yes - step 2 and step 3 of the criteria are exactly the part that child cannot write, and the weak answer on the launch board is written out so the class can see what missing it looks like.

## The decisions the walk-through does not show

**The learning.** Children will know that a vibrating object sets the air beside it vibrating, that each bit of air hands the vibration on to the next while staying where it is, and that the vibration finishes by making the eardrum vibrate. It is a **fact** in the sense this file uses: one mechanism, developed on one well-chosen case (a sound travelling through air), then used on fresh sources. It is not filed as an idea in `concepts`, because an idea is learned across instances with the evidence changing, and the instance that would change here is the *material* (sound through a solid or through water), which is outside today's objective. Today's transfer is to new sources in air, which the practice and the Apply both supply.

**The objective.** `To explain how sound travels from a source to our ears`, used word for word on the board. Nothing added: the brief's own note (the vibration travels through the air) is the route I teach it by, not a second assessed outcome. End performance: a written four-step explanation of an unseen sound reaching the child's own ear.

**Curriculum boundary.** In: vibrations travelling through air from a source to the ear, and the eardrum vibrating. Out, deliberately: pitch, volume, how sound fades with distance, sound through solids and liquids, and anything about particles, waves, compressions or the inner ear. Each of those is a later lesson in the Year 4 sound unit and teaching it today would empty one.

**Prior knowledge.** The brief says children have made sounds with instruments and know sounds are made when something vibrates. The starter makes that visible rather than assuming it, and the word `vibration` is re-carded because every sentence of today's explanation runs on it. No previous lesson files were supplied, so nothing is reused verbatim from an earlier design.

**Structure.** Content-based. Children are building and explaining scientific knowledge, and the route is Teach → Do three times into a larger practice. Discovery was the genuine alternative and I compared the actual pupil work: everything a Year 4 class can safely observe (a drum skin, a tuning fork in water, a speaker cone) shows the *source* vibrating, which the brief says they already hold. Nothing observable shows the air's part, so the finding would have to be told anyway and the lesson's time would go to re-establishing prior learning. The two bounded observations that are worth having (the tissue paper and the arrows drawn live) sit inside the content route instead, at two minutes each.

**The dominant sticking point.** `The sound leaves the drum and flies across the room on its own; the air is just the empty space in between.` Exposed and replaced in "How does the shaking reach you?", met as a case where it visibly fails in "Could you hear it in space?", and retested once in "Put the journey in order" through the card that does not belong. It is not re-run after that: the practice and the Apply ask for the objective's own explanation, not for the correction again. A second, lighter misconception (that the air itself travels across the room like a puff of breath) gets one clean touch, the third line of Teach 2's board, plus one judged claim on the worksheet.

**Independent assessment evidence.** The written clap explanation. It cannot be passed from the board: the criteria name four jobs without supplying any of their content, the source is new, and the weak answer printed on the launch slide is precisely the one a child gives from everyday sense, so copying it fails. Worked the other way, a child holding "the sound just goes" writes step 1 and step 4 and has nothing for steps 2 and 3.

**Success criteria.** Four how-to steps, kept visible through the practice and reused unchanged on the worksheet. Steps rather than a labelled set, because the child is carrying out a procedure (explaining a journey in order) rather than matching an instance to categories. Marked `drawLive`, because the whole sound unit that follows assumes this chain and it is worth writing up large and leaving on the wall.

**Worksheet.** Generated, per child, optional separate practice. Three questions: complete the three missing boxes on a drawn sound path from an ice-cream van's chime to a child in a garden; explain how you hear a balloon pop; and judge Mia's claim that her breath travels across the playground to her friend's ear. It prices at about 185 mm of a 250 mm page, so nothing is pre-authorised for removal.

**Load-bearing visuals.** The sound path diagram (`rep-001`) carries the lesson's whole explanation and is completed live in Teach 2; the labelled ear diagram (`rep-002`) is what makes the eardrum a real place rather than a word. Four photographs: classroom percussion for the starter, rice jumping on a drum skin for Teach 1 and the `vibration` card, a loudspeaker for Do 1 and the `sound source` card, an astronaut on a spacewalk for Do 2, and a back door from the garden for the Apply (five in total, and the back door is the one the lesson survives without).

**Safety.** One specific precaution, held in the teacher's information for Do 1: keep the volume moderate and hold the tissue paper about 10 cm from the speaker without touching it. No generic safety line anywhere, because nothing else in this lesson puts equipment in a child's hands.

**Reasoning and problem solving.** The shape is "change one condition and say what follows", and it sits in "Could you hear it in space?" - the air is removed, everything else is held still, and the answer is decided only by the taught mechanism. The Apply then runs the objective's own question backwards.

**Deliberate omissions.** No enactment beat (a line of children passing a nudge along): it is a good model and it costs four or five minutes of movement with thirty children that the arrows on the diagram buy for nothing. No bell jar and vacuum pump: the space case does the same job and needs no equipment. No card kit at tables for the ordering: five short cards read from the board and answered with letters is faster, and the beat before the main work is the one place the lesson has no minutes spare.

**The light line.** The material handed one over and it is in Do 2's reveal: astronauts can be floating an arm's length apart and still have to call each other up on the radio. True, and it is the lesson's own point arriving as something worth telling someone at home.
