# To explain why the Vikings came to Britain - Year 4 History, 45 minutes

## The journey, in one line

The land a Viking family actually had to live on → there isn't enough of it, and there are more children every year → what a British monastery held, and how easily a ship reached it → which target a captain would pick → the wide flat fields of eastern Britain → village names that are still on the map → why each of two ships crossed the sea → take the silver away, would they still have come?

## Teacher orientation

Teacher orientation: This lesson builds three reasons the Vikings came to Britain and then has children write them down. They look at the steep, cramped land a Viking family farmed, at what a British monastery held and how easy it was to reach, and at the wide flat farmland that made whole families pack up and move here. They finish by writing two short explanations in their books, one for a ship that raided and one for a family that settled. The tricky move is that raiding and settling are two different things done by the same people; it is taught across the last two chunks, and children first have to choose between them on the village-names slide. Print the two ships' stories if your weaker readers need them at the table, and the main writing sits at tables after the village-names slide.

## The lesson, slide by slide

### Slide 1 - Where the Vikings came from (starter)

Blank `Date` label and the LO across the top.

**On the board.** A map of the North Sea with the Scandinavian peninsula marked A and Britain marked B, no names printed. Beside it, four questions:

- Which letter shows Scandinavia, where the Vikings lived?
- Which letter shows Britain?
- Who was already living in Britain before the Vikings came?
- Scandinavia and Britain are separated by sea, so how did the Vikings have to travel here?

**The thought.** Scandinavia is over there and Britain is over here, so what is in between?

**What happens.** You read the map with them and they answer the four. The first two are quick and the last two are the ones worth waiting for: the Anglo-Saxons were already here, and the only way across was by ship. Then the check slide.

**What you land.** The Anglo-Saxons were living here. The Vikings had to cross the sea in ships to reach them.

**Why it is here.** It puts the crossing and the people already here in mind, which is what the first Teach builds its reason on top of. Without it, "they came" has nowhere to start from.

### Slide 2 - Why did Viking families need more land? (Teach 1)

**On the board.** A photograph of a Norwegian fjord: steep rock straight down to the water, one thin strip of green with a small farm on it. Beside it:

*This is where a Viking family lived and farmed.*

*Every family needs food on the table and somewhere warm to sleep, and a Viking family got all of that from their own land.*

*Their land was a thin strip of green between the mountain and the water. Look at how little of it there is.*

*When a farm was passed on it nearly always went to the eldest son, so his brothers grew up knowing there would be nothing left for them.*

Question: *Look at the green strip beside the water. How many families could feed themselves from that?*

Star fact: *In Viking lands the ground was steep and rocky, so there was very little farmland and many young people had none of their own.*

**The thought.** If the farm goes to the eldest brother, what happens to the others?

**What happens.** You point at the strip of green and ask them to count the fields. They will see there are barely any. Then the inheritance: the farm goes to one son, and the others get nothing. This is where you say out loud that we are not asking whether the Vikings were nice or nasty, we are asking what they needed.

**What you land.** There was very little farmland, and many young Vikings had none of their own.

**Why it is here.** It gives them the shortage at home, which Do 1 pushes on and which both practice explanations need. Nothing later works without it: without the shortage, the whole lesson is Vikings deciding to be unpleasant.

### Slide 3 - More children, the same farms (Do 1)

**On the board.** *Viking families often had six or seven children who grew up. The farms never got any bigger.*

Then: *Write one sentence: what did that mean for young Vikings?*

**The thought.** More people every year, and the same small farms. Who ends up with nothing?

**What happens.** Every child writes one sentence. You are listening for the shortage getting worse, not just repeating that it existed.

**Look for.** More people, the same land; land running out; having to leave to find land.

**Why it is here.** It turns a fact into a pressure. They can now say the problem was growing, which is what makes getting into a boat worth the risk in the practice.

### Slide 4 - Key vocabulary: raid, monastery

**On the board.** Two cards.

*raid* - A raid is a quick attack to take things and then leave. The people who raid do not stay. (No picture: nothing you can photograph is a raid, and every candidate in this lesson names something else.)

*monastery* - A monastery was a group of buildings where monks lived, prayed and copied out books by hand. (Beside it, the Lindisfarne photograph.)

**Why it is here.** Both words are needed to follow the next slide, and there is no meaning to show them first. This one sits beside the spine.

### Slide 5 - Why did the Vikings raid monasteries? (Teach 2)

**On the board.** The ruins of Lindisfarne Priory with the sea close behind, and a photograph of a Viking-age silver hoard. Beside them:

*Monks lived and prayed at Lindisfarne, on an island off the north-east coast of England.*

*So a young Viking needed land, or he needed something valuable enough to buy it. Neither was at home.*

*Across the sea, Britain's monasteries had been collecting gifts for hundreds of years: silver crosses, gold cups, book covers set with jewels. They were built close to the water, and monks did not fight, so there were no soldiers and no walls.*

*Look at how near the sea comes. A longship could be pulled up on that beach, and the crew could be away again before anyone inland even knew.*

Question: *Look at how close the sea comes. How far would the crew have to carry the silver?*

Star fact: *British monasteries held gold and silver, stood close to the sea, and had no soldiers to defend them.*

**The thought.** Of all the rich places in Britain, why choose this one?

**What happens.** You show them the silver first so "treasure" is a real object and not a word, then the island. Three things together make it worth the journey: the silver, nobody to fight back, and a ship that can land right beside it. Tell them the date: ships came to Lindisfarne in 793.

**What you land.** The monasteries held gold and silver, sat by the sea, and had nobody to defend them.

**Why it is here.** Do 2 is a choice between three targets and cannot be made without those three features, and the raiding half of the practice explanation rests on the same three.

### Slide 6 - Which one would a Viking captain raid? (Do 2)

**On the board.** *A Viking captain has one ship and one summer. He can only raid one of these places.*

Three options:

- A monastery on a small island: a stone church, silver crosses, and twelve monks.
- A farming village a day's walk inland: barley, a few cows, and no silver.
- A king's hall a mile up the river, with his war-band eating inside.

Then: *Tick the place he would choose. Write one sentence saying why.*

**The thought.** Where is the treasure that nobody is guarding?

**What happens.** Every child commits. The king's hall is the tempting wrong answer, because that is where a child assumes the wealth is, and it is exactly where the fighting men are. Take two reasons before you confirm.

**Look for.** Silver worth taking; nobody to fight back; the ship can reach it. Watch for children choosing the king's hall because that is where the most wealth is.

**Why it is here.** It makes them use the three features rather than hear them. They can now say what made a place worth raiding, which the practice needs.

### Slide 7 - Key vocabulary: settle

**On the board.** *settle* - To settle means to move to a new place and stay there, building homes and farming the land. Beside it, the photograph of flat English farmland.

**Why it is here.** They need the word to follow the next slide's teaching. Beside the spine.

### Slide 8 - Why did Viking families come to stay? (Teach 3)

**On the board.** A photograph of wide, flat farmland beside a river in eastern England. Beside it:

*These are the fields Viking crews found in the east of Britain.*

*Those first ships came, took what they could carry and sailed home again. That is a raid.*

*But the crews had seen something on the way. The land in the east of Britain was low and flat, with wide fields beside the rivers and good soil for wheat and barley. There was more farmland in one valley than in a whole Viking fjord.*

*So within a lifetime, families were loading their sheep, their ploughs and their sacks of seed corn onto the ships. They were not coming to take things home. They were coming to live here.*

Question: *Sheep, a plough and seed corn. What does a family bring those for?*

Star fact: *A raid meant sailing over, taking what was valuable and sailing home. Settling meant bringing your family and staying to farm.*

**The thought.** Why put your children and your animals on a boat instead of just taking the silver?

**What happens.** Ask them to remember the fjord from the start of the lesson, then show them this. The cargo list is the thing to point at: you do not load sheep and seed corn for a raid, because there is nowhere to put the silver on the way home.

**What you land.** Raiding and settling are two different things, and the same people did both.

**Why it is here.** It gives them the second half of the distinction, which Do 3 tests and which the practice asks them to choose between on each ship.

### Slide 9 - Viking names on today's map (Do 3)

**On the board.** *Whitby. Selby. Danby. Thirkleby.*

*These are all real places in the north of England. Their names end in -by, which was the Viking word for a village or a farm.*

Then: *Do these names show Vikings raiding Britain, or Vikings settling in Britain? Write which one, and how you know.*

**The thought.** Would a crew who sailed home in the autumn bother naming a village?

**What happens.** Every child writes one word and one line. If they are stuck, ask them: if you were only here for one summer, would you give somebody's village a new name? That is the whole answer and they can reach it.

**Look for.** They stayed; they lived there; the names are still on the map today.

**Why it is here.** It is the one supported go at the raid-or-settle decision before the practice asks for it twice, and it is the point where "the Vikings only raided" is visibly wrong.

### Slide 10 - Two Viking ships, two reasons (launch)

**On the board.** What we have found: there was not enough farmland at home, Britain's monasteries were full of silver and had nobody guarding them, and Britain's fields were wide and flat.

A weak answer beside a strong one:

- Weak: *Sigurd came to get treasure.*
- Strong: *Sigurd came because there was not enough farmland at home for him, and the monastery had silver and nobody to fight back.*

The difference: the strong one says what he did not have and what Britain had.

Then the steps: read both ships' stories; write about Sigurd's ship first; then write about Halla's.

### Slide 11 - Two Viking ships, two reasons (Practise)

**On the board.**

*Some Viking ships came to Britain and sailed home again. Others carried whole families who never went back.*

*Sigurd's ship left Norway in the spring and was home before the winter. It carried back a silver cross, two gold cups and a book cover set with jewels.*

*Halla's ship left the same village that summer. On board were Halla, her husband, their three children, six sheep and sacks of seed corn. They never came home. They ploughed a field beside a river in Yorkshire.*

*Why did Vikings like Sigurd sail to Britain?*

*Why did families like Halla's sail to Britain?*

Success criteria, visible while they write:

1. Say what the Vikings did not have enough of at home.
2. Say what Britain had that they wanted.
3. Say whether this ship came to raid or to settle.
4. Use one detail from the ship's story to show how you know.

**The thought.** What did this family not have, and what was here that they wanted?

**What happens.** They move to tables and write two short explanations in their books, about eight minutes. Then the model goes up and they check their own against it.

**What you land.** The two ships crossed the same sea for different reasons, and both reasons started with what was missing at home.

**Why it is here.** It is the objective. They have written more than one reason down, which is what the closing question then asks them to weigh.

### Slide 12 - What if the monasteries had been poor? (Apply)

**On the board.**

*Imagine every monastery in Britain had been poor, with nothing inside but bread and books.*

*Would Viking ships still have come to Britain? Write yes or no, and one reason.*

*So was silver the only reason the Vikings came?*

**The thought.** Take the silver away. Is there anything left that would still bring them here?

**What happens.** Thirty seconds to commit, then hands. Children who say no have usually forgotten Halla; send them back to what they just wrote about her. Then the reveal, and the second question aloud.

**What you land.** Yes, ships would still have come, because the farmland was here whether the monasteries were rich or not. Silver was one reason and never the only one.

**Why it is here.** It is the last beat and nothing follows it. It takes the lesson's own question and turns it round, which is the only place a child finds out whether they are holding one reason or three.

---

## The read-back

By the end, children will explain why the Vikings came to Britain, giving more than one reason and telling raiding from settling, because the lesson gives them what a Viking family did not have at home and what Britain had that they wanted, in place of the idea that the Vikings came because they were cruel, evidenced by two independent written explanations, one for a ship that raided and one for a family that settled, each naming a shortage, a pull and a detail from its own story.

---

## The decisions the walk-through does not show

**The learning.** Children will know that Scandinavian families were short of farmland, that British monasteries were rich and undefended, and that eastern Britain had far more good farmland than home; and they will be able to say that a raid and a settlement are different things done by the same people. The kind of learning is an idea, cause: a movement of people happens for more than one reason, and each reason comes from something lacked at home and something offered by the new place. Its instances are the three Teach→Do pairs, the practice and the closing question; the evidence changes each time (the fjord, the monastery, the fields, two ships' cargoes, the monasteries emptied) while the question holds still.

**Objective and boundary.** The teacher's objective is used exactly as written and the board shows it unchanged. The end performance is two short written explanations. Today covers why they came: the shortage at home, the wealth of the monasteries, the farmland, and the raid/settle distinction. Deliberately left for later lessons: the Danelaw and Alfred, the Battle of Stamford Bridge and 1066, Viking gods and burial, and the detail of how a longship was built.

**Prior knowledge and structure.** The brief gives Scandinavia and Britain on a map and the Anglo-Saxons here first, so the starter retrieves exactly that and adds the sea crossing, which is the fact the lesson's first reason sits on. The new learning is knowledge, not a procedure, so the structure is Content-based: three chunks, each used before the next arrives. Dialogic was the nearest alternative and was rejected because the question is not genuinely contested and the children have no knowledge to speak from yet; task-centred was rejected because they hold almost none of the content at the start.

**The dominant sticking point.** Children explain the Vikings by character: they came because they were violent and greedy. It is pre-empted rather than argued with, by teaching what a Viking family needed before any raid is mentioned (Teach 1), so the first time raiding appears it is already a practical decision. It is met head-on at Do 3, where the place names make "they only raided" visibly wrong, and retested once at the Apply, where taking the silver away leaves the farmland standing. The second misconception, that raiding and settling are the same thing, is handled at Teach 3 and Do 3 and retested in the worksheet's last question.

**The independent evidence.** The two practice explanations. A child cannot pass them from the stimulus alone: the ship stories say what each ship carried but never why, and the criteria require a shortage at home, which is only in the lesson. Worked the other way, a child holding the wrong rule writes "Sigurd came because Vikings liked stealing" and fails criteria 1 and 2 outright, which is what makes the task a test of the learning rather than of attention.

**Success criteria and worksheet.** Four steps, because the child is carrying out a procedure of sorts: find the lack, find the pull, name the purpose, evidence it. Marked plain except for the two taught words in step 3. Not marked `drawLive`: it is written for these two ships and would not mean anything on a wall. The worksheet is a generated per-child question set with four fresh cases, no printed criteria (the criteria name "this ship's story", which is not on the sheet), and the raid/settle fact printed as the sheet's one reference.

**Load-bearing material.** The fjord photograph and the flat-farmland photograph are the lesson's comparison and both are essential; they sit on separate slides rather than one board, with the script carrying the comparison, because a matched evidence set would have to be all real with no generated fallback and a missing picture would cost more than the side-by-side gains. The Lindisfarne photograph is essential and must be the real place, because the lesson names it. The silver hoard is worth having and the lesson survives without it. The raid of 793 is taught as the taking of the treasure; the killing is left out, which is a teacher judgement and is flagged.

**The amount, and what went.** The honest budget is a five-minute starter, three Teach→Do pairs at six to seven minutes each, a two-minute launch, eight minutes of writing with a two-minute check, and three minutes on the closing question. That fills the forty-five and left no room for a fourth chunk on how a longship crossed the open sea, which was cut: it is genuinely interesting and it answers a question nobody in this lesson is asking.

**Reasoning.** The lesson's reasoning shape is the closing question turned round, at the Apply: remove the reason children are most likely to over-weight and ask whether the conclusion survives. It is every child's, not an extension, and it is thirty seconds of writing.

**The light line.** The material offered one and it is Do 2's king's hall, where the obvious rich target is the one full of men with axes; the script takes it lightly ("and everyone in it is armed, and they have just had their dinner"). Nothing else here is funny and the raid itself should not be made so.

**The invented people's names.** The run drew Zoe, Priya, Arjun and Harry. They are not used, because every invented person in this lesson is a Viking of the eighth or ninth century, and a Viking longship captain called Harry would be a lie about the period. Sigurd, Halla and Ulf are ordinary Norse names, and each opens on what was true for many people before the story starts, so the question is about the group with the person as the example.

**Deliberate omissions.** No timeline, because the lesson holds only one period and the dates that matter sit in their own sentences. No card kit, because the only sort-shaped beat is a two-minute tick that belongs on the board. No separate answer slide for the Do beats, per the delivery rule.

**Flags.** Two: no unit plan or enquiry question was supplied, so the lesson stands alone and names what it banks; and the Lindisfarne violence is left out as a judgement the teacher owns.
