# Year 4 Maths: To multiply a two-digit number by a one-digit number using partitioning

## The journey, in one line

what 3 lots of 2 tens is → split 23 into 20 and 3 → multiply both parts, then add → what happens when the ones answer is bigger than ten → catch someone who only multiplied one part

## Teacher orientation

Teacher orientation: Children learn to multiply a two-digit number by a one-digit number by partitioning it into tens and ones, multiplying each part, and adding the two answers. They work in books: a starter, two short practice sets and a final question where they judge somebody else's working. The tricky move is that both parts have to be multiplied. A child who has just split 26 into 20 and 6 very often multiplies the 20 and then adds the 6 on, and that is met head-on in the second My Turn, on 26 × 3. Have the board ready to write on, because every My Turn and Our Turn carries a blank part-whole model the class watches you fill.

---

## Slide 1. Tens, ones and times tables (starter)

**On the board.** Date and LO header, then four questions:

> Partition 46 into tens and ones.
>
> Partition 70 into tens and ones.
>
> 8 × 7 =
>
> 5 × 9 =

**The thought.** How many tens and how many ones are in this number, and what is that ten actually worth?

**What happens.** Children answer all four in books. The brief says partitioning and the 2, 3, 4, 5 and 8 facts are secure, so this is not a gift starter: 70 has no ones at all, which is the item that catches children who read the digits rather than the value, and 8 × 7 and 5 × 9 sit at the far end of the tables they hold rather than the near end.

**What you land.** 40 and 6. 70 and 0. 56. 45. The check slide follows.

**Why it is here.** Both halves of today's method come out of this: the split itself, and the facts each part gets multiplied by. The first model uses both within two minutes.

## Slide 2. Tens, ones and times tables - check

**On the board.** The four answers.

## Slide 3. What is 3 lots of 20? (prepare, short explanation)

**On the board.**

> 20 is 2 tens.
>
> 3 lots of 2 tens is 6 tens.
>
> 6 tens is 60, so 3 × 20 = 60.

Plus the star fact: *3 lots of 2 tens is 6 tens, so 3 × 20 = 60.*

**The thought.** If 3 lots of 2 is 6, what is 3 lots of 2 tens?

**What happens.** You say it and they try one in their heads (4 × 30). This is the one thing the method needs that the brief does not say they have. Every partitioned multiplication in the lesson starts by multiplying a whole ten, and a child who thinks 3 × 20 is 6 is stuck on the first line of every question.

**What you land.** 3 × 20 is 60, because it is 6 tens. Listen for anyone saying 6: they have multiplied the digits and dropped the ten.

**Why it is here.** It makes the tens half of every example workable, which the My Turn on 23 × 3 needs on its very first line.

## Slide 4. Key vocabulary: partition and product

**On the board.** Two cards. *Partition*: when you partition a number, you split it into parts that add back together, so 46 partitions into 40 and 6. *Product*: a product is the answer you get when you multiply, so the product of 20 and 3 is 60.

**The thought.** Beside the spine. This is a reference the class glances back at.

**What happens.** You read both cards. Partition is the word the objective itself uses and the word step 1 of the criteria is written in, so it is taught rather than assumed. Product is the word that lets a child say what they did: "I multiplied both parts and added the two products."

**Why it is here.** Beside the spine, deliberately: the words are needed before the first instruction, and neither of them answers a question a later beat is about to ask.

## Slide 5. My Turn - 23 × 3

**On the board.** The question, `23 × 3 =`, a blank part-whole model with 23 in the whole and both parts empty, and the success criteria:

> 1. Partition the two-digit number into tens and ones.
> 2. Multiply the tens by the other number.
> 3. Multiply the ones by the other number as well.
> 4. Add your two answers together.

**The thought.** None. You are working and they are watching.

**What happens.** You fill the model live: 20 in one part, 3 in the other, 60 written beside the 20, 9 written beside the 3, then 60 + 9 = 69. The script names the wrong move out loud at the point where it is tempting: "some of you will want to write 60 and then just put the 3 on the end, because the 3 is sitting right there. But the 3 didn't stay as 3. I multiplied it, and it turned into 9."

**What you land.** 23 × 3 = 69. Both parts get multiplied.

**Why it is here.** They have now seen the whole method run once on a clean example, which is what the guided attempt on 34 × 2 repeats with them.

## Slide 6. Our Turn - 34 × 2

**On the board.** `34 × 2 =`, a fresh blank part-whole model, the same four criteria.

**The thought.** Which part of 34 do I multiply first, and what is that part actually worth?

**What happens.** They do the thinking and you write. The guiding questions are spoken one at a time and live in the notes, not on the board: how do we partition 34, which part first, say 30 × 2 in tens, what is 4 × 2, what do we do with the 8. If anyone offers 64, they have multiplied the 30 and then added the 4 without multiplying it, which is the lesson's central error appearing for the second time.

**What you land.** 34 × 2 = 68.

**Why it is here.** They have now run the method once with help, which is what makes the two questions in the Your Turn attemptable alone.

## Slide 7. Your Turn - partition and multiply

**On the board.**

> Partition each number, then multiply.
>
> 21 × 4 =
>
> 32 × 3 =

A blank part-whole model beside each, and the same criteria.

**The thought.** What is the tens part actually worth, and have I multiplied both parts before I add?

**What happens.** Two questions, not more: this is the quick check on whether the class can do it yet, not the main practice. Both have a small ones digit so the two answers add without crossing a ten, and nothing new is being asked. What changes between them is the tens digit and the multiplier, so the child runs the whole method twice rather than reusing one fact.

**What you look for.** Both parts multiplied, not just the tens. 80 and 4 written separately before they are added.

**Why it is here.** They can now run the method themselves on clean numbers, which is exactly what the next model changes, by making the ones answer bigger than ten.

## Slide 8. Your Turn - partition and multiply - check

**On the board.** 21 × 4 = 84, from 80 and 4. 32 × 3 = 96, from 90 and 6.

## Slide 9. My Turn - 26 × 3

**On the board.** `26 × 3 =`, a blank part-whole model, the same criteria, the star fact.

**The thought.** None. You are working and they are watching.

**What happens.** Same method, one thing changed: 6 × 3 is 18, so the ones answer is bigger than ten. The script says the wrong answer out loud before it resolves it: "if I forget to multiply and I just add the 6 on, I get 66, and 66 is wrong. My 6 became 18." Then 60 + 18 = 78, said in words as well as written.

**What you land.** 26 × 3 = 78. A ones answer bigger than ten does not break anything.

**Why it is here.** They have seen what happens when the ones answer goes past ten, which the mixed set needs because half of its questions do it.

## Slide 10. Our Turn - 24 × 4

**On the board.** `24 × 4 =`, a fresh blank model, the same criteria.

**The thought.** The ones answer is bigger than ten. Does that change what I do with it?

**What happens.** One guided example, short, because only the size of the ones answer is new. You ask for the partition, then the tens said in tens, then 4 × 4, then what gets added to 80. "Not 4. Tell your partner why."

**What you land.** 24 × 4 = 96.

**Why it is here.** They have handled a big ones answer with help, so the mixed set can put the two cases next to each other.

## Slide 11. Your Turn - all four

**On the board.**

> Partition each number, then multiply.
>
> Draw your own part-whole model for each one.
>
> 43 × 2 =
>
> 27 × 3 =
>
> 52 × 4 =
>
> 36 × 5 =

The criteria stay. No part-whole models are printed here: children draw their own, because by now the model is part of the method rather than something to read.

**The thought.** Is my ones answer big enough to change what I add, and have I kept my tens answer as tens?

**What happens.** This is the main practice and the mixed set. The two cases are shuffled rather than blocked, because each cycle's Your Turn was the block: 43 × 2 and 52 × 4 keep the ones answer small, 27 × 3 and 36 × 5 push it past ten, and the last two take the answer over 100 so the method is used at the size the objective names. No two questions share a fact.

**What you look for.** Both parts multiplied every time. The tens answer written as 80 or 200, not 8 or 2.

**Why it is here.** They can now run the method unaided on both cases, which is what the final question asks them to judge in someone else's working.

## Slide 12. Your Turn - all four - check

**On the board.** 86 from 80 and 6. 81 from 60 and 21. 208 from 200 and 8. 180 from 150 and 30.

## Slide 13. Is Chloe right? (Apply)

**On the board.**

> Chloe worked out 34 × 3. She wrote:
>
> 30 × 3 = 90
>
> 90 + 4 = 94
>
> Is Chloe right? Explain your answer.
>
> What happened to Chloe's 4?

**The thought.** Chloe's two lines both look right on their own. Which part of 34 never got multiplied?

**What happens.** They are not calculating now, they are checking. Chloe's first line is genuinely correct, so a child cannot answer by glancing at the shape of the slide: they have to take her second line apart. The second question is the way in for anyone stuck, and the script offers it rather than the answer.

**What you land.** No. He partitioned 34 into 30 and 4 and multiplied the 30, but he never multiplied the 4, he just added it on. 4 × 3 is 12, so it should be 90 + 12 = 102.

**Why it is here.** Final performance. It is the one retest of the lesson's central error, and it asks for the method to be used backwards, on work the child did not produce.

## Slide 14. Is Chloe right? - check

**On the board.** The model answer, in a child's words.

## The read-back

By the end, children will multiply a two-digit number by a one-digit number by partitioning it, because the lesson replaces the wrong rule that only the tens part gets multiplied with a method that multiplies both parts and then adds them, evidenced by four unaided mixed calculations and a judgement of a worked example whose first line is correct.

---

## The decisions the walk-through does not show

**The learning.** Children can split any two-digit number into its tens and its ones, multiply each part by the one-digit number, and add the two answers, and they know that the part they split off still has to be multiplied. The kind of learning is a **method**, so one well-practised procedure is the lesson rather than a set of instances.

**The objective and its boundary.** The teacher's objective is used verbatim on the board, method tail included, because partitioning is the learning and not just the route to it. Today's boundary stops at two-digit by one-digit with the tables the class holds; the formal written column method, and multiplying by a two-digit number, are deliberately left for later lessons.

**Prior knowledge and structure.** The brief gives partitioning and the 2, 3, 4, 5 and 8 facts as secure. It does not say children can multiply a whole ten, which the method's first step needs, so that is made visible in a short explanation beat rather than assumed or reteaching place value from scratch. Skill-based fits because the objective names a repeatable procedure the class must perform reliably, and the teacher asked for standard My Turn, Our Turn, Your Turn. The nearest alternative, content-based, would teach the idea of partitioning as knowledge and never secure the execution.

**Two cycles, one concept, one set of criteria.** The two cycles run the same procedure with the same four steps, so they are one concept rather than two. What changes in cycle two is that the ones answer goes past ten, which is the case the independent practice is full of and which must be modelled before it is met alone. It gets its own cycle rather than a second My Turn beside the first, so the clean case is guided and checked before the harder one arrives.

**The dominant sticking point.** After partitioning, multiply the tens and then simply add the ones digit on: 26 × 3 becomes 60 + 6 = 66. It is exposed in the first My Turn's script at the exact moment it tempts, made most visible in the second My Turn where 6 becomes 18, watched for in both Our Turns, and retested once at the end on Chloe's working. It is not re-run at every response moment; the mixed set asks for the method, not for the correction.

**The second misconception.** Reading 3 × 20 as 6, losing the ten. One clean touch: the explanation beat teaches it in tens, and a line of teacher information names what to listen for. It also comes back once on the worksheet's reasoning question so it is checked away from the board.

**Independent evidence.** The mixed Your Turn and the Apply. The mixed set cannot be passed by surface cue because the two cases are shuffled, no two questions share a fact, and the ones answers vary. The Apply cannot be passed by pattern because Chloe's first line is correct, so "no, she's wrong" is not readable off the format, and the explanation has to name the step she missed.

**Success criteria.** Four how-to steps, because the task is carrying out a procedure. They are marked to be built live and kept, since this method runs across the multiplication unit and the class will want it on the wall next lesson. Worded so a stuck child can run them: "Multiply the ones by the other number as well" carries the lesson's whole sticking point in its last two words.

**Representation.** One family: the part-whole model, with the whole filled and both parts blank for live completion. It is load-bearing during both cycles because it is where the child sees the two multiplications the method makes. It is deliberately dropped for the mixed Your Turn, where drawing it is now part of the practice, and the criteria stay visible. This is a support decision, not an oversight.

**Reasoning and problem solving.** The reasoning shape is inspect-an-attempt, and it sits in the Apply: judging a worked example whose first line is correct. Problem solving is not in the deck, because two full cycles plus the judging task fill the forty-five minutes honestly; the word problem lives on the worksheet.

**Worksheet.** Generated, one per child, separate from the slide practice and using none of its numbers. Fluency first, in the board's form: the first two questions keep a blank part-whole model, the last two are bare calculations. Then one reasoning question (Nadia says 40 × 5 is 20) and one word problem. Priced against the page at roughly 230mm, so it fits with the models protected.

**A light line.** Nothing here. The material is four calculations and a child who forgot to multiply, and the one available joke would land on Chloe, who is standing in for half the class.

**Deliberately omitted.** No real-world hook, because the teacher asked for nothing fancy and a shopping context bolted onto 23 × 3 would be scenery rather than maths. No third cycle and no separate problem-solving beat: the time is not there, and thinning the two cycles to make room would take the practice away from the case that actually fails.

**flagsForTeacher.** One. The brief does not say whether the class can already multiply a whole ten, and the lesson teaches it briefly before the first model.
