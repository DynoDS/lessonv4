# Year 4 Maths: To multiply a two-digit number by a one-digit number using partitioning

## The journey

40 × 3 is 4 tens three times → 24 × 3 is 3 lots of 20 AND 3 lots of 4 → the four written lines, guided, then on our own → who multiplied both parts of 27?

## Teacher orientation

Teacher orientation: A standard My Turn, Our Turn, Your Turn lesson in two cycles. The first, short cycle multiplies a multiple of ten (40 × 3) using place-value counters on the board, so children can say "4 tens × 3 = 12 tens = 120". The second cycle is the objective: 24 × 3 by partitioning, with three rows of 24 shown as counters so the class can see that three lots of 24 is three lots of 20 and three lots of 4. Children then write the method out in four lines (partition, tens, ones, add) and finish by judging two children's working for 27 × 3, one of whom multiplied only the ones. The tricky move is that first step of the method, multiplying the tens: children who see 20 × 3 and write 6, or who add the 20 back on without multiplying it, are the ones to watch, and the first cycle plus the counters in the second My Turn are where it is taught. Nothing to prepare: children work in books from the board, and the optional worksheet takes the same shape as the Your Turn.

## Slide by slide

### Slide 1 - Starter: Tables and tens

**On the board:** LO header, blank Date. Five numbered questions:
(1) 4 × 8 =
(2) 3 × 7 =
(3) 8 × ___ = 40
(4) Partition 47 into tens and ones.
(5) 60 is how many tens?

**The thought:** Which times-table fact is this, and how many tens is 60?

**What happens:** "Five quick ones to warm up your tables and your partitioning, because we're using both of them today." Children answer all five; the check slide follows.

**What you look for:** All five should come back fast. Question 5 is the one the lesson leans on: a child who cannot say that 60 is 6 tens will stall on the tens step of the method, so it is worth hearing that one aloud.

**Why it is here:** The facts in the 2, 3, 4, 5 and 8 tables and the tens-and-ones partition are the two things every multiplication today runs on. Question 5 is the piece of place value the first cycle picks up straight away.

### Slide 2 - Starter - check

The five answers: 32, 21, 5, 40 and 7, 6 tens.

### Slide 3 - My Turn: 40 × 3

**On the board:** `40 × 3 =`. A place-value chart (H, T, O) with three rows, each showing 40 as four ten-counters, and a blank fourth row. The success criteria for this cycle:
1. Say the number as tens: 40 is 4 tens.
2. Multiply that many tens: 4 tens × 3 = 12 tens.
3. Write the tens as a number: 12 tens = 120.

**The thought:** none; the class watches.

**What happens:** "Before we multiply a number like 24, we need to be sure about the tens on their own. 40 × 3. Look at the chart: there's 40, and again, and again, three lots of 40. 40 is 4 tens, so each row has four ten-counters. I'm not counting them in ones. I know 4 × 3 = 12, so 4 tens × 3 is 12 tens. And 12 tens as a number? Ten tens make a hundred, and there are two more tens: 120." The teacher completes the blank row with 120 (one hundred-counter and two ten-counters, or the digits). Then the trap, named out loud: "This catches a lot of people out. They see 4 × 3 = 12 and write 12. We weren't multiplying 4, we were multiplying 4 tens, so the answer is 12 tens. That zero on the 40 has to come with us."

**What you land:** 40 × 3 is 4 tens × 3, which is 12 tens, which is 120.

**Why it is here:** The partition method's first line is always a multiple of ten times a one-digit number, and the brief does not say children can do that yet. Taught here on its own, it is a move they own before it becomes step 2 of the real method on slide 8.

### Slide 4 - Our Turn: multiply the tens

**On the board:** Two questions, each with its own chart (H, T, O, rows of ten-counters, blank answer row) and the same success criteria:
(a) 30 × 2 =
(b) 40 × 5 =

**The thought:** How many tens am I multiplying, and what number is that many tens?

**What happens:** The teacher works each with the class, one question at a time: "How many tens is 30? So what am I multiplying? 3 tens × 2, and 3 × 2 is? So 6 tens, which is?" Then (b): "4 tens × 5, what fact do I need? 4 × 5 = 20, so it's 20 tens. Careful: what is 20 tens as a number? Ten tens is a hundred, so twenty tens is 200. Not 20. Twenty tens." Every child says or writes the answer before it is confirmed.

**What you look for:** 60 and 200. A child who says 20 for (b) has multiplied the digits and dropped the tens; send them back to the chart to count the ten-counters.

**Why it is here:** (b) is the case where the tens product reaches the hundreds, which 26 × 5 on slide 9 and 34 × 5 on slide 10 both need.

### Slide 5 - Your Turn: multiply the tens

**On the board:** Two questions, the success criteria, no chart:
(1) 20 × 4 =
(2) 30 × 8 =

**The thought:** How many tens is 30, and what is 24 tens as a number?

**What happens:** "Two on your own. Say the number as tens first, every time." Children write both; the check slide follows.

**What you look for:** 80 and 240. Watch for 20 × 4 written as 8, and 30 × 8 written as 24 or 2,400.

**Why it is here:** It is the quick check that the class can multiply a multiple of ten before it is buried inside a longer method. Two questions, because this is a bridging cycle.

### Slide 6 - Your Turn: multiply the tens - check

80 and 240.

### Slide 7 - Key vocabulary: partition, product

**On the board:** Two cards. *partition*: To partition a number is to split it into its parts, so 24 is 20 and 4. (visual: 24 = 20 + 4). *product*: The product is the answer you get when you multiply two numbers. (visual: 4 × 8 = 32).

**What happens:** "Two words for the rest of the lesson. Partition, you know: we've partitioned to add all year, and today we partition to multiply. Product is the answer when you multiply, so 32 is the product of 4 and 8. Every question today gives us two products to add."

**Why it is here:** Beside the spine. Both words are used on the very next slide and in the success criteria that follow.

### Slide 8 - My Turn: 24 × 3

**On the board:** `24 × 3 =`. A place-value chart (H, T, O) with three rows, each showing 24 as two ten-counters and four one-counters, so the tens column holds six ten-counters and the ones column twelve one-counters. Room for the four lines of working. The success criteria for this cycle:
1. Partition the number into tens and ones.
2. Multiply the tens, counting in tens: 2 tens × 3 = 6 tens = 60.
3. Multiply the ones.
4. Add the two products.
Sticky fact: 24 × 3 is 3 lots of 24, so it is 3 lots of 20 and 3 lots of 4.

**The thought:** none; the class watches.

**What happens:** "24 × 3 means 3 lots of 24, and there they are: 24, 24, 24. I don't have a 24 times table, so I partition it: 24 is 20 and 4." Teacher writes `24 = 20 + 4`. "Look at the chart. Every row has two ten-counters and four one-counters, so three lots of 24 is three lots of 20, and three lots of 4. Both parts, three times. Tens first: 20 × 3 is 2 tens × 3 = 6 tens = 60." Writes `20 × 3 = 60`. "Count the ten-counters: six. Now the ones: 4 × 3 = 12." Writes `4 × 3 = 12`. "Twelve one-counters in that column, and 12 is just 12. Last step, add the two products: 60 + 12 = 72." Writes `60 + 12 = 72`. Then the mistake, named: "Some people do 4 × 3 = 12, then put the 20 back on: 20 + 12 = 32. Look at the chart. Is there one 20 in there, or three? Three. So the 20 gets multiplied by 3 too."

**What you land:** The sticky fact, and the four lines: partition, tens, ones, add.

**Why it is here:** The chart is the one place children can see why both parts get multiplied. The guided frame on slide 9 and the judgement on slide 11 both rest on that sight.

### Slide 9 - Our Turn: partition and multiply

**On the board:** Two questions, one at a time, each in a blank method frame with four labelled lines (Partition: `___ = ___ + ___`; Tens: `___ × ___ = ___`; Ones: `___ × ___ = ___`; Add: `___ + ___ = ___`), the same success criteria and the sticky fact:
(a) 23 × 4 =
(b) 26 × 5 =

**The thought:** What goes on each line of the frame, and does the 20 get multiplied too?

**What happens:** The teacher fills the frame line by line from the class's answers. (a): "Partition 23 for me. Tens first, 20 × 4: think in tens, 2 tens × 4 is 8 tens, which is 80. Ones: 3 × 4? Add the products: 80 + 12? Do I have to multiply the 20 as well as the 3? Why?" (b): "26 × 5. Partition it. Tens: 2 tens × 5 = 10 tens. What number is 10 tens? Ones: 6 × 5 = 30. Add: 100 + 30. Some of you wanted to write 100 and 30 side by side and get 10030. It's an add, not a squash together."

**What you look for:** 92 and 130. The answer to "why does the 20 get multiplied" should be "because it's four lots of 23", not a repeat of the step.

**Why it is here:** The frame shows the class how the four lines are set out, and (b) is the first time the tens product reaches 100 inside the full method, which questions (4) and (5) of the Your Turn need.

### Slide 10 - Your Turn: partition and multiply

**On the board:** Five questions, the success criteria, the sticky fact, no frame:
(1) 32 × 3 =
(2) 21 × 4 =
(3) 47 × 2 =
(4) 34 × 5 =
(5) 13 × 8 =

**The thought:** Have I multiplied both parts, and what is the tens product as a number?

**What happens:** "Five on your own. Set your working out the way the frame did: partition, tens, ones, add. The last two have a tens product that goes past 100, so say it in tens." Children write the working and the answer for each; the check slide follows.

**What you look for:** 96, 84, 94, 170, 104. Only the ones multiplied (32 × 3 written as 36); the two products written side by side (9006); a tens product missing its zero (90 written as 9).

**Why it is here:** This is the objective performed unaided, and it is what the Apply asks them to judge in someone else's working.

### Slide 11 - Your Turn: partition and multiply - check

96, 84, 94, 170, 104, each with its two products shown.

### Slide 12 - Apply: Two answers for 27 × 3

**On the board:** `27 × 3 =`, then two speech bubbles. [Name 1] says: "27 is 20 and 7. 20 × 3 = 60. 7 × 3 = 21. 60 + 21 = 81. So 27 × 3 = 81." [Name 2] says: "27 is 20 and 7. 7 × 3 = 21. 20 + 21 = 41. So 27 × 3 = 41." Then: Who is right? Explain what the other person did wrong.

**The thought:** Did each of them multiply both parts of 27, or only one?

**What happens:** "They both worked out 27 × 3 and got different answers. Read both. Decide who is right, and write one sentence saying what the other person did wrong. If you're stuck, ask yourself how many lots of 27 we've got, and so how many lots of 20." Each child decides and writes their sentence; the model answer follows.

**What you land:** [Name 1] is right: 81. [Name 2] only multiplied the 7; it is three lots of 27, so the 20 has to be multiplied by 3 as well, giving 60, not 20.

**Why it is here:** The final beat, nothing after it. It turns the lesson's own sticking point round: children have executed the method, now they have to see it missing in working that looks complete.

## Read-back

By the end, children will multiply a two-digit number by a one-digit number by partitioning it, multiplying the tens and the ones separately and adding the products, because the lesson gives them the tens step as a move of its own (4 tens × 3 = 12 tens) and shows them on counters that three lots of 24 is three lots of 20 and three lots of 4, which replaces the wrong rule that only the ones get multiplied, evidenced by five independent calculations on slide 10 and the judgement of two pieces of working on slide 12.

Read from the other end: a child who only multiplied the ones gets 36, 24, 54, 40 and 34 on slide 10 and picks 41 on slide 12, so the check catches exactly what the clause after "because" names.

## Decisions the walk-through does not show

- **The learning.** A method: partition, multiply the tens, multiply the ones, add the products. Underneath it one fact children could not have said before: three lots of 24 is three lots of 20 and three lots of 4, so both parts are multiplied. The counters chart is what makes that fact visible.
- **Objective and boundary.** The teacher's objective is used verbatim and displayed cut at "using partitioning", the method tail, so the board reads "To multiply a two-digit number by a one-digit number". Multipliers stay in the 2, 3, 4, 5 and 8 tables the brief names. The formal column layout and three-digit numbers are later learning and stay out.
- **Prior knowledge and structure.** Tables and partitioning are the visible foundation (the starter). Multiplying a multiple of ten is not listed as prior, so it is assumed new and taught as a short first cycle rather than folded into the main My Turn, where it would be a second new idea inside one teach block. Skill-based, as the teacher asked, and as a repeatable method needs; two cycles rather than one because the tens step is a distinct move the second cycle's success criteria wrap around.
- **Dominant sticking point.** Multiplying only the ones and adding the tens back unchanged (24 × 3 = 20 + 12 = 32), carried over from a year of partitioning to add. Exposed on slide 8 against the counters, checked by the "why" question on slide 9, retested once on slide 12. Two secondary errors get one touch each: the tens product losing its zero (20 × 3 = 6) is pre-empted by the first cycle and watched for on slide 5; the two products written side by side (60 and 12 as 612) is named on slide 9 and watched for on slide 10.
- **Independent evidence.** Slide 10's five questions cannot be passed by pattern: the multipliers and the tens vary, three of five have a two-digit ones product and two cross 100. Slide 12 cannot be passed by the format, because the two bubbles are the same shape and the only difference is the missing line.
- **Success criteria and worksheet evidence.** Steps for both concepts. The second cycle's steps carry the tens cue from the first (step 2), and are marked to draw live and keep, because the same method carries into three-digit numbers and the formal layout. The worksheet is a fresh question set in the same shape: six fluency questions, the first two in the method frame the class met on slide 9 and the rest set out by the child, then one spot-the-mistake targeting the side-by-side products error, then one word problem.
- **Load-bearing representation.** The place-value counters chart on slides 3, 4 and 8: three rows of the same number, counters aligned in columns, so the class can count six ten-counters and twelve one-counters. It leaves after slide 8 because by 26 × 5 it would hold 30 ones and the point has moved to the written lines. The method frame on slide 9 is support, not load-bearing.
- **Reasoning and problem solving.** Reasoning is slide 12, compare two pieces of working, which is also the misconception retest. Problem solving is the worksheet's last question; it does not fit the 45 minutes on the board alongside two cycles.
- **Deliberate omissions.** No real-world hook: nothing true lives inside 24 × 3 and a made-up one would be scenery. No estimate-first beat: it is worth doing, and it would take the time the first cycle needs; the model answer on slide 12 says 41 is far too small so the size check is still heard once. The counters are not on the Our Turn or Your Turn, on purpose.
- **Light line.** None: the material is bare numbers, and a joke at the wrong answer would land on the child holding it.
- **Flags for the teacher.** None.
