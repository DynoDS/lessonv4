# Year 4 Maths: To multiply a two-digit number by a one-digit number using partitioning

**Structure:** Skill-based (My Turn, Our Turn, Your Turn), one concept, 45 minutes.

## The journey, in one line

tables and tens-and-ones we already have → 20 × 3 is six tens, so 60 → partition, multiply each part, add the two answers → the tens answer is tens, not a digit → any story with a multiplication hiding in it

## Teacher orientation

Teacher orientation: Children multiply a two-digit number by a one-digit number by partitioning it into tens and ones, multiplying each part and adding the two answers. The starter warms up the table facts and the partitioning they already have, then one short board explanation with base-ten rods shows why 20 × 3 is six tens. You model 23 × 3 and then 28 × 3 on a part-whole model with the working written beside it, guide the class through 32 × 4 and 47 × 5, and they do five on their own in books. They then judge a wrong claim (34 × 4 = 1216) and finish on two word problems. The tricky move is the tens part: 30 × 4 is 3 tens × 4 = 12 tens, which is 120, and the two slips to watch are writing 12 instead of 120, and writing the two digit answers side by side (3 × 4 = 12, 4 × 4 = 16, so "1216"). The tens step is taught on the slide straight after the vocabulary card, and the side-by-side slip is named out loud in your second modelled example.

## The lesson, slide by slide

### 1. Starter: Tables and tens and ones

- **Heading:** Tables and tens and ones
- **On the board:** Date and LO in the header. Five numbered questions:
  4 × 7 =
  8 × 6 =
  3 × 9 =
  Partition 64 into tens and ones.
  Partition 38 into tens and ones.
- **The thought:** Which table fact is this, and which digit in this number is the tens?
- **What happens:** "Five quick ones to wake your brains up. Three are table facts, two are partitioning." Children answer all five, then the check slide shows 28, 48, 27, 60 and 4, 30 and 8.
- **What you look for:** 64 partitioned as "6 and 4" rather than 60 and 4. That child will drop the zero on the tens step later, so it is worth catching here.
- **Why it is here:** Every question in the lesson runs on exactly these two things, a table fact and a tens-and-ones split. The My Turn picks them up by name ("it starts with something you can already do").

### 2. Vocabulary: partition

- **Heading:** partition
- **On the board:** One card. *partition*: Split a number into its tens and ones, so 23 becomes 20 and 3. Beside it, a small completed part-whole model, 23 at the top with 20 and 3 underneath.
- **The thought:** none needed, this is a glance reference for a word the class already owns.
- **What happens:** "You've already used this word this morning. When you split 64 into 60 and 4, that's partitioning." Point at the picture: 23 at the top, the two parts underneath. "Every question today starts with one, because it turns one big multiplication into two small ones you know."
- **Why it is here:** The objective names the word, so the class has to be able to read the LO they copy down. It sits beside the spine, placed here because the starter has just had them do the thing it names.

### 3. Explanation: Multiplying tens

- **Heading:** Multiplying tens
- **On the board:** 20 × 3 written large. Base-ten rods: three groups of two ten-rods, no ones cubes. Four lines of route beside them:
  20 is 2 tens. So 20 × 3 is 2 tens × 3.
  2 × 3 = 6, so 2 tens × 3 = 6 tens.
  6 tens is 60. So 20 × 3 = 60.
  Star fact: 30 × 4 = 120, because 3 tens × 4 = 12 tens.
- **The thought:** Why does 2 × 3 tell me 20 × 3?
- **What happens:** "Look at these rods. Each one is a ten. Here's twenty, two tens, and I've got three lots of it. Count the rods with me: two, four, six. Six rods, every rod a ten, so 6 tens, which is 60." Then the quick check in heads: 30 × 4. "Three tens times four: 3 × 4 is 12, so 12 tens. And 12 tens is 120. Not 12." That sentence is the star fact and it stays on the board for the rest of the lesson.
- **What you land:** 20 × 3 = 60, and the answer to a tens multiplication is a number of tens.
- **Why it is here:** The tens step of every example needs it. The prior learning says children multiply by 2, 3, 4, 5 and 8, not multiples of ten by those numbers, so this is the one piece of new ground the method rests on, made visible on the smallest possible case before it appears inside a bigger one. The My Turn's second line ("20 × 3, that's 2 tens × 3, which is 6 tens") is this slide said again.

### 4. My Turn: 23 × 3, then 28 × 3

- **Heading:** My Turn
- **On the board:** Two examples, one under the other, each written as its own question:
  23 × 3 =
  28 × 3 =
  Beside each, a part-whole model with the whole filled in (23, then 28) and both parts blank, and room next to it for three lines of working. The success criteria in their panel:
  1. First, partition the number into tens and ones.
  2. Multiply the tens part.
  3. Multiply the ones part.
  4. Add the two answers together.
  The star fact from slide 3.
- **The thought:** none (children watch).
- **What happens:** You work 23 × 3 live: fill the parts (20 and 3), write 20 × 3 = 60, then 3 × 3 = 9, then 60 + 9 = 69. Then 28 × 3, with only the ones digit changed: 20 and 8, 20 × 3 = 60 again, 8 × 3 = 24, and here you stop. "Some people see the 6 and the 24 and write 624. That 6 isn't a six, it's 60. I'm not sticking the answers next to each other, I'm adding them. 60 + 24 = 84." The two examples hold the multiplier and the tens still, so the only thing that changes is the ones product becoming two digits, which is exactly where the side-by-side mistake first shows.
- **What you land:** Partition, times the tens, times the ones, add. And the tens answer is added, never written beside the ones answer.
- **Why it is here:** This is the method being seen in full, twice. The Our Turn then asks the class to tell you each step in order on numbers whose tens product goes past 100.

### 5. Our Turn: 32 × 4 and 47 × 5

- **Heading:** Our Turn
- **On the board:** Two lettered examples:
  32 × 4 =
  47 × 5 =
  Each with its own part-whole model (whole filled, parts blank) and room for working. The same four success criteria. The star fact.
- **The thought:** Which part am I multiplying now, and is the answer in tens or in ones?
- **What happens:** The class tells you what to do, one step at a time, and you write it. Guiding questions, in order and spoken, not printed: What's the first step? What's 32 partitioned? Which part first? What's 30 × 4, thinking in tens? So what do I write, 12 or 120? What's next? And now? Then 47 × 5: 40 and 7; 40 × 5 is 4 tens × 5 = 20 tens = 200; 7 × 5 = 35; 200 + 35 = 235. Before release: "Tell your partner the four steps without looking."
- **What you look for:** 30 × 4 written as 12. The two answers added rather than placed side by side.
- **Why it is here:** Both examples cross 100 on the tens step on purpose, because that is where 12 tens gets written as 12 and it is the one case the My Turn did not show. After this, every case in the Your Turn has been worked in front of the class.

### 6. Your Turn: five on your own

- **Heading:** Your Turn
- **On the board:** Work these out using partitioning. Show your working.
  21 × 4 =
  27 × 3 =
  43 × 4 =
  36 × 5 =
  48 × 8 =
  The four success criteria. The star fact.
- **The thought:** What are the two smaller multiplications hiding inside this one, and have I added both answers?
- **What happens:** Children work in books, drawing their own part-whole for each one and writing the two multiplications and the add. The check slide follows: 84, 81, 172, 180, 384.
- **What you look for:** 43 × 4 coming out as 1612 (side by side) instead of 172; 36 × 5 with the tens answer written as 150 in full, not 15.
- **Why it is here:** It is the lesson's evidence that each child can run the method alone. The set climbs through the cases: clean, ones product two digits, tens product over 100, both together with a zero in the sum, then the 8 table. The two beats after it both lean on children being able to execute the method without help.

### 7. Is Ava right?

- **Heading:** Is Ava right?
- **On the board:** A speech bubble: Ava says: "34 × 4 = 1216, because 3 × 4 = 12 and 4 × 4 = 16." Then the question: Is Ava right? Explain your answer. The star fact is deliberately not on this slide.
- **The thought:** What is the 3 in 34 actually worth, and what does that make the first multiplication?
- **What happens:** "Thirty-four lots of four, and the answer's over a thousand?" Children decide on their own first, then tell a partner, then write one sentence saying what has actually happened. Way in for a stuck child: partition 34 and do it properly, then compare. The model answer on the check slide: No. The 3 in 34 is 3 tens, so it is 30 × 4 = 120, not 12. 120 + 16 = 136.
- **What you look for:** An explanation that names the 3 as 30 or 3 tens, not just a corrected number.
- **Why it is here:** This is the one retest of the lesson's wrong rule, on a number the class has not seen, with the reference that would hand over 30 × 4 taken off the board. It sits beside the spine: nothing after it needs it, and that is the point of a retest.

### 8. Apply: Which multiplication is hiding here?

- **Heading:** Which multiplication is hiding here?
- **On the board:** Two word problems:
  A box holds 24 pencils. How many pencils are in 4 boxes?
  A school hall has 8 rows of chairs, with 27 chairs in each row. How many chairs are there?
  Instruction: Write the multiplication, then work it out using partitioning. The four success criteria.
- **The thought:** Which multiplication is this story asking for, and what are its two parts?
- **What happens:** "Nobody's told you which calculation to do this time. Read it, decide, write the multiplication down, then partition." Check slide: 24 × 4 = 96; 27 × 8 = 216.
- **What you look for:** The calculation written before any working; 8 rows of 27 turned into 27 × 8.
- **Why it is here:** The Your Turn handed children the calculation. Here they have to find it in a story first, which is the change in thinking that earns the ending, and both problems are then worked with the method they have just practised.

## Read-back

By the end, children will multiply a two-digit number by a one-digit number because the lesson gives them the move of partitioning it into tens and ones, multiplying each part with the tens answer said in tens, and adding the two answers, and replaces the wrong rule that the two digit answers can be written side by side, evidenced by five independent calculations in books and a judged claim (34 × 4 = 1216) on a number the class has not met.

Read from the other end: the judged claim cannot be settled without knowing that the 3 is 30 and that 30 × 4 = 120 is added to 16, and the five calculations include two whose tens product passes 100 and two whose ones product is two digits, so a child holding the side-by-side rule or dropping the zero gets visibly wrong answers.

## The decisions the walk-through does not show

- **The learning:** A method. Children will be able to turn one two-digit-by-one-digit multiplication into two multiplications they already know and add the results, holding the idea that the tens digit stands for tens so its product is a number of tens. They could not do that at the start: the prior learning is single-digit table facts and partitioning, not multiplying a multiple of ten or combining the two products.
- **Objective and end performance:** The teacher's objective, verbatim, displayed as "To multiply a two-digit number by a one-digit number", with the tail "using partitioning" cut as a method after "using". End performance: five bare multiplications (multipliers 2, 3, 4, 5, 8 only, the fluent tables), one claim judged, two one-step word problems. Deferred: three-digit numbers, the formal column layout, multipliers whose tables are not yet fluent (6, 7, 9).
- **Prior knowledge and structure:** Table facts and partitioning are stated as fluent and are used, not retaught; the starter retrieves them. Multiplying a multiple of ten by a one-digit number is not stated as taught, so it is assumed new and taught explicitly on the smallest case (slide 3) before it appears inside the method. Skill-based over content-based because the objective is a repeatable procedure children must run reliably; one cycle rather than two because the cases (ones product two digits, tens product over 100) share one decision rule and are variations of the same four steps, so they are modelled inside one My Turn and one Our Turn rather than as separate moves. The teacher's own note asks for the standard My Turn, Our Turn, Your Turn shape.
- **Dominant sticking point:** The tens digit treated as a digit rather than a number of tens. It shows two ways: dropping the zero (30 × 4 = 12) and writing the digit products side by side (28 × 3 = 624). Exposed and taught on slide 3 (rods, star fact) and named out loud on the second My Turn example; watched in the Our Turn look-for; retested once at slide 7 on a fresh number with the star fact removed. The worksheet's reasoning item retests the dropped-zero form (40 × 3 = 12).
- **Independent evidence:** The Your Turn set of five cannot be passed by copying the board (fresh numbers, own part-whole) or by the side-by-side rule (it fails on 27 × 3, 43 × 4 and 36 × 5). The judged claim cannot be settled by remembering a sentence: 34 × 4 was never on the board and the star fact is off that slide.
- **Success criteria and worksheet evidence:** Four how-to steps, marked draw-live because the same four steps carry into three-digit numbers and the formal method in later lessons. The worksheet (Expected) gives four fluency questions, the first two in the board's own form (part-whole with the whole filled and parts blank, three working lines beside it) and the next two bare, then one dropped-zero claim to judge, then two one-step word problems, on fresh numbers that never reuse the deck's.
- **Load-bearing representation:** The part-whole model (whole filled, parts blank) with room for three lines of working, on My Turn, Our Turn and the first worksheet questions. Base-ten rods on slide 3 only, three groups of two ten-rods, so that "6 tens" is something the class can count rather than take on trust. No photographs: every visual is a tool the engine draws.
- **Reasoning and problem solving:** Reasoning is the practise beat at slide 7 (inspect an attempt, retesting the wrong rule); problem solving is the Apply at slide 8 (two one-step word problems where the child finds the calculation). The worksheet carries one of each.
- **Playful line:** Nothing in the material here beyond the wrong answer itself: one dry line in the slide 7 script (thirty-four fours, and it's over a thousand?), and that is all.
- **Deliberate omissions:** No second cycle for the over-100 case (same steps, so it is a guided variation, not a new move). No separate hinge slide; the Our Turn's "tell your partner the four steps" is the release check. No estimation beat; on these numbers the misconception is caught more cleanly by the judged claim than by estimating first. No printed stick-in piece: every part-whole is a figure a child rules by hand in two seconds.
