# Why the Vikings came to Britain - Year 4 History, 45 minutes

**LO: To explain why the Vikings came to Britain**

---

## The journey, in one line

what a Viking farm had to live on → why some families had no land at all → what Lindisfarne had that nobody was guarding → why later Vikings ploughed instead of sailing home → two reasons of your own, and which kind of Viking each one is about

---

## Teacher orientation

Teacher orientation: This lesson answers one question, why the Vikings came to Britain, and children finish it by writing two reasons of their own into a three-part frame. The three teaching slides build the answer in order: what Viking Scandinavia was short of, what Lindisfarne had that made it worth the journey, and why later Vikings stayed on English land instead of sailing home. The tricky move is that last one, telling a raid apart from settling, because children hear both of them as "the Vikings attacked us". It is taught on the third teaching slide and practised straight after on the card sort, so by the time they write, they have already sorted six examples.

---

## Slide 1 - Britain before the Vikings (starter)

**On the board.** The `Date` label and `LO: To explain why the Vikings came to Britain` in the header. Three numbered questions:

(1) Who was living in Britain when the Vikings first arrived?

(2) Put these three in order, earliest first: Anglo-Saxons, Romans, Vikings.

(3) The Vikings sailed from Scandinavia to Britain. What did they have to cross to get here?

**The thought.** Where do the Vikings sit against the people I already know about?

**What happens.** Children answer the three, then the check slide shows them: the Anglo-Saxons; Romans, Anglo-Saxons, Vikings; the sea.

**What you land.** The Vikings arrive after the Anglo-Saxons, and they had a sea to cross to get here.

**Why it is here.** Question 3 is the one the lesson uses: a sea crossing is a lot of trouble to go to, which is what makes the first Teach's question worth asking. Questions 1 and 2 put the period in order, because a Year 4 who can chant "Romans, Anglo-Saxons, Vikings" may not know which came first.

---

## Slide 2 - What was home like for a Viking family?

**On the board.** A photograph of a Norwegian fjord farm: mountains coming almost down to the water, and a thin green strip of flat land beside it. Beside it, the route in whole sentences:

> A family who farms needs flat land, good soil, and a summer long enough to grow food in.
>
> Look at this farm in Norway. The mountains come almost down to the water, and the flat land is a thin green strip beside the fjord.
>
> A farm like that could feed one family. When the sons grew up, there was no new land to give them.

Key question, in blue: *That family has sons. When they grow up, where do they put their own farms?*

Star fact: **There was very little good farmland in Viking Scandinavia, so many families had no land of their own.**

**The thought.** If there's no land left, what does a grown-up son do?

**What happens.** You walk the route, pointing at the strip of green and then at the mountain behind it. You ask the key question and take two or three answers; the honest ones are "there isn't anywhere" and "he'd have to go".

**What you land.** The star fact, said and left on the board.

**Why it is here.** Children can now see that a Viking family could genuinely run out of land at home. Without it, the next slide's question, why sail all that way, has no force, and the class answers it with "because they were fierce".

---

## Slide 3 - Could this family stay?

**On the board.** Four Viking farms, one line each, and two group headings.

> Not every Viking family was in the same trouble.
>
> Read each farm. Put it in one of these two groups:
>
> **This family had enough land** - **Someone in this family had to find land somewhere else**
>
> - Inga's family farmed the flat land beside a wide fjord, and there was still empty ground next to them.
> - Bjorn's father had one farm to split between his five sons.
> - Gunnar's farm was high up the mountainside. The summer there was too short for the grain to ripen.
> - Freya's family had a small field, and Freya was their only child.

Then: *Which one was hardest to decide? Tell your partner why you put it there.*

**The thought.** Is there enough good land here for everyone in this family to farm?

**What happens.** Every child commits to four placements. Inga and Freya stay; Bjorn and Gunnar go. Gunnar is the hard one, because the land is there and the summer isn't, and Freya is the other, because a small field is enough for one child.

**Look for.** Gunnar placed with "had enough land" because the farm is big; Freya moved to "had to leave" because her field is small.

**Why it is here.** Children can now say why a Viking family might have to go, which is what the next slide needs when it asks what was worth crossing the sea for.

---

## Slide 4 - Key vocabulary: monastery, raid

**On the board.** Two cards.

- **monastery** - A monastery was a place where monks lived together and prayed. *Beside it: the photograph of Lindisfarne.*
- **raid** - A raid is a fast attack on a place. You take what you want, and then you leave. *Beside it: the carved stone from Lindisfarne showing raiders with raised axes.*

**The thought.** null - you read the two cards and the class takes them in.

**What happens.** You read both. On `raid` you point at the carving: this was cut into stone by people who lived there.

**Why it is here.** Both words are in the first sentence of the next slide and in every question after it. Children can't follow the teaching without them. It sits beside the spine, not on it.

---

## Slide 5 - Why did the Vikings sail to Lindisfarne?

**On the board.** The photograph of Lindisfarne, a low island with the priory on it, seen from across the water; and a Viking longship. The route:

> In the year 793, Viking ships landed at Lindisfarne, a monastery on a small island off the coast of Britain.
>
> A Viking ship was shallow, so it could be rowed straight onto the beach. There was no harbour to stop it.
>
> Look at the island: no wall, no gate, no soldiers. Inside, the monks kept cups and crosses of silver and gold, because people had been giving gifts to that church for a hundred years.
>
> They hadn't sailed all that way looking for a fight. They came for what was inside.

Headline: **This is Holy Island, where the monastery stood.**

Key question, in blue: *Look at the island. What's protecting it?*

Star fact: **Monasteries like Lindisfarne kept silver and gold and had nobody guarding them, so they were easy to raid.**

**The thought.** What made this place worth sailing all that way for?

**What happens.** You set 793 and the island, then point at the ship and say what shallow means: you don't need a harbour, you run it up the sand. Then the two things together, the treasure and the empty beach. The last two sentences are where you meet the idea that they came for a fight and put it down.

**What you land.** The star fact.

**Why it is here.** Children now know what Britain had that Scandinavia did not, and that the choice was about silver rather than about violence. The next beat makes them use both halves, and the final explanation uses this as one of its two reasons.

---

## Slide 6 - Where would they go next?

**On the board.**

> In the years after 793, Viking crews chose where to raid next.
>
> Which one do you think they chose? Write A, B or C, and write one reason.
>
> **A** - A monastery on the coast, with silver crosses and no wall.
> **B** - A walled town with the king's soldiers inside.
> **C** - A farming village far up a river, with only sheep.

**The thought.** Which one has treasure they could actually get away with?

**What happens.** Every child writes a letter and a reason. B is the tempting wrong one, because a town has more in it. Then you tell them what actually happened: Jarrow in 794, Iona in 795, both monasteries.

**Look for.** B chosen because "a town has more treasure"; that child has the first half and not the second.

**Why it is here.** Children can now name what made a place worth raiding, rich and unguarded, which the sort at the end of the lesson has to tell apart from a reason to settle.

---

## Slide 7 - Key vocabulary: settle

**On the board.** One card.

- **settle** - When people settle somewhere, they come to live there for good. They build homes, farm the land and bring their families. *Beside it: the Viking-age settlement at Jorvik.*

**The thought.** null - you read the card.

**Why it is here.** The next slide's whole route uses the word, and its star fact turns on it. Beside the spine.

---

## Slide 8 - The Vikings who never sailed home

**On the board.** A photograph of flat, wide farmland in eastern England, and a small timeline showing 793 and 865 with the seventy years between them. The route:

> For about seventy years, Viking ships came in the summer, took what they could carry, and sailed home again.
>
> Then in 865 a much bigger Viking army landed, and this one did not leave.
>
> Look at this land in the east of England, and think back to Gunnar's farm high up the mountainside with a summer too short for grain. Here the Vikings could plough, sow, and get a harvest in.

Headline: **The land in eastern England was flat, wide and warm enough to grow grain.**

Key question, in blue: *The Vikings at Lindisfarne sailed home. Why would these ones want to stay?*

Star fact: **The Vikings did not only raid. Later ones came with their families and settled on English land.**

**The thought.** What did this land give them that home could not?

**What happens.** You put the seventy years on the timeline first, so the class feels that raiding went on for a lifetime before anybody settled. Then the land, held straight against Gunnar's mountainside from slide 3. The town of York was theirs; they called it Jorvik.

**What you land.** The star fact.

**Why it is here.** Children can now tell a Viking who came to take from one who came to stay, which is what the final explanation has to do for every reason it gives, and which the card sort tests straight away.

---

## Slide 9 - Raid or settle?

**On the board.** Six short cards and two headings.

> Vikings did both of these things in Britain, but not at the same time.
>
> Sort each card: **Raid** - **Settle**
>
> - They came in the summer and were gone before the winter.
> - They gave the villages Viking names: Grimsby, Whitby, Derby.
> - They carried the silver cups back to their ships.
> - They ploughed the fields and sowed a crop for the next year.
> - They set up a mint in York and made coins there.
> - They sailed home with a boatful of gold and never came back.

Then: *Pick the one you found hardest. Tell your partner why you put it there.*

**The thought.** Did these Vikings come to take something and leave, or come to live here?

**What happens.** Every child places six. The coins one is the trap, because coins look like treasure. The place names one is where the lesson gets its lightest moment: Derby, Whitby, Grimsby are all still on the map, and if anyone's been to Grimsby they've been to a Viking village and nobody told them.

**Look for.** The mint placed under Raid; that child is sorting on "is it shiny" rather than on "did they stay".

**Why it is here.** Children can now tell a raiding fact from a settling fact, which is the sentence the final task ends on.

---

## Slide 10 - Put it together (launch)

**On the board.** What we know, then two answers side by side.

> We know what Viking Scandinavia was short of, what Lindisfarne had, and why later Vikings stayed on English land.
>
> **Not strong enough:** The Vikings came to Britain because they wanted treasure.
>
> **Stronger:** The Vikings came to Britain for more than one reason. They left Scandinavia because the land was mostly mountain, and a family with four sons couldn't give them all a farm.
>
> The first one gives one reason and stops. The second one says there was more than one, then gives a reason with a *because* in it.

Then the steps, in the order children take them.

**The thought.** null - you are setting the task.

**Why it is here.** The class has never written this shape before. It sits beside the spine as the launch of the task on the next slide.

---

## Slide 11 - Why did the Vikings come to Britain?

**On the board.** The task, the frame, and the success criteria beside it.

> Write your answer to today's question: why did the Vikings come to Britain? Fill in each part of the frame below.
>
> **Why they left Scandinavia**
> **What Britain had that they wanted**
> **How raiding and settling were different**

Success criteria, visible while they write:

1. Start by saying the Vikings came for more than one reason.
2. Write one reason that is about their home in Scandinavia. Use *because*.
3. Write one reason that is about what Britain had. Use *because*.
4. Finish by saying how raiding was different from settling.

**The thought.** Which two of the things I know actually answer *why did they come?*

**What happens.** Children write. You circulate. Then the check slide shows a strong answer:

> The Vikings came to Britain for more than one reason. They left Scandinavia because there was hardly any good flat land to farm there, and a family with lots of sons had none left to give them. Britain was worth the journey because monasteries like Lindisfarne were full of silver and gold, and nobody was guarding them. A raid was quick. They took what they could carry and sailed home. Settling was different. Those Vikings brought their families and stayed on the land they took.

**Why it is here.** This is the lesson's evidence: two reasons, each with its own *because*, and the distinction the teacher asked for.

---

## Slide 12 - Is Daniel right?

**On the board.** Daniel, in a speech bubble:

> "The Vikings came to Britain because they liked fighting."

Then, in blue: *Is Daniel right? Use two things you learned today to explain.*

Underneath, smaller: *If you're stuck: what were the Vikings taking home from Lindisfarne?*

**The thought.** Does "they liked fighting" explain anything I learned today?

**What happens.** Children answer aloud or in a line. The answer they should reach: no, because they needed land they couldn't get at home, and because at Lindisfarne they came for the silver and then went. Some of them stayed and farmed, which nobody does for a fight.

**Look for.** "No, they were farmers not fighters"; that child has over-corrected and needs putting back, because they did raid.

**Why it is here.** This is the one retest of the idea the lesson is built against, and it takes the lesson's own question and turns it round: instead of *why did they come*, it asks *is this why they came*. It is the last thing the class does.

---

## The read-back

By the end, children will explain why the Vikings came to Britain using more than one reason, because the lesson gives them what Viking Scandinavia was short of and what Britain held that was worth the crossing, replacing the idea that they came because they liked fighting, evidenced by a written explanation that has to carry two reasons and tell raiding apart from settling.

---

## The decisions the walk-through does not show

**The learning.** Children leave knowing that good farmland was scarce in Viking Scandinavia, that Lindisfarne and monasteries like it held silver with nobody guarding them, and that later Vikings came with their families and farmed English land. The understanding underneath is an **idea**, not a fact: a big movement of people has more than one reason, and the reasons work in two directions, what home was short of and what the new place had. It is named in `concepts` and met on three separate pieces of evidence, the Norwegian farm, Lindisfarne, and the eastern English farmland; the question stays the same and the evidence changes each time.

**The objective and its boundary.** The teacher's objective is used as written and displayed as written. Today covers why they came: the push from Scandinavia, the pull of undefended wealth, and the pull of farmland. Deliberately left out: the Danelaw as a named thing, Alfred, 1066, the names of the Norse gods, and any weighing of whether the Vikings were "good" or "bad" for Britain, which the history reference rules out as a question answered from modern ideas rather than from the period.

**Prior knowledge and structure.** The brief gives two things: Scandinavia and Britain on a map, and the Anglo-Saxons here first. The starter makes both visible rather than assuming them. The new learning is knowledge, so the route is **content-based**: three Teach chunks, each with its own Do, then one practice that uses all of it. Dialogic was the nearest alternative and fails its first condition, because "why did the Vikings come" is not genuinely contested at Year 4; it is knowledge children do not yet hold. Task-centred fails because the class does not hold the knowledge to begin with.

**The dominant sticking point.** That the Vikings came because they were violent people who liked fighting. It is the most documented misconception in primary history, and the fix is teaching what those people needed until the behaviour makes sense. It is exposed and put down once, on slide 5, at the point where the class can see the Vikings choosing a rich undefended place rather than a fight, and retested once on slide 12. The second misconception, that the Vikings only raided, is the teacher's own request; it is taught on slide 8 and sorted on slide 9. Neither is allowed to become the lesson: seven of the twelve slides are about the period rather than about the correction.

**The independent evidence.** Slide 11. It cannot be passed from a surface cue because the frame asks for two reasons of different kinds and a *because* in each, and nothing on the board at that moment supplies either reason; the success criteria say the shape, not the content. A child who slept through the teaching could fill the first heading with nothing.

**Success criteria and worksheet.** Steps, because the child is carrying out a procedure (writing to a frame), so the panel has to tell a stuck child what to do next. The worksheet is generated and optional: a fresh raid-or-settle sort on four statements the lesson never showed, then the same three-part frame as the printed alternative for a teacher who would rather not use books. Priced against one side: sort about 55mm, three frame sections about 105mm, support about 40mm, which fits.

**Load-bearing material.** Six photographs. The Norwegian fjord farm and the eastern English farmland are a matched pair and the lesson's central contrast, so slide 8 fails without either. Lindisfarne, the longship and the carved stone are authentic evidence about a real place and a real event and cannot be generated. The Jorvik settlement carries the word *settle*. The timeline is supporting rather than load-bearing; its job is the seventy-year gap between 793 and 865.

**Names.** The Viking children in the slide 3 sort keep Norse names, because a modern name on a Viking farm would misteach the period and the history reference requires the period stay in view. The drawn name is used for the modern classmate who voices the claim on slide 12, which is the lesson's one speech bubble.

**One case is not the group.** The four families on slide 3 open on "Not every Viking family was in the same trouble", and every question in the lesson is asked about Viking families rather than about one child. All the history is told in the past tense and the titles name the people rather than "them".

**Reasoning and problem solving.** The objective supports judging a rival explanation, and that is the shape designed: slide 12 puts a claim in front of the class that a child could genuinely hold and asks them to test it against what they now know. The practice itself is composition rather than reasoning, which is right, because the objective is *explain*.

**The light line.** The material handed one over, and it was taken: the Viking place names on slide 9. Derby, Whitby and Grimsby are still on the map, and the line in the script is that anyone who has been to Grimsby has been to a Viking village and nobody told them.

**Three children reading below age.** Handled in the design rather than in a separate sheet: the two main Do beats are card sorts on one-line strips rather than reading tasks, the vocabulary cards each carry a real picture, the slide 12 answer may be given aloud, and the launch on slide 10 shows the product before anyone writes.

**Deliberately omitted.** No Viking gods or longship-building beat, both of which are interesting and neither of which answers the question. No timeline beat of its own; the timeline sits on slide 8 where it is needed. No second practice after slide 11, because the frame already is the synthesis and slide 12 changes the demand rather than repeating it.
