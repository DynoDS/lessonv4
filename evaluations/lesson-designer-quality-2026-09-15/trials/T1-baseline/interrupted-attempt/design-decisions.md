# Why the Vikings came to Britain (Year 4 History, 45 minutes)

**The journey, in one line.** Viking families short of farmland at home → Lindisfarne: treasure with no wall, taken and carried home → a Viking army that didn't go home, then farms and place names → raid or settle: which is which → explain both reasons for two Vikings → would they still have come if the monasteries had been empty?

**Teacher orientation:** This lesson teaches why the Vikings came to Britain, keeping two different reasons apart: raiders came for treasure and sailed home, settlers came for farmland and stayed. The starter recaps Scandinavia, Britain and the Anglo-Saxons on a map. Three short teaching chunks follow, each with a quick task: why farmland was short in Norway, why raiders picked Lindisfarne in 793, and why settlers stayed from the 860s. Children then write a three-part explanation about two invented Vikings, Bjorn the raider and Astrid's settler family, and finish by deciding whether the Vikings would still have come if every monastery had been empty. The tricky move is the sort on the "Came to raid, or came to settle?" slide: a Viking who fought the Anglo-Saxons for land and then ploughed it counts as a settler, because what matters is whether they came to stay, and the script on that slide says why.

---

## The lesson, slide by slide

### 1. Scandinavia and Britain (starter)

**On the board:** a map of Europe with three lettered markers and no country names: A on Scandinavia, B on Britain, C on Spain.

(1) Which letter marks Scandinavia?
(2) Which letter marks Britain?
(3) Name one country in Scandinavia today.
(4) Who was living in Britain before the Vikings arrived?

**The thought:** which of these lettered places is Scandinavia, and which is Britain?

**What happens:** children answer the four questions, then the check slide names the answers on the same map: A Scandinavia, B Britain, Norway, Denmark or Sweden, the Anglo-Saxons. The script points at the sea between A and B: that stretch of water is the North Sea, and everything today happens because people crossed it.

**Look for:** C chosen for Scandinavia (they are reaching for "the big bit of land"); "the Romans" for question 4.

**Why it is here:** they can place Scandinavia and Britain with the sea between them, which the crossing on the first Teach slide needs. It is retrieval of what the brief says they hold, with one real decision per item (the distractor letter, and a country name).

### 2. Why was farmland short at home? (Teach 1)

**On the board:** a photograph of a small Norwegian farm on a thin strip of flat land beside a fjord, steep mountains straight behind it. Then, on a second board, the map with Norway and Denmark labelled, Britain labelled, and the North Sea crossing marked, with a photograph of a longship under sail.

- A Viking family lived on what its farm could grow: barley for bread, hay for the cows.
- In Norway, most of the land is mountains. The only flat farmland was a thin strip beside the water, and the farms were small.
- Look at this farm. Where could a bigger family grow more food?
- Star fact: Many Viking families could not get enough farmland at home in Scandinavia.
- Second board: Britain was only a few days away. A longship could cross the North Sea in three or four days.

**The thought:** looking at this farm, where could a bigger family grow more food?

**What happens (say to children):** Think about what a family needs every year. Food, and somewhere to grow it. A Viking family lived on what its farm could grow: barley for bread, hay for the cows over the winter. Now look at this farm in Norway. Mountains straight behind it. Water straight in front of it. The only flat land is that thin green strip. So here's my question: if this family had four sons, and each of them wanted a farm of his own when he grew up, where would it come from? Look at the picture. There isn't anywhere. Farms were small, and when families grew, there wasn't land for every son. Denmark was flatter, but it was small, and its farms were already full. Then the second board: Now look at the map. Here's Norway, here's Denmark, and here's Britain, just across the North Sea. The Vikings were brilliant sailors. In a longship like this one, a crew could cross that sea in three or four days. So there was a problem at home, and there was somewhere close enough to reach.

**What you land:** Many Viking families could not get enough farmland at home in Scandinavia. And Britain was a few days away by longship.

**Why it is here:** this is the push. The settlers' reason at Teach 3 rests on it, and the second part of the final task asks children to explain it for Astrid's family.

### 3. Would they still have left? (Do 1)

**On the board:**

Imagine Norway had wide, flat fields, with enough land for every son to have a farm of his own.

Would Viking families still have needed to leave? Choose yes or no. Say why.

**The thought:** was it the shortage of land that pushed families out, or would they have left anyway?

**What happens:** every child chooses yes or no and gives one reason (a whiteboard, a book, a partner: the teacher's call). The script says: We're going to change one thing about Norway and see what happens. Keep the mountains, keep the winters, keep the longships. Just give them lots of flat land. Decide, then tell me why.

**What you are listening for:** No: most families would have stayed, because the thing pushing them out was not having enough land. A child who says yes and gives "treasure" as the reason is thinking about a different kind of Viking, and that is exactly where the next slide goes; say so.

**Why it is here:** they can name the shortage of farmland as the push, in their own words, rather than reading it off the board. The Practise asks for that reason again for Astrid's family.

### Vocabulary: raid, monastery (after Do 1)

Two cards, one slide. **raid:** To raid a place is to attack it suddenly, take what you want and leave quickly. Picture: the Lindisfarne raider stone, a real carving of warriors with swords and axes. **monastery:** A monastery is a place where monks lived, prayed and worked together. Picture: Lindisfarne Priory by the sea.

Script reads both cards and takes one quick boundary decision on raid: a ship lands, the crew grab the silver from a church and sail off the same day. Is that a raid? Yes. A family moves to a new country and builds a farm. Is that a raid? No. That second one has a different word, and we'll meet it later.

These sit beside the spine: the two words the next Teach uses.

### 4. Why did Viking raiders choose Lindisfarne? (Teach 2)

**On the board:** the raider stone, large, then Lindisfarne Priory with the sea beside it.

- Viking longships could cross the North Sea in a few days. In 793 some of them landed at Lindisfarne, a monastery on a small island off the north-east coast of England.
- Monasteries kept silver crosses, gold cups and books covered in jewels. The monks prayed and worked. They were not soldiers, and there was no wall.
- Look at what the men on the stone are holding. Now look where the monastery is: right by the sea, nothing to stop a ship.
- Star fact: Viking raiders came for treasure they could carry away, then sailed home.

**The thought:** what did the monastery have that a raider wanted, and what made it easy to take?

**What happens (say to children):** The first Vikings to reach Britain didn't come for land at all. In the year 793, Viking ships landed here, at Lindisfarne. It's a monastery on a little island off the north-east coast. A monastery is where monks lived. And monasteries were rich. Silver crosses, gold cups, books with jewels on the covers, all given to the church over many years. The monks prayed and worked and copied books. They weren't soldiers. Nobody had built a wall, because who would attack a monastery? Look at this stone. The monks who survived carved it afterwards. What are those men holding? Swords and axes. It was terrifying. Some of the monks were killed and others were taken away on the ships. The raiders took the treasure and sailed home. And that's the pattern for the next seventy years. Mostly young men. Come in summer, land, take what you can carry, and be back home before the autumn storms. Raiders came for treasure, and they went home.

**What you land:** Viking raiders came for treasure they could carry away, then sailed home.

**Teacher line:** the priory ruins in the photograph were built later than 793; the monastery the Vikings raided stood on the same spot. Say so in a breath if a child asks.

**Why it is here:** the first of the two reasons, on its real case. The raid-or-settle sort needs it, and the first part of the final task asks children to explain it for Bjorn.

### 5. Which place would raiders choose? (Do 2)

**On the board:** three places as cards.

A: A monastery on the coast. Silver crosses and gold cups inside. No wall and no soldiers.
B: A rich town far inland, a day's walk from the sea, behind a wooden wall with guards.
C: A small fishing village on the coast. Nets, boats and a few sheep.

Which place would Viking raiders choose? Write the letter and what tells you.

**The thought:** which place has something worth taking AND an easy way in and out?

**What happens:** every child writes a letter and a reason. Script: Raiders had a summer and a ship. They wanted to come home rich, and they wanted to come home. Look at all three. Which one, and what tells you?

**What you are listening for:** A, with a reason that names both the treasure and the missing wall or the sea. B tells you the child weighed only the treasure. C tells you they weighed only the coast.

**Why it is here:** they can judge a place by what it had and how easy it was to reach and take, which is what the first part of the final task asks them to explain about Lindisfarne.

### Vocabulary: settle (after Do 2)

One card. **settle:** To settle somewhere is to move there and make it your home. Picture: a reconstructed Viking-age farmhouse with a field beside it.

Script reads the card and takes the boundary decision: a crew take the silver and sail home in a week. Did they settle? No. A family bring their cows, plant barley and build a house. Did they settle? Yes. Beside the spine: the word Teach 3 needs.

### 6. Why did Viking settlers stay? (Teach 3)

**On the board:** a timeline with two bands, Raiding and Settling, and three dated marks: 793 the raid on Lindisfarne; 865 a great Viking army lands; 876 Vikings begin farming in the north of England. Then, on a second board, the photograph of the reconstructed Viking farm.

- For about seventy years, Viking raids went like this: land, take, sail home.
- Then in 865 a huge Viking army arrived, and it did not go home. By 876 the Vikings were sharing out the land in the north of England and farming it. Families came over after them, with their animals and their seeds.
- Britain had what Norway didn't: wide, flat, rich fields, and milder winters.
- Look at this farm. Whoever built it wasn't planning to leave.
- Grimsby, Whitby, Derby, Selby: "by" was the Vikings' word for a farm or village. Those names are still on the map today.
- Star fact: Viking settlers came for Britain's good farmland, and they stayed.

**The thought:** why would a family bring its cows across the sea if it was only going to raid?

**What happens (say to children):** By now you might be thinking: right, the Vikings came, they took treasure, they left. That's the whole story. It isn't. Look at the timeline. Seventy years of raids. Then, in 865, something different. A huge Viking army lands, thousands of men, and it doesn't go home. It fights the Anglo-Saxon kings for years. And in 876 the Vikings in the north of England do something raiders never did. They share out the land between them and they start farming it. Families come over after them. Wives, children, cows, sheep, sacks of seed. Now think back to Norway: mountains, small farms, not enough land. And here is Britain: wide flat fields, rich soil, winters that aren't so long. That's what they'd come for. Look at this farm. Turf roof, fields, animals. Whoever built this wasn't planning to leave. And here's the proof that's still on the map today. Grimsby. Whitby. Derby. Selby. That "by" on the end is the Vikings' own word for a farm or a village. Those are Viking names, and people still live in those places. So raiders came for treasure and went home. Settlers came for farmland, and they stayed.

**What you land:** Viking settlers came for Britain's good farmland, and they stayed.

**Teacher line:** this is where the wrong idea "the Vikings only came to raid" is met and undone: the army that stayed, the farms and the place names make it visibly wrong. Teach it once here; it is tested again at the end, not on every slide between.

**Why it is here:** the second reason, and the reason the two are different. The sort straight after needs it, and the second and third parts of the final task use it.

### 7. Came to raid, or came to settle? (Do 3)

**On the board:** two groups, Came to raid and Came to settle, and six cards.

1. Filled the ship with silver from a church and sailed home the same week.
2. Brought a wife, three children and two cows across the North Sea.
3. Built a farmhouse and called the place Grimsby.
4. Went home to Norway before the autumn storms.
5. Fought the Anglo-Saxons for land, then stayed and ploughed it.
6. Came back to the same monastery three summers in a row.

Put each card in the right group.

**The thought:** did this Viking want something to carry home, or a place to stay?

**What happens:** every child sorts all six (a stick-in two-column sheet is offered for this moment). Script: For each card ask yourself two things. What did this Viking want? And did they go home, or stay? Card 5 is the one to talk about afterwards: he fought, and fighting sounds like raiding, but he fought for land and then he ploughed it. He came to stay. Card 6 is the other way round: he kept coming back, but he never stayed.

**Answer (in the notes only):** raid 1, 4, 6; settle 2, 3, 5.

**Look for:** card 5 under raid.

**Why it is here:** they can tell a raider from a settler by what each wanted and whether they stayed, which the third part of the final task asks them to write.

### 8. Why did the Vikings come to Britain? (Practise)

**Launch board first:**

- We've found two different reasons. Raiders came for treasure. Settlers came for farmland.
- A weak answer: "The Vikings came to Britain because they were fierce and wanted to." A strong answer: "Raiders came because monasteries like Lindisfarne had silver and gold, and no soldiers to stop them. They took it and sailed home." The strong one says what Britain had and what the Vikings did about it.
- The steps (the success criteria, which stay on the task board):
  1. Say what Viking raiders wanted from Britain, and what they did once they had it.
  2. Say what was wrong at home for Viking families, and what Britain had instead.
  3. Say one way raiding and settling were different.
  4. Use a real place or date from today.

**Task board:**

Not every Viking who sailed to Britain wanted the same thing.

Bjorn was a young man from Norway. In 793 he rowed a longship to Lindisfarne.
Why did Vikings like Bjorn come to Britain?

Astrid's family farmed a thin strip of land beside a fjord. In 876 they loaded their cows and their seed into a ship and sailed to Yorkshire.
Why did Viking families like Astrid's come to Britain?

How was what Bjorn did different from what Astrid's family did?

**The thought:** what was wrong at home, what did Britain have, and what did each Viking do about it?

**What happens (say to children):** Two Vikings, and they wanted different things. Bjorn first. He's at Lindisfarne in 793. You know what was there and you know what he did. Write why Vikings like Bjorn came. Then Astrid's family. Look at where they lived: a thin strip beside a fjord. You know what was wrong with that, and you know what Britain had. Write why families like hers came. Last part: what's the big difference between what Bjorn did and what Astrid's family did? If you're stuck, ask yourself: who went home, and who stayed?

**What you land (the model, on the check slide):** Raiders like Bjorn came because monasteries like Lindisfarne were full of silver and gold, and the monks couldn't fight back. There was no wall, and a longship could sail straight there. They took the treasure and were home before winter. Settlers like Astrid's family came because there wasn't enough farmland at home. Their farm was a thin strip between the mountains and the water, and there was no land for a growing family. Britain had wide, flat fields and milder winters, so they brought their animals and stayed. The big difference is that Bjorn took what he wanted and went home, but Astrid's family came to stay and made Britain their home.

**Why it is here:** the final performance. Every part is a move children have already made: the push (Do 1), what made a place worth raiding (Do 2), raider or settler (Do 3).

### 9. What if the monasteries had been empty? (Apply)

**On the board:**

Imagine every monastery in Britain had been empty in 793: no silver, no gold, nothing worth carrying away.

Would the Vikings still have come to Britain? Explain your answer.
Which Vikings would have stayed at home, and which would still have come?

**The thought:** which reason for coming had nothing to do with treasure?

**What happens:** a short written answer, then the check. Script: Take the treasure away. Every monastery empty. Nothing worth a summer's rowing. Would anyone still have come? Think about the two kinds of Viking we've met.

**What you land (model):** Yes, some would still have come. Raiders might have stayed at home, because there was nothing to take. But settlers would still have come, because they needed farmland and Britain had it.

**What a wrong answer tells you:** "No, there was nothing to come for" means the child still holds "the Vikings only came to raid". That is the retest of the lesson's wrong idea, once, here.

**Why it is here:** the question turned round. It takes two minutes and it is the beat that makes "more than one reason" the thing the lesson was about, rather than a sentence on a slide. Nothing follows it.

---

**By the end, children will explain why the Vikings came to Britain, giving two different reasons and keeping them apart, because the lesson gives them what Viking families lacked at home (farmland), what Britain had for a raider (undefended treasure by the sea) and for a settler (wide fertile land), and the evidence that some Vikings stayed, which replaces the wrong rule that the Vikings only came to raid; evidenced by the three-part written explanation about Bjorn and Astrid's family and the empty-monasteries question.**

Read back: the clause after "because" is about the period's content, not a rule about sources. A child in this class could not have said it before the lesson. The check catches a child who lacks it: Bjorn's part cannot be answered without the treasure and the missing wall, Astrid's part cannot be answered without the shortage of land at home, and the empty-monasteries question cannot be answered "yes" by a child who thinks every Viking was a raider.

## The decisions the walk-through does not show

- **The learning itself.** By the end children know two facts they did not hold: Viking families in Scandinavia were short of farmland and Britain was a few days away by longship; and the Vikings who came to Britain wanted different things, raiders treasure to carry home and settlers land to stay on. They understand that "why did the Vikings come" has more than one answer and that raiding and settling are different actions by different people at different times. This is a body of knowledge with a distinction inside it, not a transferable idea, so `concepts` is empty and the lesson develops its one case (the Vikings and Britain) properly rather than sampling several.
- **The objective and end performance.** LO used verbatim: To explain why the Vikings came to Britain. End performance: a written explanation in three parts (why raiders came, why settlers came, how they differed) about two invented Vikings, plus a two-minute turned-round question. Curriculum boundary for today: reasons for coming. Deferred: what life in the Danelaw was like, Alfred and the treaty, Viking beliefs, trade (trade gets one sentence in the Teach 3 script as interesting knowledge, not an assessed reason).
- **Prior knowledge and structure.** Visible foundation: Scandinavia and Britain on the map, Anglo-Saxons already in Britain (both retrieved in the starter). Everything else is new. Content-based: the history file routes "why people acted as they did" here, and the learning is knowledge with a distinction, not a contested position (so not dialogic) and not a single sustained task children already hold the knowledge for (so not task-centred). The Do beats do the thinking with a decision or a sort rather than an explanation every time: change-one-thing, choose-and-justify, sort; the explanation is saved for the Practise.
- **Dominant sticking point.** "The Vikings only came to raid, because they were violent." Exposed and resolved once at Teach 3 (the army that stayed, the farms, the place names); retested once at the Apply (empty monasteries). A secondary blur, "fighting makes a Viking a raider", gets one clean touch: card 5 in the Do 3 sort and the script's reason for its placement.
- **Independent evidence.** The Practise cannot be passed by copying: the sticky facts are not on the task board, the cases cue which reason but not the because, and the third part needs the distinction. The Apply cannot be answered from the board at all.
- **Success criteria and worksheet.** Steps (the task is writing an explanation, so a runnable procedure), kept on the Practise board, not marked draw-live (a one-lesson method). The worksheet is a separate fresh sheet: a six-card raid-or-settle sort on new cases, two judged claims (one right, one wrong, so the format does not hand over the verdict) and a two-reason explanation with a real place. Priced at roughly 190 mm of a 250 mm page, so nothing is pre-authorised for removal.
- **Load-bearing representations and pictures.** The Europe map (starter markers, then the crossing) and the timeline (raiding before settling) are drawn by the engine. Five photographs: a fjord farm (the push, must show the thin strip of land), a longship under sail, the Lindisfarne raider stone and Lindisfarne Priory (both real, so real only), and a reconstructed Viking farm (settling). No child-facing web links; the scout searches by subject.
- **Source and sensitivity.** The raid on Lindisfarne is told plainly with one measured sentence about monks being killed or taken; this is standard Year 4 content and the teacher can read it aloud. Invented Vikings carry Norse names (Bjorn, Astrid) rather than the run's drawn modern names, because a Viking called by a modern British first name would read as wrong; the drawn names are used for the two children who make claims on the worksheet.
- **Reading access.** Three children read below their age: the Do beats are a yes/no with a reason, a letter with a reason, and a card sort; the Practise cases are two short sentences each with the question under each; the Apply has a second, smaller question as the way in.
- **Reasoning.** The Apply is the lesson's reasoning shape: a counterfactual that turns the question round. The Do 1 change-one-thing is the same shape on a smaller scale.
- **Playful line.** Nothing here: the material is a raid in which people died and families leaving home. The only light touch is "Nobody had built a wall, because who would attack a monastery?" in the Teach 2 script, which is the monks' own reasoning rather than a joke at their expense.
- **Deliberate omissions.** No Britain settlement map (the place names and the farm photograph carry "they stayed" without a third visual on an already full board). No separate misconception slide (met once at Teach 3, retested at the Apply). No trade or Alfred (another lesson).
- **Flags for the teacher.** None.
