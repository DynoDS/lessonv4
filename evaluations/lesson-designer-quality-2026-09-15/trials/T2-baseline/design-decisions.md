# To explain how sound travels from a source to our ears

Year 4 Science, 45 minutes, mixed class of 30. Content-based.

## The journey, in one line

which part is vibrating → the vibration gets into whatever is touching it → each bit of air passes it on across the room → your eardrum vibrates → no air, no sound

## Teacher orientation

Teacher orientation: Children follow one vibration all the way from a drum skin to an eardrum, and finish by writing a four-part explanation of how a guitar's sound reaches someone across the room. You need a drum or tambourine with a few grains of rice on it, and a tuning fork with a shallow bowl of water. Both are demonstrations you do at the front. The tricky move is the middle one: children usually think the sound itself flies across the room, so on "A room full of air" you draw the row of air circles one at a time and keep saying that each bit only wobbles on the spot and passes it on. Everything after that leans on it, including the Moon question at the end.

## Slide 1: Sounds and vibrating parts (starter)

**On the board.** The heading, the Date label and the LO, then:

> Name the part that is vibrating.
>
> You bang a drum. Which part of the drum is vibrating?
> You pluck a guitar string. Which part is vibrating?
> You hold a ruler over the edge of a desk and twang it. Which part is vibrating?
> You hum. Put two fingers on your throat. What can you feel vibrating?

**The thought.** Which bit of this object is actually moving?

**What happens.** You say: they already know a sound is made when something vibrates, so today they are going to find the vibrating part. Four of them. You warn them on the drum that it isn't the wooden sides. On the last one they physically put two fingers on their throat and hum, and you ask what they can feel. Every child writes four short answers.

**What you land.** The skin stretched across the top of the drum; the string; the part of the ruler sticking out over the edge; something inside your throat. A child who writes "the drum" for the first one has named the object, not the part, and that is the thing this starter is sharpening.

**Why it is here.** They can point at the exact part that vibrates on four different objects, which the first Teach needs so the drum skin is already the thing they are watching.

## Slide 3: sound source and vibration (vocabulary)

**On the board.** Two cards.

> **sound source** - The sound source is the thing that's making the sound. It's the part that's vibrating. *(photograph of the drum being struck)*
>
> **vibration** - A vibration is a really fast wobble, backwards and forwards. The thing wobbles, but it stays where it is. *(close-up of a plucked guitar string)*

**The thought.** Beside the spine: this is the language for the next twenty minutes.

**What happens.** You read both cards. On the second one you stop and say the important half out loud: the thing wobbles, but it stays where it is. The guitar string doesn't fly off across the room. That sentence is the one the fence question and the whole middle of the lesson lean on.

**Why it is here.** Beside the spine. `sound source` is the objective's own word, so the class owns the sentence they will copy into their books; `vibration` is here because "it stays where it is" is half of the lesson's central idea and children arrive with a vaguer sense of the word.

## Slide 4: What does the drum push on? (Teach)

**On the board.** A photograph of a drum being struck with grains of rice bouncing on the skin, headed *Grains of rice on a drum skin*, and:

> You already know the drum skin vibrates when you hit it. Look at what the rice does: the skin throws it up, and it drops back down. The air is pressed right up against that skin as well, all over it. So every time the skin moves out, it shoves the air out of the way.
>
> *(question)* The rice is bouncing because the skin keeps pushing it. What is that skin doing to the air?
>
> *(star fact)* A vibrating object makes the air touching it vibrate too.

**The thought.** The rice moves because the skin pushes it, so what is the skin doing to the air I cannot see?

**What happens.** You do it live: rice on the drum, one tap, and the grains jump. You say the skin is pushing on whatever is sitting on it, and then that something else is sitting on it too, all over it, and they cannot see it. Air.

**What you land.** A vibrating object makes the air touching it vibrate too.

**Why it is here.** They can say the vibrating drum skin makes the air right next to it vibrate, which the next beat tests on water and the beat after carries across the room.

## Slide 5: Will the water move? (Do)

**On the board.** A photograph of a tuning fork held just above a bowl of water, and:

> This tuning fork has just been struck, so it is vibrating. Now I am going to lower it into the water.
>
> What will the water do?
>
> Tick one. Write one reason underneath.
> - The water splashes and ripples.
> - The water stays completely still.

**The thought.** If the fork is vibrating, what will it do to the water that touches it?

**What happens.** You strike the fork, let them hear it, and point out that it looks completely still but isn't. Every child ticks and writes a reason before you dip it. Then you dip it and the water jumps.

**What you land.** It splashes, every time. The fork is vibrating, so it makes the water touching it vibrate too. Children who ticked "stays still" are usually reasoning from the fork looking still, and that is worth naming out loud.

**Why it is here.** They have seen the vibration pass into water as well as air, so the idea is about whatever is touching the source, which the fence question and the Moon question both need.

## Slide 6: A room full of air (Teach)

**On the board.** The drum on one side, Omar on the other, and the row of air circles between them, built live. Headed *A room full of air*.

> The air next to the drum is vibrating now. But look where Omar is: right over on the other side of the hall, and that little bit of air is still sitting by the drum. It doesn't pick itself up and fly over to him. It wobbles on the spot, and as it wobbles it bumps the bit of air beside it and sets that one going too.
>
> *(question)* That bit of vibrating air is still next to the drum. So what gets all the way over to Omar?
>
> *(star fact)* Each bit of air passes the vibration on to the next bit, so it travels right across the room.

**The thought.** If that bit of air stays next to the drum, what actually gets across the room to Omar?

**What happens.** This is the beat the lesson is built on. You draw the drum, draw Omar, and then draw the air between them as a row of circles, saying that really it is just the ordinary air in this room. Then you go along the row one circle at a time: the skin pushes this one, it wobbles, as it wobbles it bumps this one, that one bumps the next. Each circle only wobbles on the spot. Not one of them travels anywhere. The wobble gets passed along until it reaches Omar. You close on the line of people passing a message: nobody moves, the message gets to the end.

**What you land.** Each bit of air passes the vibration on to the next bit, so it travels right across the room.

**Why it is here.** This is where the wrong idea the teacher named gets replaced. The fence question, the guitar explanation and the Moon question all depend on it.

## Slide 7: What is passing it along the fence? (Do)

**On the board.** A photograph of a long metal railing fence, and:

> Arjun taps one end of a long metal fence. Theo is far away at the other end, too far to hear it through the air.
>
> Theo puts his ear flat against the metal, and he hears the tapping clearly.
>
> What is passing the vibration along to Theo's ear?
>
> Tick one. Write one sentence saying how you know.
> - Only the air.
> - The metal fence.
> - Nothing. The sound just goes.

**The thought.** Why would Theo press his ear on the metal instead of just standing and listening?

**What happens.** You tell it as a story and then give the way in: if you're stuck, ask yourself why Theo bothered to touch the fence at all. Every child ticks and writes one sentence.

**What you land.** The metal fence. Each bit of the metal passes the vibration on to the next bit, just like the air does. "Only the air" is the tempting one because air is all the lesson has used so far. A child who ticks "nothing, the sound just goes" is still holding the idea the lesson exists to replace, and it is worth knowing who they are before the writing task.

**Why it is here.** They have used the passing-on idea on metal as well as air, so it is about whatever sits in between, which is exactly what the Moon question takes away.

## Slide 8: eardrum (vocabulary)

**On the board.** One card.

> **eardrum** - Your eardrum is a thin piece of skin, pulled tight, a little way inside your ear. It's shaped like a tiny drum skin. *(the cut-through ear diagram)*

**The thought.** Beside the spine.

**What happens.** You say the word, then ask what else is a thin skin pulled tight. A drum skin. That is exactly why it is called an eardrum, and it is about to matter.

**Why it is here.** Beside the spine. It goes in here rather than at the start because it only means something once the vibration has arrived somewhere.

## Slide 9: The tiny drum inside your ear (Teach)

**On the board.** A labelled cut-through of the ear: the outer ear, the canal running inward, the eardrum stretched across the end of it, and an arrow for the vibrating air coming down the canal. Headed *A tunnel, and a skin stretched across the end of it*.

> The vibrating air reaches Theo's ear and goes down the little tunnel inside it. At the far end of that tunnel there's a thin piece of skin, pulled tight like cling film. The vibrating air pushes on it, over and over, and it starts vibrating too. Your brain turns that into the sound you hear.
>
> *(question)* Your eardrum is a thin skin pulled tight, just like the drum skin. What do you think the vibrating air does to it?
>
> *(star fact)* When the vibrating air reaches your eardrum, your eardrum vibrates, and that is when you hear the sound.

**The thought.** Why would a thin skin pulled tight be the right thing to have at the end of that tunnel?

**What happens.** You point along the diagram: the tunnel goes in about as far as their little finger, and right at the end there is the skin. Then you ask what a thin, tight skin does when something pushes on it over and over, which is exactly what the drum skin did at the very start of the lesson.

**What you land.** When the vibrating air reaches your eardrum, your eardrum vibrates, and that is when you hear the sound.

**Why it is here.** They can say what happens at the ear itself, which is the last line of the explanation the main task asks for.

## Slide 10: Why are the machines quieter? (Do)

**On the board.** A photograph of someone in thick ear defenders, and:

> Kai works in a noisy workshop, so he puts on thick ear defenders.
>
> The machines are banging away exactly as loudly as before, but now Kai can only just hear them.
>
> Finish this sentence: The ear defenders stop ...

**The thought.** If nothing can get to my eardrum, can I still hear it?

**What happens.** You stress that the machines have not got any quieter, and give the way in: think about what has to get to your eardrum before you hear anything at all. One line each.

**What you land.** The ear defenders stop most of the vibrating air from reaching Kai's eardrums. "The sound" on its own is the answer to press on, because sound here is the vibrating air, and that swap is the whole lesson in one word.

**Why it is here.** They have used the eardrum step on a chain that is blocked, which the Moon question then repeats for a chain that never starts.

## Slide 11: getting ready to explain (launch)

**On the board.**

> We've followed one vibration all the way from a drum skin, into the air next to it, across a whole room, and onto an eardrum.
>
> A weak explanation says: "Arjun plays the guitar and the sound goes to Omar's ear." It never says how it got there.
>
> A strong one says: "The guitar string vibrates. It makes the air touching it vibrate too. That bit of air passes the vibration on to the next bit, all the way across the room. When it gets to Omar's ear it makes his eardrum vibrate."
>
> The difference is that the strong one never lets the sound jump. Every step hands it on to the next.

**The thought.** What is missing from the weak one?

**What happens.** You read both aloud, slowly, and name the difference rather than leaving them to spot it. Then the steps go up and stay up for the task.

## Slide 12: How does Arjun's guitar reach Omar? (Practise)

**On the board.** A photograph of a child playing an acoustic guitar, the four steps in the criteria panel, and:

> Arjun is playing his guitar. Omar is sitting on the other side of the room, and he can hear it.
>
> Explain how the sound gets from the guitar to Omar's ear.
>
> Write one line for each of these:
> What vibrates first?
> What does it make vibrate?
> How does it get across the room?
> What happens at Omar's ear?

**The criteria, visible while they work.**

1. Name the part that is vibrating.
2. Say what that vibrating part makes vibrate next.
3. Say how the vibration is passed on, bit by bit, until it gets to the person listening.
4. Say what the vibration does when it reaches their eardrum.

**The thought.** Where does the vibration go next, and what hands it on?

**What happens.** You set it in a sentence and remind them of the rule that has run all lesson: the sound never jumps, every step hands it on to the next. Then they write. The answer to watch for is "the sound travels through the air", which sounds right and skips the passing on.

**What you land.** The model answer on the check slide, read against the four steps.

**Why it is here.** They have written the whole chain for a sound source the lesson never showed them, which the Moon question then takes the air away from.

## Slide 13: the model answer

## Slide 14: Could you hear a drum on the Moon? (Apply)

**On the board.** A photograph of an astronaut on the Moon, and:

> On the Moon there is no air at all. None.
>
> An astronaut bangs a drum. Another astronaut is standing a few steps away, watching.
>
> Does she hear it? Write yes or no, and then explain your answer using what you know about how sound travels.

**The thought.** If there is nothing in between to pass the vibration on, can it get anywhere at all?

**What happens.** You say why there is no air, ask the question, and give the way in: picture our line of little circles and ask what's in between. They write yes or no and a reason. Afterwards, the light line: you could be the loudest drummer in the whole solar system up there, and not one person would ever know.

**What you land.** No. The drum skin still vibrates, but there is no air next to it, so there is nothing for it to make vibrate, and nothing in between can pass the vibration on to her. If a child raises the ground, or the radio, both are worth saying, because both are the same idea: the vibration needs something to travel through.

**Why it is here.** Final performance. It is the one retest of the wrong rule the lesson was built to replace, on a case the lesson never went near.

## The read-back

By the end, children will explain how a guitar's sound reaches a listener across the room, because the lesson replaces "the sound just goes" with a chain the class has watched being built: the source vibrates, the air touching it vibrates, each bit of air passes the vibration on, and the eardrum vibrates. That is evidenced by each child's own four-part written explanation of a sound source the lesson never showed them, and by their answer to the Moon question, which a child who is still holding the wrong rule gets wrong.

## The decisions the walk-through does not show

**The learning.** Children will know that a vibrating object sets the material touching it vibrating, that the vibration is handed on from one bit of that material to the next rather than flying across a gap, and that it is the eardrum vibrating that we hear. They will be able to trace that chain for a sound source they have not met. This is an **idea**, not a fact: it transfers to sources, materials and situations the lesson never showed. Its instances are the tuning fork in water, the air across the hall, the metal fence, the blocked ear, the guitar, and the Moon. The evidence changes at every one of them; the question stays the same.

**The objective.** Used exactly as the teacher wrote it, on the board and in books. The end performance is the four-part written explanation. Today's curriculum boundary is that vibrations travel through a material to the ear. Deliberately deferred: pitch and volume, anything about how fast sound travels, wave diagrams and the words that go with them, and the rest of the anatomy of the ear beyond the canal and the eardrum.

**Prior knowledge.** The brief says children have made sounds with instruments and know sounds are made when something vibrates. The starter makes that foundation visible rather than assuming it, by asking for the vibrating part rather than for the fact, which is a real call on all four items. Nothing else is assumed.

**The structure.** Content-based, because the objective is an explanation children have to understand and be able to give, and the science file routes building and explaining scientific knowledge here. Discovery was the nearest alternative and was refused for a real reason: the thing to be discovered is invisible. Air cannot be watched passing a vibration along, so an investigation would leave children to infer the mechanism from a result that does not show it. The two bounded demonstrations sit inside the content route instead, which is what that route allows.

**The sticking point.** Children believe the sound itself travels from the object straight to the ear with nothing in between doing anything, which is the teacher's own note. It is exposed live by the key question on "A room full of air", replaced there by building the row of circles, met once more as the third option on the fence question, and retested once at the end on the Moon. A second misconception, that the air itself travels all the way into your ear, gets one clean touch on the vibration card and one line in that same script, and no more.

**The independent evidence.** The guitar explanation cannot be passed by a surface cue: the source is one the lesson never used, the four steps ask for the mechanism rather than the outcome, and the weak answer named in the launch is exactly what a child who has not got it writes. The Moon question cannot be passed by repeating a slide, because no slide says anything about the Moon and the answer requires the chain to be run in reverse.

**The criteria and the sheet.** Steps, because the child is carrying out a procedure: producing an explanation with four required links. Not marked `drawLive`, because it is this lesson's own explanation frame rather than a labelled set or a method a sequence of lessons reuses. The worksheet is a separate optional sheet with fresh material: a doorbell heard upstairs, worked as the same four-step chain; two children hearing each other under water; and Kai's claim that sound doesn't need anything to travel through, which is the sheet's retest of the lesson's wrong rule.

**Load-bearing visuals.** Two. The room diagram carries the whole middle of the lesson, so it must show the source, the row of separate bits of air, each one wobbling on the spot, the passing-on arrows and the listener's ear; without the separate bits it teaches the thing it was built to correct. The ear cut-through must show the canal running inward with the eardrum stretched across its inner end, or the word "tunnel" has nothing behind it. Seven photographs, all ordinary objects and scenes, none of them evidence about a particular real thing.

**Safety.** One specific precaution, on the fork demonstration: strike it on the heel of your hand or a rubber bung rather than the desk edge, and keep the bowl of water away from the board and the sockets. Nothing else in the lesson earns a safety line, because nothing else is handled.

**Reasoning and problem solving.** The Moon question is the lesson's reasoning shape: the idea applied in an unfamiliar context with the taught condition removed, and the answer justified. It sits in the ending beat, and it is the beat the objective genuinely earns rather than a word problem bolted on.

**The light line.** The Moon handed one over: you could be the loudest drummer in the whole solar system up there, and nobody would ever know. Taken, in the script, after the answers.

**Deliberately omitted.** A card for `medium`, because the abstract word for "the stuff in between" is what this lesson is trying to make concrete, and the child-facing phrase is "the air" or "whatever is in between". A card for `sound wave`, because a wave is a picture of a thing travelling, which is the misconception wearing a diagram. A string-telephone build, because it teaches the same idea the fence question already teaches and would cost ten minutes the explanation needs.

**Honest note on time.** The beats price at about 42 of the 45 minutes once the date and objective are copied. It is tight rather than over, and the beat with least slack is the guitar explanation. If the class needs longer there, the ear-defenders question is the one to take verbally rather than in writing.
