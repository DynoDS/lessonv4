# Blind pair comparison: three Year 4 lesson walk-throughs

Evidence is quoted from the lessons themselves (board text, scripts, tasks), never from a design's own rationale about itself.

---

## Pair 1: Why the Vikings came to Britain

### Q1. Learning - preferred: B

Both build the same three reasons: too little farmland at home, undefended monastery wealth, wide eastern farmland. Both explanations are adequate and both use real detail.

**A's main task** (slide 11) puts two ship stories on the board beside the criteria:

> *Sigurd's ship left Norway in the spring and was home before the winter. It carried back a silver cross, two gold cups and a book cover set with jewels.*
> *Halla's ship left the same village that summer. On board were Halla, her husband, their three children, six sheep and sacks of seed corn. They never came home. They ploughed a field beside a river in Yorkshire.*

Plausible misunderstanding a Year 4 could hold after A's teaching: *"Raiding Vikings and settling Vikings were two different sorts of people, and they came because Britain had things worth having."* Could that child still do A's task well? Yes. Criterion 3 ("Say whether this ship came to raid or to settle") falls out of "was home before the winter" and "They never came home" by everyday sense. Criterion 4 ("Use one detail from the ship's story") is printed on the board. Criterion 2 ("Say what Britain had that they wanted") is on the board too: silver for Sigurd, a ploughed field for Halla. Only criterion 1 needs the teaching, and the launch slide one minute earlier printed it for Sigurd: *"Sigurd came because there was not enough farmland at home for him, and the monastery had silver and nobody to fight back."* Three of four criteria are serviceable from the stimulus, and the fourth was just modelled on the same character.

**B's main task** (slide 11) puts nothing on the board but three headings and four shape-criteria:

> **Why they left Scandinavia** / **What Britain had that they wanted** / **How raiding and settling were different**

The same child cannot borrow content from the board, because there is none. Everyday sense supplies nothing. Three of four criteria must come from memory of the teaching. B's launch does leak one reason (*"They left Scandinavia because the land was mostly mountain, and a family with four sons couldn't give them all a farm"*), so the leak is not eliminated, but it is one part of three rather than most of a task.

B's task is the tighter test of whether the teaching landed. A's closing counterfactual is the better thinking beat of the two (*"Imagine every monastery in Britain had been poor... Would Viking ships still have come?"*), and it tests "more than one reason" more honestly than B's *"Is Daniel right?"*, but the main-task test decides this question.

### Q2. Teacher use - preferred: B

Board density is a wash. A's Lindisfarne slide carries about 100 words of italic prose plus two photographs plus a question plus a star fact; B's carries about 95 plus the same furniture. Neither is light.

What separates them is that B gives each teaching slide a headline that states the point, so the journey is readable with the notes closed:

> Headline: **This is Holy Island, where the monastery stood.**
> Headline: **The land in eastern England was flat, wide and warm enough to grow grain.**

A's teaching slides have a question as a title and then go straight into prose. The point is in the star fact at the bottom rather than at the top.

Both scripts read as a warm teacher. A: *"This is where you say out loud that we are not asking whether the Vikings were nice or nasty, we are asking what they needed."* B: *"if anyone's been to Grimsby they've been to a Viking village and nobody told them."*

Two small blemishes, roughly cancelling. B prints `null` in the "The thought" field three times. A prints a designer's aside on a vocabulary card: *"(No picture: nothing you can photograph is a raid, and every candidate in this lesson names something else.)"*

### Q3. Pupil experience - preferred: B

In A the substantial work is a single 8-minute block writing two four-criteria explanations, reached from three one-sentence Do beats. The reading cost at that moment is the heaviest point in either lesson: two stimulus paragraphs, two questions and four criteria on one board, at the exact moment children start writing. A's own orientation concedes the risk: *"Print the two ships' stories if your weaker readers need them at the table."*

In B the work is distributed and the reading cost is lower. Both Do beats are one-line strips (*"Bjorn's father had one farm to split between his five sons."*, *"They set up a mint in York and made coins there."*), and the writing is a three-part frame rather than composition from nothing.

A's repetition is more purposeful in form: its three Do beats vary (a sentence, a tick plus a sentence, a word plus a line), where B runs two card sorts in the same format. But B's are on different content and each catches a different thing.

A is the more honest on time, giving a costed budget with a named drop. B gives none, and with two sorts, three teaches, a framed write and a final question it is tighter than it says. That counts for A, but not enough to outweigh the mismatch between A's writing demand and the class it is written for.

### Q4. Materials and preservation - preferred: B

Both use the objective as written, defer the same later content (Danelaw, Alfred, 1066), and name sourceable real photographs. Neither widens beyond "why they came".

The difference is accuracy in what children are told. A's Do 1 board states:

> *Viking families often had six or seven children who grew up. The farms never got any bigger.*

"Six or seven children who grew up" is an overclaim for the period. B makes the same point through individual cases without the numeric claim (*"Bjorn's father had one farm to split between his five sons"*).

B's materials also do more work: the carved stone showing raiders, and a timeline carrying the seventy years between 793 and 865, which is what makes "the Vikings did not only raid" a change over time rather than an assertion. B's rationale calls the Jorvik photograph authentic evidence when it is a reconstruction, but that wobble is in the rationale, not in front of children.

**Would rather teach: B.** Strongest reason: its final task cannot be answered off its own board, so what children write is evidence of the teaching rather than of the stimulus.

## Pair 2: How sound travels from a source to our ears

### Q1. Learning - preferred: B

Both build the same chain and both teach the pivotal step well. A's narration of it is the more vivid of the two: *"Each circle only wobbles on the spot. Not one of them travels anywhere. The wobble gets passed along until it reaches Omar."*

But A's launch slide prints the answer to A's own main task, about the same guitar and the same two named children:

> A strong one says: "The guitar string vibrates. It makes the air touching it vibrate too. That bit of air passes the vibration on to the next bit, all the way across the room. When it gets to Omar's ear it makes his eardrum vibrate."

The next slide asks: *"Arjun is playing his guitar. Omar is sitting on the other side of the room... Explain how the sound gets from the guitar to Omar's ear."*

Plausible misunderstanding after A's teaching: *"The sound leaves the guitar and goes to Omar; the air is the empty space it goes through."* Could that child do A's main task well? Yes, completely, by reproducing the model they read a minute earlier on the same case. No transfer is required.

B's launch deliberately models a different case, a whistle across the playground, and then asks about a clap at the front of the room. The same child must at least carry the structure onto a new source, and steps 2 and 3 of the criteria are exactly what they cannot supply.

A has a second problem in its own teaching. Having taught that the air does not travel, A's Do 3 answer is *"The ear defenders stop most of the vibrating air from reaching Kai's eardrums"*, and its Teach 3 board says *"The vibrating air reaches Theo's ear and goes down the little tunnel inside it."* That reinstates travelling air as the answer to a response beat.

### Q2. Teacher use - preferred: A

A addresses the teacher throughout: *"You do it live: rice on the drum, one tap, and the grains jump."* / *"You strike the fork, let them hear it, and point out that it looks completely still but isn't."* / *"You close on the line of people passing a message: nobody moves, the message gets to the end."*

B narrates in the third person: *"The teacher reads the three questions"*, *"The teacher holds a strip of tissue paper about 10 cm in front of the speaker"*, *"The teacher points along the canal to the eardrum while talking."* The content is the same and the board sentences are warm, but the surrounding text reads as a description of a lesson rather than notes to a teacher. This is the one place where B's own house voice slips.

B is tidier in two ways, not enough to turn it: it gives each teaching slide a headline, and it flags where the pivotal board may need splitting. A carries three bare answer-slide stubs and writes "Beside the spine: this is the language for the next twenty minutes" into a pupil-facing field.

### Q3. Pupil experience - preferred: B

A's substantial pupil work has been removed by A's own launch. The four-line guitar explanation, which is where the thinking should sit, is a transcription of the previous slide. B's clap explanation is a genuine transfer, and its ordering beat (*"Four of these five things happen when you hear the school bell. One of them never happens at all."*) is a cheap, high-information commitment every child can make.

A's repetition is the less purposeful of the two. The fork in water, the metal fence and the ear defenders all make adjacent points about the material in between, in the fifteen minutes before the writing. A itself nominates the ear defenders as the beat to drop.

Kit load favours B: one demonstration with a speaker and a strip of tissue paper, against A's rice on a drum plus a tuning fork struck correctly and lowered into a bowl of water at the front, near a board and sockets.

A is better on honesty about time, giving a costed 42 of 45 with a named drop. B prices only the worksheet.

### Q4. Materials and preservation - preferred: B

A's resources are the more ambitious and the less certain. A tuning fork and a bowl of water is a memorable demonstration and many classrooms do not have the fork. B needs a speaker or tablet and tissue paper.

On boundary, A goes past the objective. The teacher asked for the vibration travelling through the air. A teaches it through water (*"This tuning fork has just been struck... What will the water do?"*) and through metal (*"What is passing the vibration along to Theo's ear? ... The metal fence."*). Both support a wider generalisation, and neither is what was asked for, and the fence beat costs a slot.

On accuracy, A's ear-defenders answer contradicts A's own central teaching, as set out under Q1. B's demonstration choice is also the more faithful: tissue paper fluttering in front of a speaker is vibrating air pushing a thin thing, which is the eardrum step rehearsed before children meet it.

Both give one specific safety precaution and neither pads it.

**Would rather teach: B.** Strongest reason: A prints the answer to its own main task, about the same guitar and the same two children, on the slide immediately before it.

## Pair 3: Multiplying a two-digit number by a one-digit number using partitioning

The teacher asked for a standard My Turn / Our Turn / Your Turn and nothing fancy. Neither design is novel: both use part-whole models, both run the standard cycle, neither bolts on a hook.

### Q1. Learning - preferred: B

Both develop the same method and both name the sticking point out loud at the moment it tempts. A: *"some of you will want to write 60 and then just put the 3 on the end, because the 3 is sitting right there. But the 3 didn't stay as 3. I multiplied it, and it turned into 9."* B: *"Some people see the 6 and the 24 and write 624. That 6 isn't a six, it's 60. I'm not sticking the answers next to each other, I'm adding them."*

Both independent sets are well built. A's four (43 x 2, 27 x 3, 52 x 4, 36 x 5) and B's five (21 x 4, 27 x 3, 43 x 4, 36 x 5, 48 x 8) each make a child holding the wrong rule produce visibly wrong answers.

The difference is the adequacy of the explanation of the one genuinely new piece, multiplying a multiple of ten. A teaches it in words alone:

> 20 is 2 tens. / 3 lots of 2 tens is 6 tens. / 6 tens is 60, so 3 x 20 = 60.

B teaches it on a countable representation, three groups of two ten-rods: *"Count the rods with me: two, four, six. Six rods, every rod a ten, so 6 tens, which is 60."*

Plausible misunderstanding after A's teaching: *"When I multiply the tens, I multiply the digits and the answer is a small number of tens."* A child holding it meets 52 x 4 in the main practice needing 50 x 4 = 200. A's four modelled examples produce tens answers of 60, 60, 60 and 80, and the largest tens product A ever shows is 4 x 30 = 120, tried in heads at the start. Twenty tens is never shown. That child writes 20 + 8 = 28 or similar on two of the four questions, from a case A never worked. B models 30 x 4 = 120 and 40 x 5 = 200 in the Our Turn, so 160, 150 and 320 in its set all sit inside a pattern the class has watched.

### Q2. Teacher use - preferred: A

A gives each model its own board: the calculation, one blank part-whole model, the four criteria. Nothing competes.

B puts two worked examples on the My Turn board at once (23 x 3 and 28 x 3), each with its own part-whole model and room for three lines of working, plus the four criteria and the star fact. Its Our Turn board does the same with 32 x 4 and 47 x 5. For a board the teacher writes on live while thirty children watch, that is twice the furniture and half the room.

B's script carries more verbatim teacher speech and it is good speech (*"Five quick ones to wake your brains up."* / *"You've already used this word this morning."*). A's verbatim lines are fewer but land at the two moments that matter, and A's boards are what a class can actually read from the back.

One small fault in A: the model answer for the closing question calls Chloe "he".

### Q3. Pupil experience - preferred: B

Both sit the work in books with no transitions to cost.

Readiness is the separator, and it is the same point as Q1 seen from the child's side. Two of A's four independent questions require a tens product larger than anything A modelled inside the method. Every case in B's five has been worked in front of the class.

A is the more honest on time. Its two cycles plus the judging task price out at the full forty-five, and it says why problem solving is not in the deck. B carries five calculations, a judged claim and two word problems with no budget at all, and the last beat is the one that will get cut without being named as droppable.

A's repetition is purposeful: the second cycle exists to model the ones product passing ten before children meet it alone. B's doubling inside each cycle is purposeful too, since each pair changes exactly one thing.

### Q4. Materials and preservation - preferred: A

A displays the objective as the teacher wrote it, method tail included. B trims it, displaying *"To multiply a two-digit number by a one-digit number"* and cutting "using partitioning". B's own vocabulary slide then rests on a word that is no longer there: *"The objective names the word, so the class has to be able to read the LO they copy down."*

B also widens past the objective in its final beat, two word problems where the child has to find the calculation first (*"Nobody's told you which calculation to do this time"*). Finding a calculation inside a story is a different demand from multiplying by partitioning, and it is what pushes an already full lesson over. A keeps the word problem on the worksheet.

Prior knowledge is handled more tightly by A. The brief says tables and partitioning are secure, so A's starter picks the cases that still bite (70, which has no ones; 8 x 7 and 5 x 9 at the far end of the tables) and cards both *partition* and *product* with a job for each. B spends five starter questions and a vocabulary slide on what it concedes the class already owns: *"a glance reference for a word the class already owns."*

Both sets of answers are arithmetically correct, and both representations are engine-drawn and ready. B leaves the answer to its first Our Turn example, 32 x 4, unstated.

**Would rather teach: B.** Strongest reason: every case in its independent practice has been worked in front of the class first, while two of A's four require a tens product larger than anything A modelled.
