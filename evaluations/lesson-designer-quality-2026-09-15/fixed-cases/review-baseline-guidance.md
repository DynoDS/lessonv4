# Blind review of fourteen cases (baseline reviewer guidance)

CASE H1
Verdict: REVISE
Reason: The task is a six-card good/bad sort followed by `Did the bad or the good things matter more to a Tudor family? Why?`, recorded as the evidence that children can explain the family's choice. The response "the good things, because hot meals are nice" passes it while using nothing the lesson taught: the cards can be sorted from everyday sense (hot meals good, strict master bad) and the why is answered from the card's own words, so a child who missed every Teach could produce it, which means the taught reasoning (food and a home now, one less mouth to feed, a trade to earn a living later) is left untested. Smallest repair: keep the cards and replace the question with one that makes the family's need do the work, such as `Pick the card that mattered most to a family that could not feed everyone. Why did that one matter more than hot meals?`, with the key demanding a reason about food, money or a future living rather than niceness.
Second answer: The key's placements are contestable, since `one less mouth to feed at home` is good for the family but not obviously a good thing "about being an apprentice" for the boy, and `a strict master` can be defended as good from the family's view (he learns the trade properly), so a child arguing either placement with a family reason should not be marked wrong.

CASE H2
Verdict: PASS
Reason: The task matches three fresh jobs (not the ones the slide showed) to what they gave the household, with one card deliberately left over, and asks why it does not belong. The response uses the Teach's idea, that children's work went to the family, at the exact point the task tests it: the left-over `their own pocket money` cannot be placed without holding that the work served the household, and "because the money went to the family" is the idea applied, not the slide re-read. It is recorded as a quick check before the apprentice chunk and claims no more than that, so the claim matches the delivery. No repair needed.
Second answer: `scaring birds off the wheat` can be defensibly matched to `money for the family` (the wheat was sold) rather than `food for the family`, so the key should accept that pairing with its reason as long as pocket money is still the card left over.

CASE S1
Verdict: REVISE
Reason: The task asks `Which bulb will be brighter? Why?` over two drawings in which one bulb is already drawn large and bright yellow and the other small and grey, so the answer is printed in the stimulus. The response "the left one, because it is drawn brighter" is correct by the key and shows nothing about wire length; the taught relationship (longer wire, harder flow, dimmer bulb) is never needed, and the drawing also confounds bulb size with wire length. Smallest repair: draw both bulbs identical and unlit and let wire length be the only visible difference, which is what S2 already does.
Second answer: none found.

CASE S2
Verdict: PASS
Reason: The task shows two identical unlit circuits differing only in the printed wire lengths and asks which will be brighter and what in the circuit tells you. The response "the 20 cm one, the wire is shorter so it flows easier" can only be produced by applying the taught relationship; a child who missed the Teach has no cue on the board and might equally guess the longer wire carries more electricity, so the answer depends on the learning rather than on a picture or wording cue. The task claims a prediction from the relationship and delivers it. No repair needed.
Second answer: none found; "80 cm because more wire means more electricity" is the misconception, not a defensible alternative, and the lesson's simplification holds at this level.

CASE G1
Verdict: REVISE
Reason: The task, `Name a town that is next to a river`, is recorded as the main practice for `To explain why early settlements grew near rivers`, and the response "Ashford" passes it by reading a label off the map. Nothing about water, transport or flat land is asked for, so the objective's whole explanatory content is untested and a child who missed the Teach does just as well. Smallest repair: keep the map and ask `Ashford grew beside the river. Give two reasons the first people to settle there wanted to be next to it`, with the key expecting the taught reasons in the child's words.
Second answer: Any town on the map that touches a river is a correct name, so the key must list every riverside town rather than one, but this is a key-completeness point and does not rescue the task.

CASE G2
Verdict: PASS
Reason: The task puts an unseen map with a river, hills and three sites in front of the class and asks which site to choose and what on the map made you choose it. The response "B, because it is next to the river so they can get water and it is not on the hills" applies the taught reasons (water, flat land) to a case the teaching did not show, and a child without the Teach could pick B by luck but could not give the reasons from the map alone, so the explanation is what the task evidences. The task claims a site decision with taught reasoning and delivers it. No repair needed.
Second answer: The key already admits A with a river reason; a child choosing C for a reason such as safety from flooding has given a real geographical reason the key excludes, and the teacher should hear it as a reason to weigh against water access rather than mark it wrong.

CASE M1
Verdict: PASS
Reason: The task is eight fresh three-digit numbers in no pattern, each on a number line from the lower to the upper hundred with no halfway mark, and the three taught steps in a panel; the response is all eight correct with the halfway mark drawn every time. This is fluency practice repeating a known move on purpose, which the guidance protects, and the steps panel is the method rather than the answer, so the child still has to find the halfway point and decide the side for each number, including the boundary cases 199, 449 and 505. Smallest repair: 650 sits exactly on the halfway point and the panel's `decide which side` does not settle it, so the panel or the earlier modelling needs the line that exactly halfway rounds up.
Second answer: none found; 650 to 600 would be a wrong application of the convention rather than a defensible alternative, provided the halfway-rounds-up rule is on the board as above.

CASE M2
Verdict: REVISE
Reason: The task is the same eight questions with the target hundred already circled in green on each number line, so the decision the objective names (which hundred is nearer) is made for the child before they start. The response of all eight correct shows only that the child copied the circled number; the halfway step and the side decision are never demanded, so the taught method is untested and the sheet reads as answer-giving support. Smallest repair: remove the green circle and leave the number line from lower to upper hundred, as in M1.
Second answer: none found.

CASE E1
Verdict: REVISE
Reason: The task, `Write three sentences about the forest. Include at least two adjectives in each`, is recorded as the main practice for a setting description that makes the reader feel the place, but the measure it sets is an adjective count. The response meets the count with telling words (`big`, `dark`, `scary`, `cold`) that are exactly the flat version the Teach set beside the model, so the taught distinction between showing detail and telling the feeling is never required and the child passes the task while missing the objective. Smallest repair: ask for three sentences that make the reader feel the forest is frightening without using a feeling word, each built on something the reader could see, hear or touch, and key the answer on the show/tell distinction.
Second answer: none found; the response is a correct answer to the task as written, which is the defect.

CASE E2
Verdict: PASS
Reason: The task gives four plain sentences and asks for a fronted adverbial added to each, recorded as guided practice before the writing. The response "Later that night, the fox crept into the garden." performs the taught move exactly (adverbial moved to the front, comma after it), and a child who missed the Teach would not know what to add or where the comma goes, so it is a purposeful drill of a known move rather than a test the response bypasses. The task claims guided practice and delivers it. No repair needed.
Second answer: Any adverbial of time, place or manner with the comma is correct, including a single word such as `Suddenly,`, so the key must accept the class of answers and not a listed few.

CASE R1
Verdict: REVISE
Reason: The task, `Do you like singing carols? Why?`, is recorded as the evidence for `To understand why a carol matters to a Christian`, and the response "Yes, because it is Christmassy and fun" answers it fully without touching Layla, Ethan or the taught point that belief cannot be read from the singing. It is an opinion any child could give before the lesson, so the objective's understanding is entirely untested. Smallest repair: replace it with a question that needs the taught meanings, such as `Layla and Ethan both sing this carol. Why does it matter to Layla in a way it might not to Ethan?`, and add `Could you tell from this photograph which one they are like?` to retest the cannot-tell point.
Second answer: none found; every opinion is a valid answer, which is the defect.

CASE R2
Verdict: PASS
Reason: The task asks children to write what Christmas means to them, then one thing the same or different from what it means to Layla, and the response "seeing my cousins... the same as Layla's family bit, but she also has the Jesus part and I don't" places the child's own meaning beside both taught meanings and reasons across them. A child who missed the Teach could write the first sentence but not the second, so the comparison is where the learning shows, and it matches the preference's own example of a personal reflection that uses the lesson. Smallest repair: write `Layla, a Christian` in the task so the comparison is with the group the objective names rather than one invented child, and make sure a supported compare (Ethan beside Layla) was done before this, since the case lists only the Teach.
Second answer: A child whose own meaning includes Jesus will write a "same" answer on the belief part, and the key must accept same or different on either meaning rather than expecting the difference.

CASE P1
Verdict: REVISE
Reason: The task, `What should Sam do?` on the scenario the class already discussed during the Teach, with a key that accepts "be kind and tell someone", lets the response "Sam should be kind" pass without any of the three taught boundary sentences. The scenario has been talked through already, so the answer is recall or generic virtue, and the objective's actual content (which sentence keeps the boundary and what to do next) is untested; the key's own wording is the giveaway. Smallest repair: put a fresh scenario on the board and ask every child to choose which of the three sentences Sam should say and what Sam does next, keyed to a sentence and an action.
Second answer: none found beyond the key itself, which admits an answer weaker than the taught ones.

CASE P2
Verdict: PASS
Reason: The task hands a fresh scenario (an unknown sender asking Priya where she lives) and asks every child to number the four taught step cards, so each child commits and the learning is applied to a case the Teach did not cover without any child writing about themselves. The response 1 stop, 2 don't reply, 3 tell an adult, 4 keep the message is the taught sequence used; the fresh scenario changes little about the work, since the order is the same for any message, but securing the routine on a new case is a legitimate quick check and the task claims no more. Smallest repair: `stop` and `don't reply` are hard to tell apart as separate actions, so either merge them or give the key a plain one-line difference.
Second answer: Keeping the message before telling the adult (so the adult can see it) is a defensible order that the key wrongly excludes, so steps 3 and 4 should be accepted in either order.
