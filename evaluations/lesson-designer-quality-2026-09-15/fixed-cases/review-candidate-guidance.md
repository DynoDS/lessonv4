# Blind review of fourteen cases (candidate reviewer)

CASE H1
Verdict: REVISE
Reason: The task is the six-card good/bad sort with the follow-up "Did the bad or the good things matter more? Why?", recorded as the lesson's evidence that children can explain the family's choice. A child who missed every Teach sorts all six from everyday sense (early mornings unpleasant, hot meals nice), and the judged response "the good things, because hot meals are nice" passes the follow-up while holding exactly the misunderstanding the lesson exists to remove (fed, so it was fine; the trade did not matter). The learning left untested is that the trade and one less mouth at home were the point of the deal, and the smallest repair is a case that the everyday reading gets wrong (a master who feeds Will and never teaches him: why would his family be unhappy?) or "which two of these would a family have minded losing most?", which changes the thinking demand and is the Lesson Designer's. The sort itself can stay as a two-minute orientation if it is renamed as a check and not the evidence.
Second answer: `one less mouth to feed at home` is a good thing for the family and not for the apprentice, so a child who puts it under Bad ("he had to leave home") is defending a real reading the key marks wrong; the heading pair says "being an apprentice", so the key needs a second accepted placement with its reason, or a cleaner card.

CASE H2
Verdict: PASS
Reason: The task is a three-job match to four benefits with one card left over, recorded as a quick check straight after the Teach that children's work fed the household. The jobs are fresh cases the slide did not show (scaring birds off the wheat, selling eggs, gathering branches), every child places every card, and the leftover card is the one that tests the distinction just taught: the money went to the family, not to the child, which is what the judged response says. It claims only that the class caught the distinction before the apprentice chunk, and it delivers that; the lesson's main evidence is judged elsewhere.
Second answer: `scaring birds off the wheat` is keyed to food but "money for the family" is defensible if the wheat was sold, and `selling eggs` could be argued as food; the key should accept either family benefit for those two with a reason, since the learning is family-not-self, not which benefit.

CASE S1
Verdict: REVISE
Reason: The task is "Which bulb will be brighter? Why?" over two drawings where the short-wire circuit already carries a large bright-yellow bulb and the long-wire circuit a small grey one. The judged response "the left one, because it is drawn brighter" is keyed correct and uses nothing the lesson taught; the drawing answers the question the relationship was meant to answer, so the taught link (longer wire, harder flow, dimmer bulb) is untested. The smallest repair is identical unlit bulbs with the wire lengths shown, which changes the stimulus and is the Lesson Designer's.
Second answer: none found.

CASE S2
Verdict: PASS
Reason: The task is "Which bulb will be brighter, and what in the circuit tells you?" over two identical unlit circuits differing only in printed wire length. Nothing on the board settles it except the taught relationship, and the judged response ("the 20 cm one, the wire is shorter so it flows easier") states that relationship in the child's own words. The printed lengths are the question, not a giveaway; the task claims a prediction from the relationship and delivers it.
Second answer: none found.

CASE G1
Verdict: REVISE
Reason: The task is "Name a town that is next to a river" on a map with four labelled towns and two labelled rivers, recorded as the main practice for "explain why early settlements grew near rivers". The judged response "Ashford" is read off two labels, and a child who missed the Teach gives it as easily; the taught relationship (water, transport, flat land) is never used, so the objective's "why" is untested. The smallest repair is the choose-a-site task on a map the lesson has not shown, which replaces the main activity and is the Lesson Designer's. A label-reading beat is fine only as a named recall check after a map skill, which this lesson is not teaching.
Second answer: none found; any labelled riverside town is correct and the key presumably accepts all of them.

CASE G2
Verdict: PASS
Reason: The task is to choose a village site on an unlabelled map the lesson never showed and say what on the map decided it. The judged response ("B, because it is next to the river so they can get water and it is not on the hills") applies two parts of the taught relationship to features the child had to read for themselves; nothing on the map names the answer, and the key already accepts A with a river reason if the slope is argued. The task claims application of the relationship to a new map and delivers it.
Second answer: a child choosing C on the high ground "because it is safe from floods" has a real geographical reason the lesson did not teach; excluding it from the key is defensible because the objective is the river relationship, but the acceptance condition should tell the teacher to hear the reason and redirect rather than mark it wrong.

CASE M1
Verdict: PASS
Reason: The task is eight three-digit numbers in no pattern, each with a number line from the lower to the upper hundred, no halfway mark, and the three taught steps beside them. The judged response draws the halfway mark and decides the side every time, which is the taught method repeated on fresh numbers; the steps panel enables the method without making the decision, and repetition of a known move is what practice is. One bounded repair: 650 sits exactly on the halfway point, so "decide which side" cannot settle it, and the steps panel or key must carry the halfway-rounds-up convention (or the design must show it was modelled) before that question counts.
Second answer: 650 to 600 is a defensible answer from a child applying the three steps as written, because the number has no side; the key needs the stated convention rather than a silent 700.

CASE M2
Verdict: REVISE
Reason: The task is the same eight questions with the target hundred already circled in green on every number line. The judged response of eight correct answers proves only that the child followed the circle; the decision the task claims to assess (find halfway, judge the side) is supplied by the support, so a child who cannot round at all scores full marks. The smallest repair is to remove the circles, which the M1 version already shows working; if the circled version was meant as a supported rehearsal, it must be named as one and not recorded as evidence.
Second answer: none found.

CASE E1
Verdict: REVISE
Reason: The task is "Write three sentences about the forest, include at least two adjectives in each", recorded as the main practice for making the reader feel the place. The judged response meets the count exactly and does what the Teach named as the weak move, telling the reader "scary" and "cold" rather than showing them; a child who missed the model pair writes it just as well. The taught quality (detail that does the work) is untested, and the smallest repair is the task that forbids the telling words ("make the reader feel the forest without saying scary, cold or dark"), which changes the thinking demand and is the Lesson Designer's.
Second answer: a child who writes "A branch caught my sleeve. Something rustled behind me." has done exactly what was taught and fails the two-adjective count, so the current key marks the strongest answer wrong.

CASE E2
Verdict: PASS
Reason: The task is to add a fronted adverbial to four plain sentences, recorded as guided practice before the writing. It asks for the move just modelled (adverbial to the front, comma after it) on sentences the model did not use, every child commits to each one, and the judged response shows the move with the comma in place. A short drill of a taught move is legitimate practice and the task claims nothing more than that.
Second answer: any sensible adverbial with a comma is right, so a key that lists one expected phrase per sentence would wrongly exclude most correct answers; the key should be the form (fronted, comma), not a phrase.

CASE R1
Verdict: REVISE
Reason: The task is "Do you like singing carols? Why?" over a photograph of a family singing, recorded as the evidence for understanding why a carol matters to a Christian. The judged response "Yes, because it is Christmassy and fun" answers it fully without the two taught meanings or the taught idea that belief cannot be read from the act, and a child who answered "yes, because of Jesus" would have agreed with the board rather than understood it. The smallest repair is the question that needs both meanings and the watching test ("Layla sings it for two reasons, Ethan for one: what is the difference, and could you tell from watching?"), which replaces the task and is the Lesson Designer's.
Second answer: none found; opinion has no wrong answer, which is the fault.

CASE R2
Verdict: PASS
Reason: The task is to write what Christmas means to you, then one thing the same or different from what it means to Layla, recorded as the intended personal outcome for an objective that names the child's meaning and a Christian's. The judged response places the child's own meaning beside Layla's two taught reasons and separates the family part from the Jesus part, which is the learning used, not a holiday memory; a child who missed the Teach could not name Layla's second reason. The reflection draws on the taught meanings, and the comparison move is the one the task asks for, so it claims what it delivers.
Second answer: a child who compares with Ethan ("mine is like Ethan's, family only") has used the taught meanings just as well, so the key should accept a comparison with either taught person rather than Layla alone.

CASE P1
Verdict: REVISE
Reason: The task is "What should Sam do?" on the scenario the class already discussed during the Teach, with a key accepting "be kind and tell someone". The judged response "Sam should be kind" is a general opinion that uses none of the three taught boundary sentences, and the key lets it through; because the scenario was talked through in the Teach, even a fuller answer can be recall of that discussion. The learning left untested is the taught move itself (what Sam could say to keep the boundary), and the smallest repair is "What could Sam say?" with a key expecting one of the taught sentence forms; that is a bounded wording and key correction, but if this beat is the evidence for the objective, a scenario the class has not discussed is needed and that is the Lesson Designer's.
Second answer: none found that the key wrongly excludes; its fault runs the other way, accepting an answer without the taught sentence.

CASE P2
Verdict: PASS
Reason: The task is to number the four taught step cards in the order Priya should act on a fresh scenario (a stranger asking where she lives), with no child writing about themselves. Every child commits to an order, the case is new, and the judged response is the taught sequence applied to it; the cards supply the steps, but the task claims a check of the sequence on a new worry, not the generation of the steps, so it claims what it delivers as a Do beat rather than the main evidence.
Second answer: "keep the message" before "tell an adult" is a defensible order (do not delete it, then show the adult), because the safety learning is that both happen after stopping and not replying; the key should accept either order for steps three and four, with the reason, rather than mark that child wrong.
