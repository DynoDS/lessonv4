# Rules followed word for word

An audit of the lesson rules, 2 November 2026. Nothing in the plugin has been changed. Most harmful first. Each rule has a line at the end for your answer.

**All ten settled with Daniel, 3 November. Nothing is built yet.** The latest agreed answer is under each rule, and it replaces the suggested wording above it wherever the two differ.

**Update after Daniel's reply, 2 November.** His answer runs through all ten: these are good teaching techniques, not blanket rules, and the designer decides when each is right for these children at this moment. Several suggested wordings below were blanket rules themselves, and each is marked under its rule. Nothing in the plugin has changed.

**Correction to rule 1.** A small allowance for wording changes after the design stage already exists, so the honest note under rule 1 overstated what is missing.

**The pattern.** The ten rules below were all good rulings. Each one went wrong in one of three ways:

- it carried a phrase or example that designers copied
- it set a number, and designers worked to the number instead of the reason
- it pulled against another rule, and the one with a check behind it won

**Already changed today, so not in this list:** the journey rule, the map-in-hands rule and "one thing to look at". Those changes are not saved yet.

**How far to trust this.** The evidence is saved lessons and each run's own notes, and none of it comes from teaching. Most of the examples come from Geography Lesson 1. The counts cover all saved lessons, including ones made before a rule existed, so they are rough.

## 1. The time check: 16 board words a minute, 50 minutes in all

**The rule now.** Every word on the board costs time, at 16 words a minute. A lesson over 50 minutes is refused. The speaker notes are not counted.

**What it was for.** Real time. On 20 October a cover teacher took 40 minutes over slides planned at 17, and the class had had enough.

**How it goes wrong.** The check only sees board words, so the easy way to pass is to cut words or move them into the notes. That is the opposite of "the slide carries the teaching".

- At least seven saved lessons have run notes about hitting this limit.
- In Geography Lesson 1 the reviewer tried to add what a border is and was refused. The voice editor then shortened that line. You said it reads oddly and helps nobody.
- On slide 16 the reason for "same biome" went into the notes.
- The slow part in that cover lesson was the teacher's questioning, and cutting a board sentence does not change that.

**Suggested wording.**

> The time check measures how much is in the lesson. When a lesson is over, take out a whole thing: an idea, a task or a case. Never pass the check by cutting words from an explanation or moving a because into the notes. A clearer sentence on the board is always allowed, and if it tips the lesson over, something else comes out.

**Honest note.** Wording alone will not fix this, because the check will still refuse the sentence. It also needs to let small clarity changes through and report the overrun for a person to judge. Your 40 minute question is a separate decision.

**Daniel, 2 November:** Keep 50 as the normal shape, but it should not be a wall: 52 should not be refused. "Take out a whole thing" is too strict, because cutting a task or idea can make a lesson worse, and cutting a line is fine if the explanation still lands. He wants the lesson designer deciding, not a fixed if-this-then-that. **Suggested wording above withdrawn.** Being worked through first. He added: "too long" is not only start to finish time. It is also the content, how heavy the lesson is, how well it was chunked, what tasks children did, how much thinking they managed, how many links they made, and how much of it felt like the teacher just explaining. **Agreed direction:** the feel is the main decision, judged early by the designer and again by the reviewer, and the clock is only a backstop. Lesson 35 scores 56 and he says it felt a little too long, so about 55 is the working line for the backstop. He has said maths is a little different, and that still needs his explanation. The saved Lesson 35 has 34 slides, not the 26 in its plan: every My Turn bar model builds up click by click (three slides, then an Answers slide), from his 19 October yes to building it up. That one yes became the default for every My Turn. **His ruling, 3 November:** the build-up made no sense there; the class needed the full bar model, blank, for him to write in, with nothing clicked or revealed. He also said why it felt long: a starter, teaching, two My Turns, a couple of Our Turns and a Your Turn, then a new My Turn, Our Turn and Your Turn, then the Apply. In saved maths lessons, 15 of 18 run two or more full cycles, though the skills guidance says most concepts need one. **Agreed, 3 November:** before adding a cycle, the designer asks whether it teaches a new step or is the same method again. Sometimes a second cycle is just harder numbers, and those goes can sit in the Your Turn. His limit: if the new thing is worth a full cycle, it gets one, whatever is best for children, and plenty of chances to practise stay wanted either way.

**Number 1 settled, nothing built yet:** the feel of the lesson comes first, judged early and again at review; the clock is a backstop at about 55, with lessons from 50 to 55 going through with a reason; a bar model in a My Turn is full and blank.

## 2. Counting chunks of teaching

**The rule now.** One or two chunks, three at most, and "the count is not argued down". A chunk is counted as a stretch of teaching ended by a task.

**What it was for.** Lessons that were "way too much" (the cholera lesson, 18 October).

**How it goes wrong.** Because a chunk is defined by the task that ends it, taking the task away lowers the count. Geography Lesson 1 calls itself "two chunks". One Teach runs across slides 6 to 12 and covers the Equator's heat, rain, the rainforest, the desert and what a biome is, and no Do comes until the end of it. That is why biomes landed on one slide, and why you asked "where's my explaining?". The existing line against folding ideas together loses to a number that can be passed.

**Suggested wording.**

> Count the new ideas a child meets, whether or not a task follows them. If several new ideas sit in one stretch, that stretch has not become one chunk: split it, or teach fewer ideas. A low count is no use if it was reached by folding ideas together or leaving out the Do.

**Honest note.** This makes lessons look fuller on paper at first, and the designer then has to drop ideas. That is the point of the change.

**Daniel, 2 November:** One concept then a Do is a fantastic rule, but it cannot be blanket. A simple idea may not need a Do of its own if it builds into the next one. It depends what the ideas are. His questions: which ones cause overload if said together? Which would they forget if I moved on, so there was no point teaching it? He does not want a count. **Suggested wording above withdrawn.** **Agreed, 3 November:** one concept then a Do stays the normal habit and the count stops deciding. Before moving on, the designer asks: will they forget this if I go on; is it simple enough to carry into the next idea; would these overload the class together; how much is reaching the children. The reviewer asks the same. Nothing built yet.

## 3. The slide designer copies words exactly and makes no teaching decisions

**The rule now.** Every word a child sees is copied exactly from the lesson plan. The slide designer "makes no pedagogical decisions".

**What it was for.** Keeping your voice and the agreed teaching safe, so words do not drift between stages.

**How it goes wrong.** The slide designer is the only agent that sees the finished slide, and the reviewer reads the plan, not the slides. So nobody can act on your rule that each slide works as if there were no other slides. On slide 9 the layout needed one more line, so the designer lifted "Now think back to the Sahara" from the script. It knew the slide was weak, noted that, and could do nothing else. Slide 6 says Equator and Africa with neither shown.

**Suggested wording.**

> The words stay exact and the teaching stays the lesson designer's. But you are the only one who sees the finished slide, so read each one as if it were the only slide. If it points at something that is not on it, bring that thing on from the lesson: the picture, the map or the earlier line. If that cannot fix it, send it back to the lesson designer, naming the slide and what is missing. Never fill a gap with a line lifted from the script.

**Daniel, 2 November:** The rule stays for what it protects: the slide designer never changes a good Teach or Do because it cannot fit it or prefers something else. But it does make visual teaching decisions: comparing two pictures, it shows both, because that has most impact on children. That leaves the lesson as it was. **Agreed, 3 November:** it never changes what is taught, the task or the words, and never because something will not fit. It does think about the child looking at the slide: what do they need in front of them to do what this slide asks? It reads each slide as if there were no others, and sends back a slide that cannot work without changing the teaching. The layout that forced the line on slide 9 is a separate fault. Nothing built yet.

## 4. Ask before you teach: "What do you notice?" and "predict first"

**The rule now.** In geography: "Where the pattern is legible, show it and ask before you explain." Across subjects: a situation opens with `What do you notice?`, and a quick Do can be "a prediction the next board answers".

**What it was for.** Getting children thinking before being told. `What do you notice?` is your own phrase for reading a situation, and it works when the answer is there to be seen.

**How it goes wrong.** It has become a stock line, on the slides of seven saved decks. It also pulls against "the takeaway usually leads", and against "a blind guess is not useful" in the science file.

- Slide 15: you said it "hopes a greater depth child sees it", with nothing to help anyone else. Slide 16, straight after, tells it with a because, and you liked that one.
- Slide 5: "will it be hot or cold?" came with nothing to reason from, so it was a guess.
- A good one, from Geography Lesson 2: "It's hot and wet on the ground under the canopy. Will the ground be thick with plants, or will hardly any plants grow?" The child has something to reason with.

**Suggested wording.**

> Ask before you explain only when most of this class could really work it out from what is in front of them. Say exactly what to look at, and give the child who cannot see it a way in (two places to compare, a choice, a sentence to finish). A prediction needs something to reason from. If only your strongest child would see it, tell it, with the because, and use it straight after.

**Daniel, 2 November:** Thinking before being told is right when it is right, not always and not never. `What do you notice?` is a real technique: it draws learning out, surfaces misconceptions and shows what children already know. A prediction earns its place if it is worthwhile for the learning, not because something is about to be shown. His wider worry: wording and approaches were so confusing that only greater depth children could follow the lesson. **Suggested wording above withdrawn: its "only when" was a blanket rule.** **Agreed, 3 November:** these stay techniques with no rule on when. The designer asks what the question will draw out and what happens with the answers, whether it is worth doing here or only habit, and whether the child who understands least can do something with it. That last question applies to all wording and tasks, and the reviewer asks it of the actual questions. Nothing built yet.

## 5. Start where the children are, and tell one connected story

**The rule now.** Every lesson starts from the children's own lives, drawn out with a question, and is told as one connected story whose parts could not be shuffled.

**What it was for.** A new idea needs somewhere to sit. "What is the story with this lesson?" was your question on 28 September.

**How it goes wrong.** The opening gets done for its own sake, and the story becomes a wrapper around the teaching. Slide 3 (the UK wood, "We're flying south") obeys the rule and teaches nothing. Today's geography rewrite added the limit "each step arrives with something worth knowing", but that limit lives only in geography. The same trap is open in every other subject, where a story can become a frame rather than a reason for the order.

**Suggested wording.** Add to the general rule:

> The start is there to make the first idea make sense, so it teaches something the next slide uses. If it could be removed and nothing would be lost, cut it. The story is the reason each part leads to the next. It is not a pretend trip, a character walking through or a line that only moves the class along.

**Daniel, 2 November:** Starting from children's lives and telling a story are good teaching decisions, usually worth making, and neither is required of every lesson. **Suggested wording above softened: the designer chooses them when they are the best way in.** **Agreed, 3 November:** both are choices, and the designer may find other ways in. What stays firm: children never arrive blind, and ideas never arrive from nowhere. Nothing built yet.

## 6. "Let's work the way botanists work"

**The rule now.** Added today in 4.2.335: say `let's work the way botanists work` or `let's do some science`, never `you are scientists`. Also: show how the conclusion was reached (`How do I know? Watch what I look at`).

**What it was for.** The limit was on *not* telling children they are scientists. The phrase was an example of a safer way to say it. Your aim was the real subject work, "Being a X".

**How it goes wrong.** The example became a script line within hours.

- Slide 13 prints "Geographers use the weather and the plants to work out what biome a place is." You called it random and abstract.
- Its notes say "Let's work the way geographers work", almost word for word from the rule.
- Slide 18: "Let's do what geographers do".
- Lesson 3: "Geographers use what's on the map" and "How do we know? Watch what I look at".

**Suggested wording.**

> The subject shows in what children do, not in a line announcing it. You rarely need to name the subject's people at all. Say what we are doing and why (`We'll use the weather and the plants to decide`). Never tell children they are scientists. When a teacher shows how they reached something, show it on this case, in words that fit it.

**Daniel, 2 November:** **My diagnosis was wrong.** Slide 13 was random because of where it sat and what came before it, not because of the line about geographers. He disagrees with "never say you are scientists": "we're being scientists in that lesson, so we're scientists". Geographers can be named, and it just should not appear every time. Where the rule came from: the research report behind "Being a X", which took it from studies of mostly 4 to 6 year olds and says its own wording is an inference and untested. **Suggested wording above withdrawn; the ban to come out.** **Agreed, 3 November:** the ban comes out. Children work as the subject's people do, and the designer may say so (`as geographers`, `you're being scientists today`) whenever it helps, never as a stock line. The doing matters more than the saying. The cautions about sensitive professional roles and pretend discoveries stay. Nothing built yet.

## 7. Word cards go straight before the first slide showing the word

**The rule now.** A card sits straight before the first slide that prints the word, and the check enforces it. A card "is not where a word is taught". A word needing real teaching also gets a Teach, but nothing says which words need that.

**What it was for.** Your 28 September ruling: "it's before slides with that word". Words read out at the start were not helping.

**How it goes wrong.** The position is checked and the teaching is not, so the card ends up doing the teaching. The Equator card moved to slide 4 to satisfy the check. Its best teaching ("nobody has painted it on the ground") sits in the card's notes, and no Teach says what or where the Equator is, which is what you asked for on slides 5 and 6. Biome is the same: a card, then one slide. It also pulls against "the subject word comes last".

**Suggested wording.**

> Ask of each word: will children have to use this to do a task or explain something? If yes, it gets real teaching on a Teach slide, and the card only introduces it. A card never holds the only teaching of a word the lesson turns on. Where the word's meaning can be shown first, the Teach and the word can come together, and the card follows where that reads best.

**Daniel, 2 November:** Cards before the slide showing the word stay. A card is "we're going to use this word today, let's get familiar with it", and the next slides teach it. Words are chosen at the start: what children should know and use. Never open a slide with "these are two biomes" and define biome afterwards. **My line letting a card follow the teaching is withdrawn.** **Agreed, 3 November:** the card stays straight before the first slide using the word, and the slides after it teach any word children need to understand or use. A slide never uses a topic word before the class has met it. When words are chosen does not matter to him as long as they are valuable for the lesson, the learning and the children, so that timing stays as it is. Nothing built yet.

## 8. "Name the wrong answer, and put it in the question"

**The rule now.** Before wording a Do on a case, write the wrong answer and put it into the question, as a named child or an option.

**What it was for.** A real fault on 7 October: 8 of 12 practice tasks had the sentence just taught as their right answer.

**How it goes wrong.** This is the one that pulls hardest against today's ruling. Today's rule describes "the taught rule, a new case, one right answer, a tempting wrong one" as a test item. But the lesson designer's own steps still walk it through writing exactly that. One reference file limits the step to questions that check understanding, and the lesson designer's own instructions do not say so. About one Do in ten is now a named child's claim. A named child stays a good task, and Abdi was fine. He was too wordy, with nothing to help anyone below greater depth, and that comes from writing the test item before asking what the child gets better at.

**Suggested wording.**

> First decide what the child gets better at. Only when a Do's job is to find out who has understood do you name the wrong answer first. A named child saying something wrong is one good way to do that, not the default.

**Daniel, 2 November:** Naming a wrong answer is a good move and one among several. A named child giving a claim makes the class think. **My "only when a Do's job is to find out" was another blanket rule, so it is withdrawn.** **Agreed, 3 November:** the designer first decides what the Do makes the child better at, then chooses the best task. A named child's claim, right or wrong, is one good option among many, short, believable and with a way in for the child who understands least. Naming the likely wrong answer stays useful as planning for the teacher. The check against saying back the taught sentence stays. Nothing built yet.

## 9. "Most Teach beats earn a key question"

**The rule now.** "Most Teach beats earn one." Every Teach must also name what children are thinking.

**What it was for.** Four decks in one week had no questions during teaching, so the class just waited.

**How it goes wrong.** "Most" now means nearly all: 70 of 73 Teach beats in saved lessons carry one. A question gets added whether or not it helps, and it can take the place of explaining. On slide 16, the one you liked, it filled the slide with "Find where we landed... Follow the Equator west", the directions you found hard to follow. On slide 9 the question is nearly all the teaching there is.

**Suggested wording.**

> A key question earns its place when it makes children look at something or work something out that the explanation then uses. Plenty of good teaching is simply explaining well. Leave the question out when it would only interrupt, and never let it stand where the explanation should be.

**Daniel, 2 November:** Questioning draws out experience, ideas and misconceptions, and shows the teacher who is with them. It is generally good, and without it the teacher is only telling. Not every Teach needs one. The designer should never think "I've got to get a question in there somewhere": if it's right do it, if not, don't. **Agreed, 3 November:** "Most Teach beats earn one" comes out. Questioning is described as a usual part of good teaching, for drawing things out of children and seeing who is following. The designer asks what this question would do here, and it never stands in for the explanation. Nothing built yet.

## 10. The case goes on a short slide of its own before the model

**The rule now.** Before a final task, one line sets the case: who it is, what happened, what they are being asked.

**What it was for.** Your question on 18 September: "shouldnt we show sam first and his situation?"

**How it goes wrong.** When a task has no real case, the designer invents one to fill the space. Slide 20 reads "Someone has never heard of the Amazon. What would you tell them?" It follows the shape, and it has no link from Abdi, as you said.

**Suggested wording.**

> This line joins what the class has just done to the task they are about to do. Use a real case when the task has one. When it has none, say what they now know and what they will do with it. Never invent someone to fill the line.

**Daniel, 2 November:** A good way of doing it. If the designer has to invent something to fill the space, it is the wrong thing. Sam worked because the lesson dived in and setting the scene made sense at that moment. **Agreed, 3 November:** the designer asks what the class needs to start this task: a real case, a reminder of what they found out, or nothing if the task is clear. It never invents a person or situation to fill the line. A final writing task is still shown with a good example. Nothing built yet.
