# Who lives in the rainforest? (Year 4 Geography, lesson 3 of "Why are rainforests important to us?")

**LO:** To describe how Indigenous peoples use the Amazon rainforest

## The journey

the rainforest's layers, and a village in the middle of them → who Indigenous peoples are, and that they live in many different ways → what would you need if this village were your home? → the river: water, fish and the only road, so villages sit beside rivers → where would a family build a new home, and what would be hard at the other place? → the forest: homes, food and medicine → how do Indigenous peoples use the Amazon rainforest, shown on a new village?

## The retelling (the child who understands least, at home that evening)

> Lots of people live in the Amazon rainforest. Indigenous peoples are the people whose families were there first, more than 10,000 years ago, and they still live there today, in lots of different ways. Many of them build their villages right next to a river, because the river gives them water to drink, fish to eat, and it's like a road for their canoes. They use the forest too. They build their homes from wood and big leaves, they collect nuts and grow food in little gardens, and they make medicine from plants.

New things this asks that child to hold: Indigenous peoples, more than 10,000 years, many groups living in different ways (villages, towns, a city), the river for water and fish, the river as the road, villages beside rivers, homes from wood and palm leaves, nuts and small gardens, medicine from plants. Nine things, all in one story about one kind of place: a village in the rainforest and what is around it. The Amazon, South America and the layers are retrieved, not new.

## The telling (the lesson with no slides)

> Let's remember the rainforest from last lesson. Here it is, cut through from the top of the tallest trees down to the ground. Name each layer, from the top down. And which continent is the Amazon rainforest in?
> (Children name the four layers and the continent. We check them.)
>
> Last lesson we went up through those layers: the giant emergent trees poking out of the top, the thick canopy, the dark understorey and the forest floor. Lots of animals live up there. But here's something you might not expect. People live in the Amazon rainforest too. Look at this village. It's in a clear space in the forest, right beside a river. The people who live here are Indigenous people. Indigenous peoples are the groups of people whose families were the first to live in a place, long before anyone else arrived. In the Amazon, that's more than 10,000 years ago. And they still live there today. There are hundreds of different Indigenous groups in the Amazon, and they don't all live the same way. Some live in villages like this one. Some live in towns, or even in Manaus, a big city right in the middle of the forest, where about two million people live. So there isn't one way of living as an Indigenous person in the Amazon.
> (Pairs imagine their family lives in this village, a long boat ride from the nearest shop. What would they need every day, and where around them could they get it? The class's list goes on the board: water, food, a home, a way to get about, medicine.)
>
> Lots of you said water first. Look where this village is: right on the bank of the river. Why do you think they built it right here? Most Indigenous villages in the Amazon are next to a river, and here's why. The river gives them water to drink, to cook with and to wash in. It's full of fish to catch and eat; the Amazon has more kinds of fish than any other river in the world. And there's something else. There are no roads through most of the forest, because the trees grow too thick. So the river is their road. People paddle canoes, long thin boats, or drive small boats, to go fishing, to visit other villages and to reach the towns. So many Indigenous villages are built next to rivers, because the river gives them water, fish and a way to travel.
> (Pairs look at a photograph of the forest from above, with a river and two places marked, A on the river bank and B deep in the trees. Which place would be better for a family's new home? What would be hard for them at the other place?)
>
> The river isn't everything they need, though. You said a home, food and medicine too, and those come from the forest. Look at this house. The posts are tree trunks, and the roof is big palm leaves woven together, so the rain runs off. Everything in it came from the forest around it. Food: remember the emergent layer from our starter? Brazil nut trees grow right up into it. Their hard round pods fall all the way down to the forest floor, and people collect them and crack them open for the nuts inside. Families also clear a small patch of forest to make a garden, and they grow food there, like cassava, a root a bit like a potato. And medicine: when someone is ill, there's no chemist down the road. Over thousands of years, Indigenous peoples have learned which plants help, and they make medicine from them. So the forest gives Indigenous peoples their homes, much of their food and their medicine.
> (Here's a different village, beside another river. Children see a good description of how its people use the rainforest beside a weak one, tell a partner one thing the village gets from the river and one from the forest, then write: How do Indigenous peoples use the Amazon rainforest?)

Read back as the child who understands least: the new word is said with its meaning in the sentence that brings it in (Indigenous peoples, on the village they are looking at). `Community` was in the first draft and is gone: `village` says it in a word the class already has. Canoe gets its few words (long thin boats). Cassava gets its few words (a root a bit like a potato). The surprising bit a child needs to picture, that there are no roads, is said with its because (the trees grow too thick). The telling stays on its lines: no cutting down of the forest, no mining and no threats (lesson 4), no detail on the layers beyond the one Brazil nut link.

Amount and feel: one story, one kind of place, moving outward from the village to the river and then the forest around it. Three short stretches of teaching. The first is short (who lives here) and its quick Do builds the list the other two answer. The second ends on a map task in pairs at tables or on the carpet, which is the lesson's real break from listening. The third is the longest telling (three things, each with its own picture) and runs straight into the final task, which is its use. Children stop listening and do something four times: the starter, the list, the map task and the writing.

## Teacher orientation

Teacher orientation: Children find out who lives in the Amazon rainforest and how Indigenous peoples use the river and the forest around them, then write a description of how a new village uses the rainforest, using its photograph. The tricky move is why villages sit beside rivers: the river is their water, their fish and their only road, because there are no roads through the thick forest. It is taught in "Why build a village by the river?" and tested straight after in "Where would you build a new home?", where B, deep in the trees, pulls children who think the river is dangerous. From the slides: the map task runs on the board, with pairs saying A or B and what would be hard at the other place. To print: the photograph with A and B, one between two, and the worksheet, which is the final task printed and replaces it.

## The slides, in order

### Starter: "Rainforest layers and the Amazon"

- **On the board:** the rainforest cross-section with the four layers drawn and their names left blank. (1) `Name each layer of the rainforest, from the top down.` (2) `Which continent is the Amazon rainforest in?`
- **Thought:** Which layer sits where, from the tallest trees to the ground?
- **What happens:** children name the four layers and the continent; answers on the next slide. The script points out the emergent layer, because the lesson comes back to it.
- **Land:** emergent layer, canopy, understorey, forest floor; South America.
- **Why here:** the layers are the forest the village sits in, and the emergent layer comes back with the Brazil nuts in the forest Teach.

### Vocabulary: Indigenous peoples

`Indigenous peoples are the groups of people whose families were the first to live in a place, long before anyone else arrived.` Picture: Indigenous people in their own village today. Shown straight before the Teach whose first board uses it.

### Teach 1: "Who lives in the Amazon rainforest?"

- **On the board:** the village photograph. Headline: `People live in the Amazon rainforest too, and they have for more than 10,000 years.` Then: `The people in this village are one of the Amazon's Indigenous peoples. Their families were here first, long before anyone else arrived.` Then, with the photograph of Manaus: `There are hundreds of different Indigenous groups in the Amazon, and they don't all live the same way. Some live in villages like this one. Some live in towns, or in Manaus, a big city in the forest.` Key question: `What can you see that tells you people live here?`
- **Thought:** Who were the first people to live in this forest, and do they all live the same way?
- **What happens:** the script links from the layers to the people, tells who Indigenous peoples are on the village in front of them, then shows the city so the class does not leave thinking everyone lives in huts by a river.
- **Land:** people live in the rainforest, and Indigenous peoples have been there for more than 10,000 years, living in many different ways.
- **Why here:** the class now has the village and the people it belongs to, which the list of needs is built on.

### Do 1: "What would you need if you lived here?" (quick)

- **On the board:** the village photograph. `Imagine your family lives in this village. The nearest shop is a long boat ride away.` / `What would you need every day?` / `Where around you could you get it?`
- **Thought:** With no shop nearby, what would this family need every day, and where around them could it come from?
- **What happens:** pairs list their needs and point at where in the photograph each could come from. The teacher writes the class's list on the board in the children's words. This asks before teaching on purpose: the next two Teaches go down this list.
- **Levels:** board only: pairs say their list and the teacher writes the class's list, so a sheet would only reprint the photograph.
- **Look for:** water, food, a home, a way to get about, medicine when someone is ill. The answer that tells you a child is still picturing their own street: "We'd go to the shop", or "a car".
- **Why here:** the class's list is what the river Teach and the forest Teach answer, item by item.
- **Develops:** reading a photograph of a place for what it could give the people who live there, the link between people and place the whole lesson describes.

### Teach 2: "Why build a village by the river?"

- **On the board:** the village photograph again. Headline: `Look where this village is: right on the bank of the river.` Key question: `Why do you think they built their village right here?` Then, with a photograph of people fishing: `The river gives them water to drink, to cook with and to wash in.` / `It's full of fish to catch and eat.` Then, with a photograph of a canoe on the river: `There are no roads through most of the forest, because the trees grow too thick. So the river is their road, for canoes and small boats.` Star line: `Many Indigenous villages are built next to rivers, because the river gives them water, fish and a way to travel.` (The most-kinds-of-fish fact and where the boats go are said in the script.)
- **Thought:** Why would a family choose to live right next to the river?
- **What happens:** the class answers the key question from their own list first (water), then the teacher gives the two things they rarely think of: the fish, and the river as the road.
- **Land:** the star line.
- **Why here:** the three things the river gives are what children use next to judge two places on a photograph they have not seen, and what the final description says about the river.

### Do 2: "Where would you build a new home?"

- **On the board:** a photograph of the rainforest from above with a river winding through it, and two places marked: A on the bank of the river, B deep in the forest a long way from it. `Geographers work out why people live in one place and not another. This photograph shows the forest from above. A family wants to build a new home here.` / `Which place would be better for their home, A or B?` / `What would be hard for them at the other place?`
- **Thought:** What would this family be missing if they lived away from the river?
- **What happens:** pairs choose a place, then work out what would be hard at the other one. On a printed copy they ring their place and write the hard things beside the other.
- **Levels:** from the slides: pairs say A or B and what would be hard at the other place. To print: the photograph with A and B, one between two, to ring a place and write beside the other.
- **Look for:** A, with at B: carrying water a long way, no fish nearby, no river to travel on. The tempting wrong answer is B, "because the river is dangerous" or "because there are more trees for building", which leaves the river as water, food and road out.
- **Why here:** children have used the river's three jobs on a new place, which the final description needs when it says why the village is by the river.
- **Develops:** geography's why here and not there: weighing two places for what the river gives a community, on a photograph the lesson has not shown.

### Teach 3: "What does the forest give them?"

- **On the board:** Headline: `The forest gives Indigenous peoples their homes, much of their food and their medicine.` Then three things, each with its own photograph: (homes, the house photograph) `Look at this house. The posts are tree trunks, and the roof is made of big palm leaves woven together, so the rain runs off.`; (food, the Brazil nut pod) `Brazil nut trees grow right up into the emergent layer. Their hard pods fall to the forest floor, and people collect the nuts inside.`, with the small gardens and cassava said in the script; (medicine, the forest plants) `When someone is ill, there's no chemist nearby. Over thousands of years, Indigenous peoples have learned which plants help, and they make medicine from them.` Key question: `What is this house made from?`
- **Thought:** Where in the forest does each thing this family needs come from?
- **What happens:** the script goes down the rest of the class's list (a home, food, medicine) and ticks each one off. The Brazil nut line reaches back to the starter's emergent layer.
- **Land:** the headline.
- **Why here:** the forest's three jobs are the second half of the final description.

### Practise launch and task: "How do Indigenous peoples use the Amazon rainforest?"

- **On the board (launch):** `Here's a different village, beside another river in the Amazon.` with its photograph. Two descriptions side by side. Strong: `Indigenous peoples like the people in this village live next to the river. It gives them water to drink and fish to eat, and they travel along it by canoe. I can see the river right next to their houses.` / `They use the forest too. They build their homes from wood and leaves, and they make medicine from plants.` Weak: `They live in the rainforest. They have houses and a river. They eat food.` The difference: `The strong one says what they get from the river and the forest, and what they use it for.`
- **On the board (task):** the question, the photograph of the new village, and the success criteria (say what Indigenous peoples get from the river, and what they use it for; say what they get from the forest, and what they use it for; say what you can see in the photo that shows it), with the words river, forest, because, so.
- **Thought:** What does this village get from the river and from the forest, and what do they use each thing for?
- **What happens:** both descriptions come off the board. Each child tells a partner one thing the village gets from the river and one from the forest, and the partner asks "What do they use it for?" Then they write. Twelve minutes: two descriptions read together, a minute of partner talk, then about seven minutes of writing.
- **Levels:** the worksheet is this task printed (the photograph and lines) and replaces it; from the slides, children write from the photograph and the steps on the board.
- **Look for:** each thing linked to what it is for. If a child writes "They have a river", ask what the river gives them.
- **Why here:** the final task, drawing together who Indigenous peoples are, the river and the forest, on a village the lesson has not shown.

## Read-back

By the end, children will describe how Indigenous peoples use the Amazon rainforest, the river for water, fish and travel and the forest for homes, food and medicine, because the lesson gives them the knowledge that these communities get what they need from the river and forest around them, and why that puts their villages beside rivers, evidenced by the two-places task (where B, deep in the trees, catches a child who has missed the river's jobs) and their own written description of a new village.

Checked from the other end: a child who missed the river Teach chooses B, or chooses A and cannot say what would be hard at B. A child who missed the teaching writes "They have houses and a river", which the weak model and the criteria show is not yet a description.

## Decisions the walk-through does not show

- **The learning:** Indigenous peoples are the groups whose families were first to live in the Amazon, more than 10,000 years ago, and they live there today in many different ways; many communities live beside rivers because the river gives them water, fish and the only road; the forest gives them homes, food (nuts, gardens) and medicine. This is factual and descriptive knowledge with one relationship inside it (river → why villages are there), taught on one well-developed case, so `concepts` is empty.
- **Objective and boundary:** describe how Indigenous peoples use the rainforest means say what they use from the river and the forest and what each is for, and say why they live beside rivers. Today stays out of how the rainforest is changing (lesson 4). The layers are only retrieved and used once (Brazil nuts).
- **Prior knowledge:** lesson 1 (the Amazon, South America, biome) and lesson 2 (the four layers) are retrieved in the starter and the layers are used once more. Nothing about Indigenous peoples is assumed.
- **Structure:** content-based, because the learning is knowledge about a place and its people with a relationship to understand. The nearest alternative, a dialogic lesson on how people feel about the forest, would ask for views before the class knows anything about how the forest is used.
- **Sticking point:** "you need roads and shops to get what you need, so a home deep in the trees is as good as one by the river, or safer". Named in the river Teach (no roads, so the river is the road), tested in "Where would you build a new home?" with B pulling, met again in the final description's river step. The second misconception, "Indigenous peoples all live the same old way, or lived long ago", gets one touch: the city photograph and the line that they live in many different ways, in the first Teach.
- **Independent evidence:** the two-places task cannot be passed by remembering "villages are by rivers" alone, because it asks what would be hard at the other place, which needs the river's three jobs. The final description is written with a good one in mind (the model is shown first, then taken off the board), so it shows what each child can describe with a model in mind and a photograph to look at, not unaided form.
- **Success criteria:** three steps for the description, visible while children write and never on the sheet. The worksheet is the final task printed, at three levels, and replaces the slide task.
- **Representations and pictures:** the engine draws the rainforest cross-section for the starter, and the two places are marked on the aerial photograph by the labelled-diagram helper. Photographs (ten): the riverside village, Indigenous people in their village (the word card), Manaus, fishing, a canoe, the forest from above, a house with a palm-leaf roof, a Brazil nut pod, forest plants, and a second village for the final task. The two villages, the people and Manaus are real places and people named as evidence, so they are searched for as real and never made; the others are ordinary scenes that a faithful made picture could stand in for.
- **Lesson question:** None. The plan's title is a question, but the final task answers the objective's own question.
- **Amount:** three chunks of teaching. The first stays because it is short (who lives here and the word the objective needs) and its quick Do builds the list the other two answer; the third runs straight into the final task, which is its use. The river chunk closes with a task in pairs. Time: 42 planned minutes across the beats; taught by asking at the board's pace, about 46, so about 51 with books out and moving about. To bring it down, the community card went (village says it), the cassava card and its picture moved to the script, and the fish fact, where the boats go and part of the model description came off the boards. What is left is each because and the final writing, so it stays at 51 and the teacher is told.
- **Reasoning:** the two-places task is why here and not there on a new place.
- **Apply:** none. The final description already draws all three chunks together on a new village; an Apply would ask the same thing again.
- **Omissions:** the plan's comparison of the 2012 and 2019 maps, the talk about who is cutting the forest down and the closing look at further changes are left for lesson 4, which teaches how rainforests are changing. The 360 video is replaced by photographs. `deforestation` is not carded (lesson 4), and `community`, from the plan's list, is said as `village`.
- **Flags:** the omissions above, in one line; the honest running time; and a check that the starter's layer names match the words lesson 2 is being redesigned with.
