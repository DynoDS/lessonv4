3. Who lives in the rainforest? |  | LO: To describe how Indigenous peoples use the Amazon rainforest | The lesson opens with a presentation recapping the rainforest layers from the previous lesson. For the attention grabber, children look at photographs of buildings in the Amazon rainforest and guess which could be found there, discovering that people live in the rainforest, from small indigenous settlements to much larger cities. The teacher explains what indigenous means and tells children that around 35 per cent of the Amazon is home to indigenous peoples, some of whom have had no contact with the outside world and some of whose ancestors settled there over 10,000 years ago. Children watch part of a 360 video of an indigenous community and think about how they use the rainforest, for drinking water, transport and fishing, medicine, building homes and growing crops, then compare two maps of the Amazon from 2012 and 2019 to spot how much has been deforested, and discuss who is cutting the rainforest down and why. They build a class mind map on how indigenous peoples use the rainforest, then complete an activity recording indigenous communities using ideas from the mind map. The lesson ends by looking at a presentation of further changes happening in the Amazon and discussing how this might affect indigenous groups, setting up the next lesson. Children's work is kept for a presentation at the end of the unit. | Children may think indigenous peoples no longer live in the rainforest, or that everyone there lives in a city.
Children may see deforestation as something natural rather than realising it is caused by people cutting the forest down on purpose.
Children may describe a change to the rainforest without linking it to how it affects indigenous communities. | What does indigenous mean? (People living in an area whose ancestors were the first groups of people to live there.)
How do indigenous peoples use the rainforest? (They use trees for drinking water, rivers for transport and fishing, plants for medicine, trees and plants to build homes, and mineral rich soil for growing crops.)
How might indigenous communities feel about the Amazon rainforest? (Connected to nature; it is their home, as their ancestors lived there.)
How is the Amazon rainforest changing? (Trees are being cut down, companies are mining areas for valuable minerals such as gold, and animals and plants are losing their habitats.) | Indigenous peoples use the rainforest to ___.

4. How are rainforests changing? |  | LO: To explain why tropical rainforests are changing | The lesson recaps the previous one using a '3, 2, 1' presentation, then the teacher reads a story about the history of the indigenous peoples and their ownership of the land, asking children how it makes them feel and whether they think it's fact or fiction (it's fact). The teacher explains that the rainforest's soil holds valuable minerals such as quartz, iron ore, copper, lead, tin and silver, shown through a presentation on mineral distribution and, optionally, a pupil video about minerals. In the main event, children discuss why we need to look after the Amazon and write their top three reasons on sticky notes for a display, then watch part of the indigenous community video again to hear how change has affected their lives. A presentation recaps the threats to the rainforest, mining, deforestation, oil drilling, mass fishing and fires, and what could be done about them, backed up by a video on indigenous rights and forest protection. Children then plan and write a letter to the President of Brazil about the importance of the Amazon, the threats it faces and what could be done to help, using a letter plan to turn ideas into full sentences. Some children read their letters aloud at the end, and the work is kept for an end of unit presentation; during the week they watch part of the Mike Leach video about why climate and environment matter. | Children may list threats to the rainforest without linking them to a specific cause, for example saying trees are dying rather than naming logging or fires.

(It is home to indigenous peoples, contains many different types of plants and animals, absorbs carbon dioxide and releases oxygen, adds water to our atmosphere, medicine is made from the plants, and the soil has many valuable minerals.)
What are businesses and outside groups doing to the Amazon? (Mining precious minerals, deforestation/logging, oil drilling, mass fishing and fires to clear spaces.)
What harm is being done to the Amazon rainforest? (Losing 5,000 species of plant and animal life a year; fewer trees and plants for building and medicines, polluted rivers and fish, less space for settlements.)

How is our local woodland used?: Data collection |  | LO: To collect data about how our local woodland is used | This is a fieldwork lesson, so it starts well before the day with risk assessing the route to a local woodland or park. In class, the teacher recaps the previous lesson with a 'Gimme five' presentation, then asks what fieldwork is and what tools can be used to collect information from people or places, such as tally charts, recordings, drawings or surveys. Using a presentation, the teacher models each data collection method the children will use: sketching, a questionnaire (including how to introduce themselves to the adults they'll ask) and mapping their route. In pre-arranged groups with a supervising adult, children are given a clipboard, paper for sketches, a tally chart, a questionnaire and a route map. Before setting off, the teacher shows the walking route on a map and asks children to spot possible risks, such as crossing roads or walking near water, and how to avoid them. Children then walk to the woodland or park and, working in their groups, complete as many questionnaires as possible, record how people are using the area on their tally chart, sketch what they see, and map their route, staying within set boundaries and with their adult throughout. Back in the classroom, they discuss what they found out and why the woodland matters to people, and the data is kept safe for analysis in the next lesson. | Children may only manage one or two questionnaire responses rather than gathering enough data to be reliable.
Children may think a risk has to be something scary, rather than something ordinary that could realistically happen on the route, like crossing a road.
Children may struggle to work out where they are on the map without a clear landmark to match it to. | What is fieldwork? (The collection of information about an area.)
What can we use to collect information from people or places? (Various answers, e.g. a tally chart, recording, drawing, survey, etc.)
How can we stay safe on our route? (Various answers depending on the route, e.g. only cross the road with an adult, stay away from water, always stay with the group, etc.)
How is our local woodland used? (Children may give multiple answers, for example, people go there to exercise, relax and see friends.)
Why is the woodland important to people? (Encourage children to identify the underlying reasons, e.g. it is a large, freely accessible public area, people like being close to nature and animals, it offers a place of quiet and relaxation, etc.) | One risk on our route is ___, so we will ___.

enquiry: An investigation into a question.
method: A way of doing something.
questionnaire: A set of questions used to get information from people.
risk: A situation where something may be dangerous.
route: A way to get from one place to another, marked on a map. | It would recap with a 'Gimme five' presentation, then use an interactive presentation on woodland fieldwork to model each data collection method, and a fieldwork route presentation where the teacher can upload their own map of the walk. Groups of five to seven children, each with a supervising adult briefed in advance, would carry a clipboard, blank paper for sketches, a printed tally chart, questionnaire and route map (using maps from Digimaps, OS maps or a search engine, or hand drawn ones). A Google Maps link would be used in class to show the walking route before setting off.
6. How is our local woodland used?: Findings |  | LO: To analyse and present findings about how our local woodland is used | The lesson starts with an anagram presentation recapping key words, risk, route, questionnaire, from the fieldwork lesson. Children look back over the data they collected and tell a partner something they found out about how the woodland is used. Using a presentation, the teacher models turning their tally chart data into a bar chart, including choosing a sensible scale, and children make their own bar chart from their tally chart data. The teacher also models how to interpret the questionnaire results, and in groups children tally up how often and when people use the woodland and pick out quotes to use from the other questions. Groups build a display board titled with the enquiry question, 'How is our local woodland used?', sticking on their bar chart, cut out tally charts, annotated sketches of things like benches or play equipment, and their route map, then practise presenting their findings, with each child explaining a different part, ready to share with an audience such as parents, a local library or a councillor. To finish, children compare how people use the local woodland with how indigenous peoples use the Amazon rainforest, and how each group might feel about their forest, recording ideas on a mind map. There's an optional unit quiz to end the unit, and if children completed a knowledge catcher in lesson 1 they can revisit it and add to it in a different colour. During the week they watch part of the Mike Leach video about climate change. | Children may draw a bar chart with an uneven or unlabelled scale, making the data hard to read.
