# What is the Amazon rainforest like? (Year 4 Geography, lesson 2 of "Why are rainforests important to us?")

**LO:** To describe the layers of a tropical rainforest (biomes and vegetation)

## The journey

last lesson's Amazon: hot, rainy, giant trees packed close → why it's shady under a tree in the park → the rainforest's vegetation grows in layers → at the top, the leaves join into a roof, the canopy, and a few emergent giants poke out above it → which trees on this photo are emergent? → under the roof the light runs out: the understorey, then the dark forest floor → which layer does each living thing belong in? → a giant tree falls: what happens on the floor below the hole? → describe the canopy and the forest floor, and say why each is like that

## The retelling (the child who understands least, at home that evening)

> In a rainforest the plants grow in layers, like the floors of a building. At the top, a few giant trees poke out into the sun and the wind. Under them is the canopy, a thick roof of leaves where most of the animals live. The roof catches nearly all the sunshine, so underneath it's dim, and the forest floor at the bottom is so dark that hardly anything grows. If a big tree falls down, the light gets in and plants grow there.

New things this asks that child to hold: vegetation, the four layers (emergent, canopy, understorey, forest floor) and the one reason under all of them: the canopy catches nearly all the sunlight, so the lower you go, the darker it gets. Biome, the Amazon in South America and "hot and rainy all year" come back from last lesson in the starter. The Equator, which last lesson taught, is not asked for, because nothing today uses it. One place, one story: the sunlight coming down through the forest, from the top of the trees to the ground.

## The telling (the lesson with no slides)

> Let's get our rainforest brains working. Three questions from last lesson. What is a biome? Which continent is the Amazon rainforest in? And why can giant trees grow so close together there?
> (Children answer the three questions. We check them: a big area with its own kind of weather and the plants and animals that suit it; South America; because it's hot and rainy all year, so the trees get all the warmth and water they need.)
>
> Last lesson we found the Amazon on the map. Today we're going to find out what it's like inside it, from the very tops of the trees right down to the ground.
> Think about a hot, sunny day in the park. Where do people go and sit? Under a big tree. Why is it shady under there? The tree's leaves are in the way. They catch the sunlight before it can reach you.
> All the plants that grow in a place are called its vegetation. In a tropical rainforest, the vegetation grows in layers, one above another, like the floors of a very tall building. Let's start at the top.
> This photo was taken from above the Amazon. Remember those giant trees packed close together? Their leaves touch and join up into one thick roof of leaves, high above the ground. That roof is called the canopy. Most of the rainforest's animals live up here, monkeys, birds, sloths, because the sunshine is up here, so this is where the fruit and the flowers grow. Just like the tree in the park, the canopy catches the sunlight first.
> But the canopy isn't the very top. Look: a few trees are even taller, and they poke right out above the roof. These are the emergent trees. They get the most sunshine, and the strongest wind and the heaviest rain too, because there's nothing above them.
> (Each child looks at a new photo of the rainforest with three letters on it and decides which letters are on emergent trees and which is on the canopy.)
>
> So the canopy catches nearly all the sunlight. What do you think it's like underneath it?
> The canopy catches nearly all the sunlight, so the lower you go, the darker it gets. Just under the canopy is the understorey. It's dim and green down here, and warm and damp, with hardly any wind. Young trees and bushes grow here, and their leaves are huge, as big as an umbrella, to catch the little bit of light that gets through. At the very bottom is the forest floor, the ground of the rainforest. It's the darkest place of all, even at midday. Look at the arrows: only about 2 rays of sunlight in every 100 get all the way down. So hardly any plants can grow on the forest floor. It's covered in dead leaves that have fallen from above. So a rainforest isn't the same all the way down. Each layer gets a different amount of light, and that's why different things grow in each one.
> (Pairs sort five rainforest living things, each with one fact about it, into the layer where it lives, then say why they put one of them there.)
>
> Sometimes a giant tree gets old and crashes down. When it falls, it leaves a big hole in the canopy. What do you think will happen on the forest floor, under that hole? Why?
> (Each child decides and gives a reason. Then the answer: sunlight reaches the forest floor there for the first time in years, so seeds that were waiting in the dark start to grow, and young trees race upwards to fill the hole.)
>
> Now you're going to describe two of the layers yourselves: the canopy and the forest floor. Here's a good description beside one that isn't so good. What does the good one do that the other one doesn't? It says why each layer is like that. (The models come off.) Tell your partner about all four layers, from the top down. Then write about the canopy and the forest floor: what it's like there, and why.
> (Children say it to a partner, are asked "Why is it like that?", then write.)

## Teacher orientation

Teacher orientation: Children find out that a tropical rainforest's vegetation grows in four layers, from the emergent trees at the top, through the canopy and the understorey, down to the forest floor, and that the canopy catches nearly all the sunlight, so the lower you go, the darker it gets. They spot emergent trees on a photo, sort five rainforest living things into the layers, predict what happens when a giant tree falls, then write a description of the canopy and the forest floor. The tricky move is the reason: the forest floor is dark because the canopy catches the light first, not just because it is at the bottom. It is taught in "What is it like under the canopy?" and used again in "What happens under the hole?". From the slides: the sort runs on the board, with pairs writing each living thing under its layer. To print: the sort sheet, one between two, and the worksheet, which is the final task printed and replaces it.

## The slides

### Starter: "Last lesson's rainforest"

- **On the board:** three short questions. What is a biome? Which continent is the Amazon rainforest in? Why can giant trees grow so close together there?
- **The thought:** what do I remember about the Amazon from last lesson?
- **What happens:** children answer all three, then the answer slide checks them. The script says the last answer out loud, because the giant trees packed close together are where today starts.
- **What you land:** hot and rainy all year, so the trees get all the warmth and water they need, so they grow huge and close together.
- **Why it is here:** the giant trees packed close together are the trees whose leaves join into the canopy in the first Teach. Four minutes.

### Word cards: vegetation, canopy, emergent

Before the first Teach, whose board says all three. Vegetation is pictured by the photo of the Amazon from above; canopy and emergent by the layers diagram with that one layer picked out. The script reads each card and says the class is about to see what each one means.

### "What's at the top of the rainforest?" (Teach)

- **On the board:** a photo of a big tree in a park on a sunny day, with its shade on the grass. `On a sunny day, it's shady under a big tree.` Key question: `Why is it shady under the tree?` Then `Its leaves stop the sunlight reaching you.` / `In a rainforest, the vegetation, all the plants, grows in layers, like the floors of a tall building.` Then the photo of a rainforest from above: `At the top, the trees grow so close that their leaves join up into a thick roof, the canopy. Most animals live up here, where the fruit grows in the sun.` / `A few even taller trees poke out above the canopy. These are the emergent trees.` The point lands last: `Like the tree in the park, the canopy catches the sunlight first.` The script adds that the emergent trees get the most sun, wind and rain, because nothing is above them.
- **The thought:** why would it be shady under all those leaves, and what's sticking out above them?
- **What happens:** the familiar shade comes first, the class says why, and the same idea is carried up into the rainforest. Six minutes over two or three slides: the park, then the roof, then the emergent trees.
- **What you land:** the canopy is a roof of leaves that catches the sunlight first, and the emergent trees poke out above it.
- **Why it is here:** they can tell the canopy from the emergent trees above it, which the photo task needs straight away, and they hold "leaves catch the light" for the next Teach.

### "Which trees poke out of the canopy?" (Do, quick)

- **On the board:** a new photo of a rainforest from above, with three letters on it: A on an emergent crown, B on the canopy, C on a second emergent crown. `Look at this new rainforest. Which letters are on emergent trees, and which are on the canopy?` with `Geographers can tell the layers apart from above. Find the roof of touching treetops first, then look for any tree sticking up out of it.` It is practice of telling the two apart on a fresh picture, not a test of a new idea.
- **The thought:** is this tree part of the roof, or sticking up out of it?
- **What happens:** each child decides for all three letters, then we hear the reasons.
- **Levels:** board only: the answer is three letters, so a sheet would only reprint the photo.
- **What you look for:** A and C are emergent, B is the canopy. A child who calls the tallest crown the canopy thinks the canopy is the very top; point at the roof and ask what is sticking out of it.
- **Why it is here:** the top two layers are told apart on a picture the lesson did not show, so the canopy is secure as the roof before the next Teach explains what that roof does to the light. Three minutes.

### Word card: understorey

Straight before the next Teach. Pictured by the layers diagram with the understorey picked out. The definition says where it is and what grows there, not what it's like, so it doesn't answer the Teach's opening question.

### "What is it like under the canopy?" (Teach)

- **On the board:** opening question first, `What do you think it's like under the canopy?` Then the layers diagram with the sun and its arrows thinning band by band, the bottom two layers at full strength, and its own note `about 2 rays in every 100` on the forest floor. Headline: `The canopy catches nearly all the sunlight, so the lower you go, the darker it gets.` Then the understorey photo: `Under the canopy is the understorey. Young trees grow here in dim light, with huge leaves to catch what light gets through.` Then the forest floor photo: `At the very bottom is the forest floor. It's the darkest place of all, even at midday.` / `So hardly any plants grow there. The ground is covered in dead leaves.` / `A rainforest isn't the same all the way down. Each layer gets a different amount of light.`
- **They see:** sunlight coming down through the layers, with fewer and fewer rays getting through each one, until only about 2 rays in every 100 reach the forest floor (the layers diagram with its light arrows).
- **The thought:** if the roof catches the light, what is left for the layers below?
- **What happens:** the class guesses first from what they now know about the roof, then the diagram shows the light running out, layer by layer. Six minutes over two or three slides.
- **What you land:** the sticky fact, said once as the headline.
- **Why it is here:** they can say how much light each lower layer gets and what grows there because of it, which the sort and the fallen-tree prediction both need.

### "Which layer does each one live in?" (Do, task)

- **On the board:** the labelled layers diagram, and five cards with a picture and one fact each. Kapok tree: `It grows much taller than the trees around it.` Harpy eagle: `It perches on the highest branches in the forest.` Howler monkey: `It lives high up in thick leaves, eating fruit.` Young tree: `It grows slowly in dim light, with leaves as big as an umbrella.` Fungus: `It grows on rotting leaves and needs no sunlight.` Task: `Which layer does each one live in? Choose one and say why it lives there.`, set with `Geographers describe a rainforest by what lives in each layer. Read what each living thing needs, then put it under the layer where it lives.` The star fact sits on this slide as the reference children sort by: it is the rule they apply, not the answer to any card.
- **The thought:** what does this living thing need, and which layer has it?
- **What happens:** pairs place all five, then each pair says why for one. Seven minutes: five cards to read and decide, then the hardest two talked through.
- **Levels:** from the slides, pairs write each name under its layer; printed, the sort sheet one between two, with a box under each layer for the letters. Nothing to collect.
- **What you look for:** the harpy eagle in the canopy (the canopy is the top), and the young tree on the forest floor (it's dark down there, so it must be the bottom). Both are put right by asking what each layer is like.
- **Why it is here:** they have used what each layer is like to place living things the lesson never showed, which the final description uses when it says what the vegetation is like and why.

### "What happens when a giant tree falls?" (Teach, short)

- **On the board:** a photo of a big tree lying fallen on the rainforest floor, with light coming down through the gap it left. `Sometimes a giant rainforest tree gets old and crashes down.` / `When it falls, it leaves a big hole in the canopy, like a hole in a roof.` / `This happens all the time in a rainforest.`
- **The thought:** what were that tree's leaves doing for the ground underneath before it fell?
- **What happens:** the case is set in a minute or two, with the photo, so the prediction that follows has something real to work on. It brings in one plain fact (giant trees fall and leave holes) and no new idea.
- **Why it is here:** it puts the hole in the canopy in front of the class for the prediction straight after.

### "What happens under the hole?" (Do, quick)

- **On the board:** the same fallen-tree photo. `What will happen on the forest floor under the hole? Why?`
- **The thought:** with no roof above it now, what does the ground get, and what will that do?
- **What happens:** each child decides and gives a reason, then the answer slide says what happens: `Plants will grow there. Sunlight can now reach the forest floor through the hole, so seeds start to grow and young trees race up to fill the gap.`
- **Levels:** board only: a prediction and its reason, said aloud.
- **What you look for:** "nothing will grow, because nothing grows on the forest floor" is the half-listener's answer; the reason that settles it is that sunlight can now reach the ground.
- **Why it is here:** they have used the reason (the canopy catches the light) on a changed case, which is the because the final description has to give for the forest floor. Three minutes.

### "What is it like in the canopy and on the forest floor?" (Practise, with launch)

- **Launch:** a strong description of this very task beside a weak one. Strong: `The canopy gets lots of sunlight because it is near the top. So the vegetation is thick and green, and most animals live there.` / `The forest floor gets hardly any sunlight because the canopy catches it first. So the vegetation is thin, with only a few small plants.` Weak: `The canopy is green and has lots of animals. The forest floor is dark and has dead leaves.` Difference: `The strong one says how much sunlight each layer gets, and why.` The model is the same task, as the teacher wants: the writing then shows what each child writes with a good one in mind. No separate answer slide follows, because the good one has already been on the board.
- **On the board while they write:** the question `What is it like in the canopy and on the forest floor, and why?`, the labelled layers diagram, the success criteria and the words `sunlight`, `vegetation`, `because`.
- **The thought:** how much light gets here, why, and what does that mean for what grows?
- **What happens:** the models come off. Each child tells a partner about all four layers from the top down, the partner asks "Why is it like that?", then each child writes about the canopy and the forest floor. Eleven minutes: about three for the two models, two for the partner talk, six to write two short descriptions.
- **Levels:** the worksheet is this task printed, and replaces it.
- **What you look for:** the because on the forest floor. A child who writes "it is dark because it is at the bottom" has the place and not the reason; ask what is above it that stops the light.
- **Why it is here:** the lesson's final description, the objective's own performance.

## Read-back

By the end, children will describe the four layers of a tropical rainforest, saying what the canopy and the forest floor are like and why, because the lesson gives them the four layers in order and the one reason under all of them, that the canopy catches nearly all the sunlight so each layer down is darker, replacing the idea that a rainforest is the same all the way through, evidenced by the fallen-tree prediction they make without a model and the written description of the canopy and the forest floor.

Read from the other end: a child who missed the light idea can say the forest floor is dark (they could guess that from "at the bottom") but cannot predict that plants will grow under the fallen tree's hole, and cannot give "because the canopy catches it first" as the reason.

## Closing decisions

- **The learning:** a rainforest's vegetation grows in four layers, emergent, canopy, understorey and forest floor, and each is different because the canopy catches nearly all the sunlight, so each layer down gets less light and different things grow there. It is factual learning (the names and the order) held together by one relationship (the light), developed as one well-developed case, so `concepts` is empty.
- **The objective and its boundary:** the objective is used as written. The board shows `To describe the layers of a tropical rainforest`, the objective's own opening words with the bracketed strand tag `(biomes and vegetation)` left off, because the tag names the curriculum strand rather than anything children do; `vegetation` is still taught and carded, and `biome` is retrieved in the starter. Plant adaptations (drip tips, buttress roots, lianas), vegetation belts and minerals are left for another lesson.
- **Prior knowledge and structure:** lesson 1 taught biome, the Equator, the Amazon in South America (mostly Brazil) and that the rainforest is hot and rainy all year so giant trees grow close together; the starter brings back the parts today uses, in those words, and its vocabulary (biome) is reused as written. Content-based, because describing a place's layers is a body of knowledge with a reason inside it; there is no procedure to model and no contested question to talk out.
- **Sticking point:** children think a rainforest is the same all the way through, hot, wet and full of plants right down to the ground. Exposed by the opening question of "What is it like under the canopy?", put right on that board, checked in the sort (the young tree, the fungus), and retested in "What happens when a giant tree falls?" and the final description. A second, smaller one: the canopy is the very top. Named where the canopy is taught and checked once on the fresh photo.
- **Independent evidence:** the fallen-tree prediction is answered with nothing on the board saying what happens; a child holding only "hardly anything grows on the forest floor" gets it wrong. The final description is written with a good one in mind, so it shows what each child can write after seeing a model, not unaided invention.
- **Success criteria and worksheet:** two steps, each with a sentence starter: say how much sunlight gets to the layer and why, then say what the vegetation is like there. The worksheet is the final task printed, with the labelled layers diagram, and it replaces the slide task.
- **Representations and pictures:** the engine's rainforest-layers diagram carries the light idea in its band tint and arrows; it first appears on a Teach board in "What is it like under the canopy?", where it shows the light running out, and is the reference for the sort and the final task. Photos show the park shade, the canopy from above, the understorey, the forest floor, the fresh photo with letters, the fallen tree and the five living things. Eleven pictures.
- **Awe:** the forest floor is nearly dark at midday, with only about 2 rays in every 100 getting down; and the race to fill the hole a fallen tree leaves.
- **Amount:** two chunks of teaching (the top two layers, then the bottom two and the light), one task (the sort), two quick Dos and the final task. The check counts three chunks, because the fallen tree has a short board of its own. It stays: that board is two minutes setting a real case (giant trees fall and leave a hole), it carries no new idea, and the prediction it sets up is the lesson's one unaided use of the reason the final description needs. Taught by asking about every line, the lesson comes to about 51 minutes with books out and moving to tables, a little over the usual 50, and the teacher is told so; the Equator question came out of the starter because nothing today uses it, and the boards were cut back to the sentences each one needs.
- **Reasoning:** the fallen-tree prediction (change one thing and say what follows) is the lesson's reasoning beat; the final description asks for the because.
- **Lesson question:** None. A describing lesson with no why-here question that one final task answers better than the plain objective.
- **Deliberate omissions:** Kapow's 360 degree video, the cut-and-stick rainforest diagram and the drag-and-drop paragraph; the sort and the photos do the looking, and the final description replaces the paragraph. Plant adaptations, vegetation belts and minerals are flagged for the teacher.
