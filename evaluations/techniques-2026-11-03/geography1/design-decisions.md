# Where in the world are tropical rainforests? (Year 4 Geography, lesson 1 of "Why are rainforests important to us?")

**LO:** To describe a biome and locate the Amazon rainforest

## The journey

our own weather and our own trees → the weather decides what can grow (a desert and a rainforest) → that kind of area is a biome → could a rainforest grow in our school field? → where all the rainforests are: along the Equator, and why → which of these places could have one? → the biggest, the Amazon, in South America → where is the Amazon and what is it like there?

## The retelling (the child who understands least, at home that evening)

> Every place has its own kind of weather, and the weather decides what plants can grow there. A big area like that is called a biome. A rainforest is a biome where it's hot and it rains nearly every day, so giant trees grow really close together. Rainforests are near the Equator, the line round the middle of the Earth, because the Sun is really strong there all year. The biggest one is the Amazon. It's in South America, and most of it is in Brazil.

New things this asks that child to hold: biome, tropical rainforest, the Equator, why it is hot and rainy there, the Amazon, Brazil. Desert and South America are met as familiar words (South America is retrieved in the starter). Six new things, one story moving outward from our park to one rainforest on the other side of the world.

## The telling (the lesson with no slides)

> Let's warm up our map brains. Here's the world. Five continents have a letter on them. What's A? B? Which one do we live in?
> (Children name the five lettered continents. We check them.)
>
> Think about the weather here in Britain. Summer is warm, winter is cold, and it rains on lots of days. Look at this park. Oak trees grow well here, and when winter comes they drop their leaves. Now look at these two places. This is a hot desert. It hardly ever rains there. Plants need water to grow, so only a few tough little plants can live. And this is a tropical rainforest. It's hot all year, every month, and it rains nearly every day. The plants have all the warmth and all the water they need, so huge trees grow packed close together and they stay green all year. Why can't a forest like this grow in the desert? So the weather in a place decides what plants can grow there. A big area of the world with its own kind of weather, and the plants and animals that suit it, is called a biome. The desert is one biome. The tropical rainforest is another.
> (Each child decides whether Ayaan is right: "Even if we planted rainforest trees in our school field, they wouldn't grow into a rainforest." They say why, using our weather.)
>
> So where in the world do rainforests grow? On this map the green shows where tropical rainforests grow. Here's a line round the middle of the Earth, called the Equator. What do you notice about where the green is? They're all close to the Equator. Here's why. Near the Equator, the Sun shines down strongly all year round, so it's hot every month. There's no cold winter. All that heat lifts lots of water up into the air, from the trees and the sea, and it falls again as heavy rain, nearly every day. Hot and wet all year is just what rainforest trees need. Further from the Equator, the Sun is lower in the sky and winters get cooler. So tropical rainforests grow near the Equator, where it is hot and rainy all year.
> (With a partner, children look at five places marked on a new world map with no rainforests shown. They decide which could have a tropical rainforest, and say why for one of them.)
>
> The biggest tropical rainforest in the world is the Amazon. Remember South America from our starter? That's where it is. Most of it is in Brazil, but it spreads into other countries too, like Peru and Colombia. The Equator runs right through it, so it's hot and rainy all year. A huge river, the Amazon River, winds through the middle of it. And it is enormous: more than 20 times bigger than the whole of the UK.
> (Children see a good description of the Amazon beside a weak one, say theirs to a partner, then write: Where is the Amazon rainforest, and what is it like there?)

Read back as the child who understands least: every new word is said with its meaning in the sentence that brings it in (biome after both places have been seen; the Equator as the line round the middle, with the map showing it). The why of the Equator is said plainly as Sun, heat, rain. The telling stays on its lines: no layers (lesson 2), no people (lesson 3), no cutting down (lesson 4), no list of other biomes.

Amount and feel: one story, outward from our park. Three short stretches of teaching, each about five minutes, each followed by children doing something (a claim to judge, a map task in pairs, then the final writing). The third stretch is short (a name and a place on a map the class already reads) and runs straight into the final task, which is its use. The longest listening stretch is the first Teach, and the claim about our school field breaks it.

## Teacher orientation

Teacher orientation: Children find out what a biome is by comparing a hot desert with a tropical rainforest, find out why rainforests grow near the Equator, then locate the Amazon rainforest and write a short description of where it is and what it is like there. The tricky move is the link between weather and plants: being near the Equator makes a place hot and rainy all year, and that is what lets the trees grow. It is taught in "Where do rainforests grow?" and tested on five new places in "Which places could have a tropical rainforest?" straight after. From the slides: the map task runs on the board, with pairs saying the letters they choose. To print: the map of five places, one between two, and the worksheet, which is the final task printed and replaces it.

## The slides, in order

### Starter: "Continents of the world"

- **On the board:** a world map with five continents lettered A to E. `Name each continent.` (1) A (2) B (3) C (4) D (5) E.
- **Thought:** Which continent is which shape, and where is it on the map?
- **What happens:** children name the five; answers on the next slide. The script asks which one we live in (Europe) and points at South America, because the lesson comes back to it.
- **Land:** A North America, B South America, C Europe, D Africa, E Asia.
- **Why here:** South America and Africa are needed again in the places task and for locating the Amazon outward from the continent.

### Vocabulary: biome

`A biome is a big area of the world with its own kind of weather, and the plants and animals that suit it.` Picture: the rainforest photograph the next Teach uses. Shown just before the Teach slide that first says the word.

### Teach 1: "What decides what can grow in a place?"

- **On the board:** `The weather in a place decides what plants can grow there.` Then, each with its own picture: `Here in Britain, summers are warm, winters are cold, and it rains on lots of days. Oak trees grow well here.` / `In a hot desert, it hardly ever rains, so only a few tough plants can grow.` / `In a tropical rainforest, it's hot and rainy all year, so huge trees grow close together and stay green.` / `A big area like this, with its own kind of weather and the plants and animals that suit it, is called a biome.` Key question: `Why can't a forest like this grow in the desert?`
- **Thought:** What is different about the weather in each place, and how does that change what grows?
- **What happens:** the script opens from our own weather, then the two contrasting places side by side, then the word biome.
- **Land:** the weather in a place decides what can grow there; a biome is a big area with its own weather and plants.
- **Why here:** the weather-to-plants link is what the school field claim uses next, and what the final description needs (the weather, then what grows because of it).

### Do 1: "Could a rainforest grow in our school field?" (quick)

- **On the board:** our park photograph beside the rainforest photograph. `Ayaan says: "Even if we planted rainforest trees in our school field, they wouldn't grow into a rainforest."` `Is Ayaan right? Explain your answer.`
- **Thought:** Is our weather what a rainforest needs?
- **What happens:** each child decides yes or no, then tells a partner why, using our weather. The teacher takes a few reasons.
- **Levels:** board only: each child says a verdict and a reason aloud, so a sheet would only reprint the claim.
- **Look for:** yes, Ayaan is right, because our winters are cold and a rainforest needs it hot all year and rain nearly every day. The wrong answer is "No, trees grow anywhere if you plant them and water them", which is the weather left out.
- **Why here:** children have used the weather to decide what grows on a place they know, which the places task then does on the world map.
- **Develops:** reading a place's weather to decide what can grow there, the reasoning a biome rests on, on their own place.

### Vocabulary: Equator

`The Equator is an imaginary line around the middle of the Earth, halfway between the North Pole and the South Pole.` Picture: a world map with the Equator drawn on it.

### Teach 2: "Where do rainforests grow?"

- **On the board:** `The green on this map shows where tropical rainforests grow.` with the world map, rainforests shaded green and the Equator drawn. Key question: `What do you notice about where they are?` Then, with the picture of the Sun's rays hitting the middle of the Earth straight on: `Near the Equator, the Sun shines strongly all year, so it's hot every month.` / `The heat lifts water up into the air, and it falls again as heavy rain, nearly every day.` / `Further from the Equator, the Sun's rays arrive at a slant, so it's cooler.` Star line: `Tropical rainforests grow near the Equator, where it is hot and rainy all year.`
- **They see:** the Sun's rays hitting the middle of the Earth straight on and spreading out at a slant towards the top and the bottom, so the middle gets the strongest sunshine.
- **Thought:** Why would being near the Equator make a place hot and wet all year?
- **What happens:** children read the pattern off the map first (they are all near the Equator), then the teacher explains why.
- **Land:** the star line.
- **Why here:** the Equator rule, with its reason, is what children use to judge five new places next, and what the final description uses to say why it is hot there.

### Do 2: "Which places could have a tropical rainforest?"

- **On the board:** a new world map with the Equator drawn and five places marked A to E, no rainforests shown (A in central Africa on the Equator, B in the Sahara Desert, C the UK, D on the island of Borneo on the Equator, E in the far south of South America). `Geographers can work out where a rainforest could grow from where a place is in the world. Look at the five places on the map.` / `Which of these places could have a tropical rainforest?` / `Explain one of your choices.`
- **Thought:** Is this place near the Equator, so is it hot and rainy all year?
- **What happens:** pairs decide for each of the five, then write or say the reason for one. The teacher goes through them.
- **Levels:** from the slides: pairs say the letters they chose. To print: the map with the five places, one between two, so pairs can ring the places on their own copy. Board version stands alone.
- **Look for:** A and D. B is the tempting wrong one ("it's in Africa, and Africa is hot"), and so is E ("the Amazon is in South America").
- **Why here:** children have used the Equator, not "hot" or "the right continent", to place a rainforest, which the final description needs when it locates the Amazon.
- **Develops:** using the Equator to judge where a rainforest could grow, geography's why here and not there, on places the lesson has not shown.

### Teach 3: "Where is the Amazon rainforest?"

- **On the board:** a map of northern South America with the countries named, the Amazon rainforest shaded and the Equator drawn, then a photograph of the Amazon from above. Headline: `The Amazon is the biggest tropical rainforest in the world.` Then: `It's in South America. Most of it is in Brazil, but it spreads into other countries too, like Peru and Colombia.` / `The Equator runs right through it, so it's hot and rainy all year.` / `The Amazon River winds through the middle. The forest is more than 20 times bigger than the whole of the UK.` Key question: `Which continent from our starter is it in?`
- **Thought:** How would you tell someone where the Amazon is, starting with the continent?
- **What happens:** the script links back to South America from the starter, then country, then the Equator; the size line is the moment to be struck by.
- **Land:** the Amazon's place, continent then country then the Equator.
- **Why here:** this is what the final description locates; the task straight after uses it.

### Practise launch and task: "Where is the Amazon rainforest, and what is it like there?"

- **On the board (launch):** two descriptions side by side. Strong: `The Amazon rainforest is a tropical rainforest biome. It is in South America, and most of it is in Brazil. The Equator runs right through it, so it is hot all year and it rains nearly every day. The plants get all the warmth and water they need, so huge trees grow close together.` Weak: `The Amazon is hot. It has lots of trees. It is in Brazil. It rains a lot.` The difference: `The strong one starts with the continent, and says how the weather makes the trees grow.`
- **On the board (task):** the question, the map of South America, the success criteria (say what kind of biome it is; say where it is, starting with the continent, then narrowing it down; say how close it is to the Equator; describe the weather, then say what grows there because of it), and the words South America, Brazil, Equator, so.
- **Thought:** Where is it, starting big, and how does its weather decide what grows there?
- **What happens:** both models come off the board. Each child tells a partner where the Amazon is, starting with the continent, and the partner asks "Why do the trees grow so well there?" Then they write.
- **Levels:** the worksheet is this task printed (map and lines) and replaces it; from the slides, children write from the map and the steps on the board.
- **Look for:** the weather linked to the trees with so or because. If a child writes "It is hot. It has trees.", ask what the heat and rain do for the trees.
- **Why here:** the final task, drawing together the biome idea, the Equator and the Amazon's place.

## Read-back

By the end, children will describe the Amazon rainforest as a biome and locate it outward from its continent, because the lesson gives them the link between a place's weather and what grows there, and the reason rainforests sit near the Equator, evidenced by the five-places map task (where "hot" or "the right continent" leads a child wrong) and their own written description.

Checked from the other end: a child without the weather-to-plants link writes a list (`It is hot. It has trees.`), which the criteria's last step and the weak model show as not yet a description; a child without the Equator rule chooses B or E on the map.

## Decisions the walk-through does not show

- **The learning:** a biome is a big area whose weather decides what grows; tropical rainforests are near the Equator because the Sun is strong there all year, which makes it hot and rainy; the Amazon is the biggest, in South America, mostly Brazil, with the Equator through it. This is factual and locational knowledge with one relationship inside it (weather → plants, Equator → weather), taught on one well-developed case, so `concepts` is empty.
- **Objective and boundary:** describe a biome means describe the rainforest biome by its weather and what grows there, and say what a biome is. Today stays out of the rainforest layers (lesson 2), the Indigenous peoples (lesson 3) and how rainforests are changing (lesson 4). Other biomes are not named or listed: the desert is one contrast, there only to show what the word means. The Tropics, latitude and the word climate are left for later.
- **Prior knowledge:** continents (Key Stage 1) are retrieved in the starter and used twice. Nothing about biomes, the Equator or the Amazon is assumed.
- **Structure:** content-based, because the learning is knowledge with a relationship to understand and locate, not a map procedure to practise. The nearest alternative, skill-based map work, would teach map reading and leave the biome and the why untaught.
- **Sticking point:** "rainforests grow anywhere hot" (and "Africa is hot, so it's rainforest"). Exposed and answered on the board in Teach 2 (the Equator, and the reason), tested in Do 2 with the Sahara and the far south of South America, and met again in the final task's "how close it is to the Equator". The second misconception, "you could grow a rainforest anywhere if you planted the trees", gets one touch: Do 1.
- **Independent evidence:** Do 2 cannot be passed by "hot" or "the right continent", because B and E are built to catch exactly those. The final description is written with a good one in mind (the model is shown first, then taken off the board), so it shows what each child can write with a model in mind, not unaided form.
- **Success criteria:** four steps for the description, visible while children write and never on the sheet. The worksheet is the final task printed, at three levels, and replaces the slide task.
- **Representations and pictures:** the engine draws every map (world map in four configurations, the South America regional map with the sourced Amazon layer). Photographs: a British park, a hot desert, a tropical rainforest, the Amazon from above, and a made picture of the Sun's rays (the one slide that explains how something happens). The Amazon photograph is a real place named as evidence, so it is searched for as real and left out if none is found, since the map already shows the Amazon on that beat.
- **Lesson question:** None. The plan's title is a question, but the final task answers "where is the Amazon and what is it like", and the title is answered on the way.
- **Amount:** three chunks of teaching. The third stays because it is only a name and a place on a map the class can already read, and the final task is its use; leaving it out would leave half the objective, locate the Amazon, untaught. Teach 1 and Teach 2 each close with a task children do (a claim judged, a map in pairs). Time: 40 planned minutes across the beats; taught by asking, at the board's pace, about 44, so about 49 with books out and moving about. The Teach boards were trimmed to the sentences that carry each route (a repeat about oaks dropping their leaves and a second biome sentence moved to the script) to bring it under 50 without losing a because. The worksheet's time is the final task's: it replaces it.
- **Reasoning:** Do 1 is a right claim to confirm with a reason; Do 2 is why-here-and-not-there on new places.
- **Apply:** none. The final description already draws all three chunks together; an Apply would ask the same thing again.
- **Omissions:** no layers, animals or people (later lessons). No other biomes named.
