# Find a missing part (Year 4 Maths, lesson 35)

## The journey

a take-away and a fact family from what the class already knows → the same fact family drawn as a bar model: the whole on top, two parts underneath → an adding sentence with a part missing, where the + sign tempts children to add → the missing part can come first, and the whole is still after the = → a take-away sentence with a part missing: the whole is the first number, and the job is the same → mixed sentences where each child has to find the whole before doing anything → Zofia says an adding sentence and a take-away sentence have the same missing number: is that right?

## The retelling

What the child who understands least would say at home:

> We did number sentences with a number missing. You draw a bar model. The whole goes on top and the parts go underneath. If the number missing is a part, you take the part you know away from the whole. Even if it says plus, you still take away. The whole is the number after the equals, or the first number if it's a take-away.

New things this asks the child to hold: the bar model as a picture of a whole and two parts (the words `whole` and `part`), finding the whole in an adding sentence (after the =), finding the whole in a take-away sentence (the first number), and the one job that never changes: the whole take away the part you know. Everything else comes from earlier lessons: column subtraction across zeros (lessons 28 to 34) and fact families like 2,500 + 1,500 = 4,000 from Year 3.

## The telling

> Three to start. A take-away like last lesson's, with zeros in the way, and two gaps to fill.
> (Children work out 7,004 − 2,358, then 2,500 + 1,500 = 4,000, so 4,000 − 1,500 = ___ and 4,000 − 2,500 = ___.)
> Let's check. 7,004 take away 2,358: the ones need a ten, the tens and hundreds are 0, so the thousand gives first and each 0 becomes 10, then 9. 4,646. And the gaps: 4,000 take away 1,500 is 2,500, and 4,000 take away 2,500 is 1,500. Hold on to those, because we're going to draw them.
>
> Two words we'll use all lesson: whole and part. The whole is the full amount, everything put together. A part is one bit of the whole, and two parts added together make the whole.
>
> Here's 2,500 + 1,500 = 4,000 as a picture. It's called a bar model. The long bar on top is the whole: 4,000. Underneath, the same length is split into two parts: 2,500 and 1,500. Put them back together and you get the whole. So if you know the whole and one part, you can find the other part. Take the part you know away from the whole. 4,000 take away 2,500 leaves 1,500, just like in the starter.
> (Children look at the bar and answer: the whole is 4,000 and one part is 1,500. What's the other part?)
>
> Here's a number sentence with a number missing. 1,240 add something equals 3,860. Look at the sign: it's a plus. Some people see a plus and add the two numbers they can see. Let's not rush. Which number is the whole? In an adding sentence, the two parts go together to make the whole, so the whole is the number after the equals: 3,860. That goes on the long bar. The part I know is 1,240. The other part is missing, so it gets a question mark. Now I know the whole and one part, so I take the part I know away from the whole: 3,860 take away 1,240. Ones, 0 take away 0 is 0. Tens, 6 take away 4 is 2. Hundreds, 8 take away 2 is 6. Thousands, 3 take away 1 is 2. 2,620. That's the missing part. If I'd added, I'd have 5,100, and a part can't be bigger than the whole.
> (Children help with 2,700 + ___ = 4,950, then ___ + 1,600 = 5,000, where the missing part comes first: the whole is still after the equals.)
> (Children do 3,500 + ___ = 8,200 and ___ + 2,150 = 6,000 on their own, drawing a bar model for each.)
>
> Now a take-away with a number missing. 5,000 take away something leaves 1,850. Which number is the whole now? In a take-away we start with the whole and take a part away, so the whole is the first number: 5,000. What's left, 1,850, is one part. The part we took away is the one that's missing. Same bar model: 5,000 on top, 1,850 underneath and a question mark. And the same job: the whole take away the part we know. 5,000 take away 1,850 is 3,150. Some people think: it's a take-away, so I should add. That gives 6,850, which is more than we started with. The sign changed. What we do didn't.
> (Children help with 6,400 − ___ = 2,900.)
> (Children do six on their own, mixed: three adding sentences, one with the missing part first, and three take-away sentences.)
>
> Zofia says: "2,750 + ___ = 6,000 and 6,000 − ___ = 2,750 have the same missing number." Is Zofia correct? Draw a bar model for each one.
> (Children draw both bar models, find both missing numbers, decide, and explain in a sentence or two.)

Read back as the child who understands least: the bar model arrives on numbers they have just worked out themselves in the starter, so the picture is of something they already know is true. The trap (adding because of the + sign) is said out loud in the first model and answered with a check any child can run: a part can't be bigger than the whole. The take-away case is told as the same job with the whole in a different place, and the wrong move (adding to undo a take-away) is said and shown too big. Every calculation is column subtraction the class has practised for seven lessons, so the only new thinking is which number is the whole. The telling brings in nothing the retelling does not need: no checking by putting the answer back in (lesson 37 teaches that), no missing wholes (lesson 36), no word problems on the slides.

For feel: the class listens for about three minutes at most before doing something (the bar model teach, then each model), and the longest stretch of their own work is the second Your Turn. As the teacher standing there, it is a familiar maths shape: model, guide, check, twice, then one reasoning task. The second cycle is short because it is the same job with one new decision.

## Teacher orientation

Teacher orientation: Children learn to find a missing part in an adding or take-away number sentence by drawing a bar model: the whole on top, the two parts underneath, and the missing part is the whole take away the part they know. They meet the bar model on the starter's own fact family, then see it on 1,240 + ___ = 3,860, practise adding sentences (including one where the missing part comes first), then take-away sentences like 5,000 − ___ = 1,850; the six mixed ones in the second Your Turn are the main practice. They finish by deciding whether Zofia is right that an adding sentence and a take-away sentence have the same missing number. The tricky move is not following the sign: in 1,240 + ___ = 3,860 the answer is a take-away, and children who add get 5,100, bigger than the whole. It is shown in the first My Turn and guided in the first Our Turn. From the slides: every question runs from the board, with children drawing their own bar models and columns. To print: the worksheet, done in place of the second Your Turn.

## The lesson, slide by slide

### Starter - Take-aways and fact families

- **On the board:** (1) 7,004 − 2,358 = (2) 2,500 + 1,500 = 4,000, so 4,000 − 1,500 = ___ (3) 2,500 + 1,500 = 4,000, so 4,000 − 2,500 = ___
- **The thought:** Which column needs an exchange, and what is left when one part is taken from 4,000?
- **What happens:** Children set out the subtraction in their own columns and fill the two gaps. On the answers the teacher says what each 0 became in question 1, and asks children to hold on to questions 2 and 3.
- **What you land:** 4,646, 2,500, 1,500.
- **Levels:** board only: three short questions answered in children's own columns or as one number each.
- **Why it is here:** It brings back last lesson's exchange across zeros, which every missing part today is worked out with, and questions 2 and 3 are the fact family the bar model is drawn from next.

### Vocabulary - whole, part

- **On the board:** two cards. `whole`: The whole is the full amount, everything put together. `part`: A part is one bit of the whole. Two parts added together make the whole. Each with a small bar model of 10 split into 6 and 4, the first naming the whole bar, the second the parts.
- **What happens:** The teacher reads each card and points at the bar it names.
- **Why it is here:** Beside the spine: the next board and every step of the criteria use these two words.

### What does a bar model show?

- **On the board:** the bar model of 4,000 over 2,500 and 1,500, all three numbers written in. `This is a bar model. It shows a number and the two parts it's made of.` / `The long bar on top is the whole: 4,000. Underneath, the same length is split into two parts: 2,500 and 1,500.` / `2,500 and 1,500 make 4,000 together. So 4,000 take away 2,500 leaves 1,500, just like in the starter.` Star fact: `The two parts make the whole. So the whole take away one part leaves the other part.`
- **The thought:** Which bar is the whole, and how do the two parts fit underneath it?
- **What happens:** The teacher points along the whole bar, then each part, and asks aloud: the whole is 4,000 and one part is 1,500, what's the other part? Children point to it on the bar (2,500), then read the take-away on the board. The question is spoken, not printed, so the board keeps to five things.
- **What you land:** The two parts make the whole, so the whole take away one part leaves the other part.
- **Why it is here:** It turns the fact family children have just worked into the picture every model uses, and lands the one job (whole take away the part you know) the first My Turn runs.

### My Turn (1,240 + ___ = 3,860)

- **On the board:** `1,240 + ___ = 3,860`; a bar model with the whole bar and the left-hand part empty and the right-hand part marked ?; the criteria, `How to find a missing part`.
- **The thought:** (the class watches)
- **What happens:** The teacher reads the sentence, names the plus and the pull to add, then asks which number is the whole: in an adding sentence the parts make the whole, so it is after the =. Writes 3,860 on the whole bar, 1,240 on one part and ? on the other, works 3,860 − 1,240 in columns (no exchange), and writes 2,620 in the gap. Checks it with the class: adding would have given 5,100, bigger than the whole.
- **What you land:** 1,240 + 2,620 = 3,860. The whole is after the =, and the missing part is the whole take away the part you know.
- **Why it is here:** It shows the whole method on the case the lesson turns on, with the trap named before anyone falls in, which the Our Turn then guides.

### Our Turn (2,700 + ___ = 4,950 and ___ + 1,600 = 5,000)

- **On the board:** the two sentences, each with its own empty bar model; the criteria.
- **The thought:** Which number do the two parts make together, and which part do I already know?
- **What happens:** For each, the teacher asks which number is the whole and how they know, which part is known, and what to take away from what; children say it and work the subtraction, then the teacher writes it in. The second has the missing part first, so the teacher asks whether that changes where the whole is.
- **Levels:** board only: worked with the teacher, children answer aloud or in their own working.
- **What you land:** 2,250 and 3,400. The whole is after the = wherever the missing part sits.
- **Why it is here:** It hands children the decision (which number is the whole) on two fresh sentences, including the missing-first one the Your Turns use.

### Your Turn (two adding sentences)

- **On the board:** 3,500 + ___ = 8,200 and ___ + 2,150 = 6,000, one per line; `Draw a bar model for each one.`; the criteria.
- **The thought:** Which number is the whole here, even though the sign says +?
- **What happens:** Each child draws a bar model for each and works the missing part. Two goes, about two minutes each, then the answers.
- **Levels:** board only: two short sentences children set out in their own bar models and columns; a sheet would only reprint them.
- **What you land:** 3,500 + 4,700 = 8,200 and 3,850 + 2,150 = 6,000 (the answers slide shows each sentence completed). An 11,700 or an 8,150 means the child added because of the +.
- **Why it is here:** A quick check that each child can find the whole in an adding sentence before the take-away case is added.

### My Turn (5,000 − ___ = 1,850)

- **On the board:** `5,000 − ___ = 1,850`; an empty bar model; the criteria; the star fact.
- **The thought:** (the class watches)
- **What happens:** The teacher asks which number is the whole now: in a take-away we start with the whole and take a part away, so it is the first number. Writes 5,000 on the whole bar, 1,850 on one part and ? on the other, works 5,000 − 1,850 across the zeros, and writes 3,150 in the gap. Names the wrong move: adding to undo a take-away gives 6,850, more than we started with.
- **What you land:** 5,000 − 3,150 = 1,850. In a take-away the whole is the first number, and the job is the same.
- **Why it is here:** It is the second decision the mixed practice needs (where the whole is in a take-away), shown on its own before the class mixes the two.

### Our Turn (6,400 − ___ = 2,900)

- **On the board:** the sentence, its own empty bar model, the criteria.
- **The thought:** In a take-away, which number is the whole?
- **What happens:** The teacher asks which number we started with, which part is left, and what to take away from what; children work 6,400 − 2,900 with one exchange.
- **Levels:** board only: worked with the teacher.
- **What you land:** 3,500.
- **Why it is here:** One guided go at the take-away case before children meet it mixed with adding sentences.

### Your Turn (six mixed)

- **On the board:** 2,300 + ___ = 5,900, 7,000 − ___ = 2,450, ___ + 3,725 = 8,000, 6,250 − ___ = 3,900, 1,980 + ___ = 4,500 and 9,000 − ___ = 4,365, one per line; `Draw a bar model for each one.`; the criteria.
- **The thought:** Is this an adding or a take-away sentence, so where is the whole, before I take anything away?
- **What happens:** Each child draws a bar model for each and works the missing part in columns. About nine minutes: six goes, four of them crossing zeros or making an exchange, then the answers.
- **Levels:** board only: short sentences children set out themselves; the worksheet is the printed option, done in this Your Turn's place (four sentences, Zara's claim and one problem, about ten minutes).
- **What you land:** 3,600, 4,550, 4,275, 2,350, 2,520, 4,635, each shown on the answers slide as its completed sentence. In every bar model the whole is on top: within today's sentences the whole is always the biggest number, so the drawing, not the answer, is what shows a child found it.
- **Why it is here:** The main practice. Mixing the two kinds is where each child has to find the whole for themselves every time, which the Apply then asks them to compare.

### Apply (Zofia's claim)

- **On the board:** Zofia in a speech bubble: "2,750 + ___ = 6,000 and 6,000 − ___ = 2,750 have the same missing number." `Is Zofia correct? Explain your answer.` `Draw a bar model for each one.`; the criteria.
- **The thought:** Are the whole and the part I know the same in both sentences?
- **What happens:** Each child draws both bar models, finds both missing numbers, decides and explains in a sentence or two.
- **What you land:** Yes. Both have 6,000 as the whole and 2,750 as the part we know, so both missing numbers are 6,000 − 2,750 = 3,250. A child who follows the + sign gets 8,750 for the first and says no.
- **Why it is here:** It asks something no Your Turn did: compare two sentences with different signs and see that they are one bar model, which is the lesson's idea, the sign doesn't decide the job.

## Read-back

By the end, children will find a missing part in adding and take-away number sentences up to 4 digits because the lesson gives them the bar model (the whole on top, the parts underneath) and where the whole sits in each kind of sentence, which replaces following the printed sign; evidenced by the six mixed sentences in the second Your Turn, where the adding sentences cannot be answered by adding, and by Zofia's claim, which a child who follows the sign gets wrong.

Read from the other end: a child who did not learn where the whole sits gets 8,750 in the Apply and adds in the three adding sentences of the second Your Turn, so the evidence catches them.

## The decisions the walk-through does not show

- **The learning:** children understand that a number sentence with one part missing is a whole and two parts, and that the missing part is always the whole take away the part you know, whatever sign is printed. It is a method with an idea under it (the sign does not decide the job); the bar model is how the idea is seen.
- **Objective and boundary:** `To find a missing part (+ and −)`, word for word from the plan. Today is the known whole and one known part only. Missing wholes (lesson 36), mixed whole-or-part sentences and putting the answer back in to check (lesson 37) and checking with the inverse (lesson 39) stay out; the plan says not to introduce missing-whole questions in the first teaching step, and this lesson never does.
- **Prior knowledge and structure:** column subtraction across zeros (lessons 28 to 34) is secure enough to be said aloud, so its list is not printed; the bar model is treated as new because no earlier lesson in the plan used it, so it gets its own short Teach on numbers the class has just worked. Skill-based, because finding a missing part is a repeatable method children need to run reliably; one concept with two cycles, because the take-away case changes one decision (where the whole is) but not the job.
- **The steps on the hardest case** (9,000 − ___ = 4,365): Taking away, so the whole is the first number, 9,000 (taught in the second My Turn). Draw the bar model with 9,000 on top (taught in the Teach and both My Turns). Write 4,365 as the part you know and ? for the other (both My Turns). Work 9,000 − 4,365 across three zeros: 4,635 (done at this size in lessons 33 and 34, and in today's starter). Write 4,635 in the gap.
- **Sticking point:** following the printed sign, so 1,240 + ___ = 3,860 is answered 5,100. Exposed and answered in the first My Turn (said aloud, with "a part can't be bigger than the whole"), guided in the first Our Turn, checked in the first Your Turn, retested in the three adding sentences of the second Your Turn and in the Apply. Each child finds the whole of an adding sentence alone six times (two in the first Your Turn, three in the second, one in the Apply). The secondary wrong move, adding to undo a take-away (6,850), gets one touch in the second My Turn and its teacher notes.
- **Independent evidence:** the second Your Turn mixes the two kinds in no pattern, so the sign cannot tell a child where the whole is; the adding sentences give a wrong answer to anyone who follows the sign. The Apply's claim is right, so it cannot be answered no by habit. One honest limit: in a missing-part sentence the whole is always the biggest number, so a child using `bigger take away smaller` gets today's answers right too. The bar model each child draws (the whole on top) is what shows the whole was found, which is why every Your Turn asks for one; lesson 36, where the whole is the missing number, is where `bigger take away smaller` stops working.
- **Success criteria:** one list, `How to find a missing part`, four steps, with where the whole is as two smaller points under the first step. Marked to draw live, because lessons 36 and 37 run the same bar model. The column subtraction list is not printed: the class has owned it since lesson 28. The worksheet carries fresh numbers and one wrong claim, so the claims stay mixed with the Apply's right one.
- **Representation:** the bar model (the whole on top, the parts underneath, touching) is load-bearing: it is how children see which number is the whole. Children draw their own in their books in the Your Turns and the Apply; Below gets it printed (the adaptation's call).
- **Lesson question:** None: a method lesson whose Your Turns are the evidence.
- **Amount:** one chunk of new teaching (the bar model and the adding case) and a short second cycle (the take-away case) before the main practice. The second cycle stays because it is a genuinely different decision the mixed practice needs, and it is short (one model, one guided go). The task count reads five because a maths skill lesson keeps its My Turn, Our Turn, Your Turn rounds; the first Your Turn is a two-question check, not a second main task. The beats are planned at 41 minutes and take about 45 taught by asking, about 50 with books out. A class doing the worksheet in place of the second Your Turn spends about a minute longer there.
- **Reasoning and problem solving:** the Apply is reasoning (comparing two sentences' structure); the worksheet adds a wrong claim to judge and one problem in a context.
- **Deliberate omissions:** the plan's misconception `children may subtract one part from the other` does not arise with a known whole and one known part, so it has no beat; it belongs to lessons where two parts are given. No word problems on the slides: the worksheet carries one (Oak Class's bottle tops), after the bare sentences and Zara's claim. The worksheet has four sentences rather than five or six so it fits the time of the Your Turn it replaces.
- **Flags for the teacher:** None.
