How have children's lives changed?
1. What do sources tell us about how children's lives have changed? |  | LO: To use sources to compare children's lives. | Recap: check what children can recall about how historians learn about the past, examples and limitations of evidence, a simple chronology from Stone Age to Iron Age, and the meaning of change and continuity. Attention grabber: share childhood images and discuss what childhood means, then explain that the age children became adults has changed over time (Roman girls at 12, boys at 14; Tudor children adults at 15 but working from 7-8; Victorian children adults at 21 but working from 10; today 18) - optionally children complete the Skills catcher to revisit at the end of the unit. Main event: model making an observation and a deduction from an image of a Tudor child, then children practise this in pairs on two more sources; discuss primary versus secondary sources and why so few Tudor images of children survive. Move on to continuity and change, comparing a Roman and a Tudor school image, then children use Activity: Sources and Activity: Continuity and change in pairs to compare children's lives across two periods. Wrapping up: pairs discuss why changes happened, what questions the sources have not answered, and what other sources could answer them. | Watch for treating Roman, Tudor and Victorian childhoods as equally long, consecutive stages; mistaking a later Tudor illustration for an eyewitness image; presenting a deduction as something directly visible. One school image or apprenticeship contract cannot represent all children, and primary does not automatically mean more useful. Avoid treating adulthood ages as universal or assuming every child experienced steady improvement. | What does childhood mean? (Childhood is the time from being a baby to becoming a teenager; it is the time children play and have fun with their friends; learn new things; have lots of imagination; have few responsibilities; see every day as a big adventure.) / Which source is the primary source? Which sources are secondary? (The primary source is the 1563 apprenticeship contract made during the Tudor times, whereas the secondary sources are the Tudor images made after the Tudor times.) / How useful are these sources in showing the life of Tudor children? Why? (The sources are useful because they show how some Tudor children played and how some Tudor children worked.) / Which source do you think is the most helpful? Why? (The children may suggest: the primary source as it is from the Tudor period; the secondary sources as they show what toys or sports some Tudor children played.) / What viewpoints are the sources missing? (The Apprenticeship Contract is missing the viewpoint of the Tudor child and the images are missing the viewpoints of poorer Tudor children.) / What do 'continuity' and 'change' mean? (A continuity is something that stays the same over time, such as going to school or playing with friends. Change is something that becomes different, such as the types of toys that children play with.) / What similarities did you notice in children's lives today? (Children went to school; children learned to read and write; children had specific clothing or uniforms.) / What is different about children's lives today? (Children today use technology such as computers, tablets and smartboards; school buildings have heating, lighting and more comfortable furniture; classrooms are diverse and a wide range of subjects are learned.) / Why do you think some of the changes to children's lives happened? (New inventions like computers and the internet have made learning and playing different; childhood has improved as people understand more about how children grow and learn; communities changed their ideas about what is important for children.) / Do you have questions about children's lives in the past that the sources have not answered? (The children may suggest questions about: the types of games and toys children had in the past; what a normal day was like, the types of chores or work they had to do.) / What kind of sources would you need to answer your questions? (Books written by historians; artefacts from the past like toys, tools or the objects children used; diaries or letters written by or about children; things discovered by archaeologists to show how children lived.) | I can see ___ in this source, so I think ___. / This is a primary / secondary source because ___. / One thing that has stayed the same is ___, but one thing that has changed is ___. | childhood - The time between infancy and adolescence. / continuity - When things remain the same. / change - When things become different. / deduction - Conclusions reached after looking at sources. / historian - Someone who studies or writes about history. / observation - Looking carefully at a source to learn about the past. / primary source - An object or record created at the time of an event or period. / source - Anything that gives information about the past. / secondary source - An object or record created after an event or period. / Tudor period - Started in 1485 when Henry VII came to the throne and ended in 1603 with the death of Elizabeth I. / viewpoint - An opinion about a topic or event. | The lesson opens on a Kapow presentation called What is childhood?, which shows a range of childhood images to spark the class discussion. A second presentation, Making an observation and deduction, shows three Tudor child images with hidden eye and light bulb buttons that reveal a possible observation and deduction for each, so the teacher can model the skill before pairs try it themselves. A third, Continuity and change, sets a Roman school image against a Tudor one and reveals a worked example of a continuity and a change between them. Children work in pairs from a printed Activity: Sources sheet (one between two) to find their own sources, and a printed Activity: Continuity and change sheet (one each) to record what they notice, checked against a teacher-only Answer sheet: Continuity and change. Each child also gets a printed Resource: Knowledge organiser for the unit to keep, and there is an optional class set of a Resource: Unit vocabulary sheet for display. An optional online Skills catcher, reached through the link Assessment - History Y4: How have children's lives changed?, lets children record what they already know now and add to it in a different colour at the end of the unit.
2. Why did Tudor children work and what was it like? |  | LO: To explain why Tudor children worked. | Recap and recall: in pairs, children write three things they learned about children's lives last lesson, two things they found interesting, and one remaining question. Attention grabber: watch the Pupil video: Lots of work and little play and discuss what jobs Tudor children did, at what age, and why they needed to work. Main event: use the Kapow Primary timeline to place the Tudor period (1485-1603) among the periods studied. Look at slide 1 of Presentation: Tudor apprentices and discuss what an apprentice is and what the two apprentices pictured are doing, then discuss working conditions from the video and audio. On slide 2, children match Tudor apprentice trades to their descriptions. Children then prepare to hot-seat a Tudor apprentice: discuss what makes a good open question and question starters, model examples, then use Activity: Hot seat questions to write their own (an adult or child plays the apprentice, using Activity: Henry the apprentice as background and optional costume). Wrapping up: children choose their two best questions and question the apprentice in the hot seat, recording key information, then discuss how useful and how limited hot seating is as a way of finding out about the past, and what they have learnt about working Tudor children's lives. | Watch for assuming all Tudor children started the same job at seven, or that every apprentice was paid wages and treated alike. Poverty, training and limited schooling offered different reasons for working; parents' choices do not prove they did not care. Henry's hot-seat answers are a reconstruction, not a surviving child's testimony: plausible feelings and invented details need distinguishing from supported information. | What jobs did children do in Tudor times? (Children may suggest: boys often became apprentices; girls often worked in wealthier households as maidservants or scullery maids; many Tudor children worked on farms feeding animals, harvesting crops and collecting firewood; poorer boys worked as chimney sweeps or street sellers.) / At what age did children work? (Normally from the age of seven but sometimes under.) / Why did some children need to work? (Children may suggest: to earn money for their families; to learn a trade to support themselves as adults; education was not accessible to most children; working was one of the few options available to the children.) / Were there any jobs that surprised you? (Open discussion.) / Can you identify some periods from the last lesson? (The Romans, the Tudors, the Victorians, interwar and modern Britain.) / Can you identify the Tudor period? (The period started in 1485 when Henry VII came to the throne and ended in 1603 with the death of Elizabeth I.) / How long ago did the Tudor kings and queens rule England? (It is over 400 years since Elizabeth I died.) / What do you think an apprentice is? (Someone who learns a trade or skill by working for someone, called a master.) / What do you think the apprentices in the images are doing? (The apprentice on the left is carrying water, most likely from a well, to be used in a home or farm; the apprentice on the right is likely a papermaker or printer, carrying away finished sheets.) / From the video and audio, what can we learn about the working conditions? (They worked long hours; it was hard, tiring work, especially for children who worked on farms; those who worked in households did chores like cleaning, cooking, making bread and fetching water; some apprentices were treated well while others were mistreated; work environments were often unsafe; poorer children had to work to support their families so there was little time for education or play; wealthier children could attend school and play.) / Which job would you like and not like to do? Why? (Open discussion.) / What kind of information do you want to know about Tudor children? (The children may suggest: how long they worked for, how many days a week, how they felt leaving their home and parents, what they did in their spare time, what their job involved, what clothes they wore, etc.) / Which kind of questions will give detailed answers? (Open questions or questions that encourage detailed answers.) / What question starters should we use? ('What', 'Why', 'How', 'Describe', 'Tell me about...') / How useful is hot seating to understand the lives of child apprentices in the past? (The children may suggest: it lets them ask questions and get answers from someone acting as a child from the past; it helps them imagine what their daily lives were like and how they felt; we can learn about their experiences, work and family life in a more interactive way.) / What are the problems with hot seating? (The children may suggest: the person answering is just pretending and might not know all the facts; we cannot get real personal stories or detailed information like we can from actual historical documents; sometimes the answers might not be completely accurate if the actor doesn't have all the information.) / What have you learnt about the lives of working Tudor children? (Open discussion, could be recorded on a large sheet of paper for future lessons.) | Tudor children worked because ___. / An apprentice was ___, and their day would have involved ___. / Hot seating helped me find out ___, but it cannot tell me ___. | apprentice - A young person who learns a trade or occupation in return for a place to stay, clothing and food. / master - A skilled craftsman who takes on and teaches his trade to a young apprentice. / oath - A solemn promise. / poorer - Having less money and fewer resources, making it difficult to meet basic needs. / wealthier - Having more money and resources, making it easier to afford necessities and luxuries. / working conditions - The environment in which people work, including safety, hours and pay. | A Presentation: 3, 2, 1 slide structures the recap, prompting pairs to jot three things learned, two things that interested them and one lingering question on shared whiteboards and pens. The Pupil video Lots of work and little play kicks the lesson off, showing children the range of jobs Tudor children did. Presentation: Tudor apprentices carries audio buttons that play out what daily apprentice life was actually like, and a second slide where children drag and drop trade descriptions onto the right jobs. The class also drops into the online Kapow Primary timeline to place the Tudor period among the periods studied so far. For the hot-seating activity, children write their own questions on a printed Activity: Hot seating questions sheet (a support version is available for those who need question starters), while the adult or child playing the apprentice prepares using the printed Resource: Henry the apprentice as background knowledge, optionally dressed in Tudor apprentice clothing (a loose shirt, fitted jacket with breeches, woollen cloak and cap) and flipchart paper to record what the class learns from the hot seat.
3. What were children's jobs like in Victorian England? |  | LO: To describe Victorian children's working conditions. | Recap and recall: in pairs, children discuss and take a stance on whether it would have been great to be a Tudor child apprentice, using Presentation: Agree or disagree?. Attention grabber: use the Kapow Primary timeline to move into the Victorian period, then Presentation: Victorian children explains that Victorian society was grouped into classes and working-class children often had jobs. Listen to an audio extract from the 1842 report on children's working conditions, then hear the account of John Hobson, a 13-year-old collier interviewed for the report who had worked in the pit since age five - discuss his jobs (trapper, hooking corves on the gin, hurrying), how he was treated, and how his experience compares with Tudor children's. Discuss the source's status as primary evidence, its usefulness, and what other sources could help. Main event: children act as archivists, researching and recording historical information (optionally after watching Pupil video: History in action: Working in an archive). In pairs, each pair is assigned one of five Victorian jobs from Resource: Victorian jobs and records what they learn on Activity: Research and record, modelled first using Presentation: Recording and the 1842 mines report; devices with internet access and BBC videos on coal mines, textile mills, bird scarers and domestic servants can extend the research. A few pairs share their findings with the class. Wrapping up: pairs share what surprised or interested them, hear an extract from Presentation: Victorian child servants, then discuss the worst job a child could have and whether Tudor or Victorian working conditions were worse. | Watch for making John Hobson typical of every Victorian child, assuming only boys worked underground, or treating the recorded interview as an unfiltered account of all working conditions. His saying he was not badly treated does not erase the beatings he describes. Comparisons with Tudor work need specific jobs and evidence: later does not automatically mean better, nor does one dangerous job make every Victorian childhood worse. | Why did the children need to work? (Victorian children would have begged otherwise, as they did not have much money for food.) / Is there anything surprising in the extract? (The children may suggest: some of the children working in the mines were girls. Generally, people believed only boys worked in the mines.) / What jobs did John have in the coal mine? (He worked as a 'trapper' (opening and shutting trap doors to keep the mine ventilated); he then 'hooked the corves on the gin' (attached coal-filled baskets to a horse-powered pulley to be raised to the surface); and 'hurries' (pushes coal-filled wagons along the mine).) / Was he well-treated? (He felt he was not badly treated, but he states he was beaten when he did not do a job properly and heard plenty of bad language.) / How is John's experience of work different from that of Tudor children? (Children may suggest: John started working at a younger age than most Tudor children; John worked almost entirely underground whereas most Tudor children worked in light conditions; John's working conditions in the coal mine were more dangerous than the jobs some Tudor children did.) / Is this a primary or a secondary source? (The report is a primary source as it was written by someone during the time of the event.) / How useful is this source? Why? (The children may suggest: very useful because it gives detailed information about the working conditions Victorian children faced; it shows the long hours and hard work the children had to do; it helps people understand the difficult daily lives of Victorian children; the accounts are from the children themselves.) / What other sources could we use to better understand the jobs and working conditions of Victorian children? (Diaries or letters from Victorian children or adults; photographs; illustrations made after the time; other government records and laws about child labour; books by historians who have researched the topic.) / What do you think was the worst job a child could have? Why? (Open discussion.) / Was it worse for a child to have a job in Tudor times or Victorian times? (The children may suggest: Victorian children had more dangerous conditions than in Tudor times like working in the mines or mills; Victorian children started working at a younger age compared to Tudor children.) | John's job as a ___ meant he had to ___. / This source is useful because ___, but it does not tell us ___. / I think working conditions were worse in ___ times because ___. | archivist - Someone who takes care of important documents and records, making sure they are safely kept and can be looked at in the future. / bird scarer - A child whose job was to stand in a farmer's field scaring or chasing away birds. / class - A way of grouping people based on their job, income or education, which can affect their lifestyle and opportunities. / servant - A member of staff who kept the house clean and looked after the family. / gin - A device used to haul coal to the surface. / hurrier - A child employed to push and pull containers full of coal along the mine. / textile mill - A special factory which produced cotton, wool and other types of fabric. / trapper - A person who worked in a coal mine opening and closing trap doors to allow air to circulate. / Victorian period - The time in British history when Queen Victoria ruled, from 1837 to 1901. | The lesson opens on a Kapow presentation called Agree or disagree?, which sets up the pairs discussion about whether being a Tudor apprentice would have been a good life. The class moves into the Victorian period on the online Kapow Primary timeline, then a presentation called Victorian children carries an audio extract from the real 1842 report on children's working conditions, including John Hobson's own account of working in the coal mines, with vocabulary definitions built into the audio. To model the main task, a presentation called Recording walks through how to pull relevant information from the 1842 report column by column before children try it themselves. Each pair is given a printed Resource: Victorian jobs sheet naming one of five Victorian jobs to research, and records their findings on a printed Activity: Research and record sheet (a support version is available); devices with internet access let pairs go further using four optional BBC videos on children working in coal mines, textile mills, as bird scarers and as domestic servants, alongside an optional Pupil video, History in action: Working in an archive, that explains what an archivist actually does. Children who finish early can use the printed Activity: Letter home: extension sheet to write in more detail. The lesson closes with a further extract for the class to reflect on, shown on a presentation called Victorian child servants.

How did Lord Shaftesbury help to change the lives of children? |  | LO: To explain why Lord Shaftesbury was significant. | Recap and recall: in pairs, children use Presentation: Gimme five! to name five jobs Victorian children did and describe the activities, conditions and health risks of each. Attention grabber: use the Kapow Primary timeline to enter the Victorian period and discuss when it began and how long after the Tudor period. Watch the BBC 2 video imagining Lord Shaftesbury on a chat show with Charles Dickens, up to 01:49, and discuss who he was, what he did, why his bill failed, and whether it eventually became law (the Factory Act). Discuss what 'historically significant' means and why Lord Shaftesbury might be considered significant. Main event: in pairs, children write concerns about children's working conditions from the Tudor to Victorian period on whiteboards, then check answers on Presentation: Child labour and Lord Shaftesbury, read the Factory Inspectors' Report and discuss what was surprising and what they think of Lord Shaftesbury. Learn about the laws he tried to bring to parliament and the ragged schools he helped set up. In pairs, children use Activity: The changes and impact to sort statements into changes and impacts and match each change to its impact (the Factory Act 1833, the Factory Acts 1847 and 1850, the Mines Act 1842, and ragged schools), then discuss why it was difficult to make factory owners follow the new law and which parts of the Factory Act were broken. Wrapping up: point out that compulsory education from 5 to 16 is living proof of Lord Shaftesbury's impact, then use Presentation: Do children in Britain work today? to discuss whether children should work today, what working conditions matter most, and how Lord Shaftesbury might assess children's lives now. | Watch for crediting Shaftesbury alone with ending child labour or creating today's compulsory schooling. Significance rests on evidenced effects, not simply being famous or kind. A bill was not yet law; the 1833 restrictions and 1847 ten-hour reform were different steps. Passing an Act did not ensure compliance or protect every worker immediately. The Dickens chat show is a modern dramatisation, not a recorded interview. | When did the Victorian period officially begin? (It began in 1837, when Queen Victoria's reign began, and ended in 1901.) / If the Tudor period ended in 1603, how long after the Tudor period did the Victorian period begin? (234 years.) / Who was interviewed? (Lord Anthony Ashley Cooper, the 7th Earl of Shaftesbury.) / What did Lord Shaftesbury do? (He believed that child labour should be limited and education was a way of freeing children from poverty; he tried to introduce a bill (a proposal for a new law) reducing to ten the number of hours a child could work each day.) / Why did it fail? (There was resistance from factory owners and politicians who thought they would lose money.) / Did the bill become law? (Not immediately but the Government introduced the Factory Act, which restricted children's working hours to ten.) / What does 'historically significant' mean? (A person, event or idea which is believed to be important by historians, a person who made important changes in the past.) / Why might Lord Shaftesbury be considered a historically significant person? (He tried to improve working laws for children; he contributed to making children's lives better.) / Why was it difficult to make factory owners follow the new law? (There were few inspectors to make sure the new laws were followed; factory owners were worried it would cost them more and they would make less money; some factory owners made false reports or hid from inspectors; many children who worked depended on their jobs so they might not have complained as much or demanded better conditions.) / Which parts of the Factory Act were broken? (Some factory owners hired children younger than nine by lying about their age and working them longer than 12 hours; some factory owners did not make sure that children under 13 went to school for two hours a day.) / Do you agree with this? (The children may agree as it helps children learn responsibility, they can earn their own money and become more independent, it can give them work experience which can help in the future; the children may disagree and suggest school should be children's main focus at this age, it might interfere with their education and homework and they should have time to enjoy being children and play.) / What are the most important working conditions for today's children? (Various answers, including: workplace safety, limited working hours, support, supervision, respect, fair treatment and fair pay.) / How would Lord Shaftesbury assess children's lives today? (The children may suggest: he might be happy that children no longer work in dangerous factories and mines; he would be pleased with the laws that protect children and make sure they go to school; he would appreciate the progress in children's rights; he might be concerned that some children still work too many hours that it affects their education; he might think more needs to be done to balance work and education for children.) | Lord Shaftesbury wanted to ___ because ___. / The ___ Act changed children's lives by ___. / I think Lord Shaftesbury was / was not historically significant because ___. | bill - A proposal for a new law. / Factory Acts - A series of laws passed by Victorian governments to improve the working conditions of children. / government - The people that run a country and make laws. / historically significant - A person, event, place or idea that is considered important. / law - A rule that everyone in a place must follow, decided by the people in charge. / parliament - An assembly of people who make laws and check the work of the government. / UK Parliament - The House of Commons, the House of Lords and the monarch. / poverty - Having little money to pay for accommodation, food, heating or clothing. / ragged schools - Schools for poorer children which were run in people's homes after working hours. / reform - To make changes to something to improve it. | The recap runs from a Kapow presentation called Gimme five!, prompting pairs to name five Victorian children's jobs on shared whiteboards and pens. The online Kapow Primary timeline takes the class into the Victorian period, then an external BBC 2 video (Lord Shaftesbury and ragged schools) imagines Lord Shaftesbury being interviewed by Charles Dickens on a chat show, played to 01:49 to introduce his views and his failed bill. A presentation called Child labour and Lord Shaftesbury then carries the class through his story in stages: an arrow-reveal slide of children's concerns, a link through to the Factory Inspectors' Report to read as a class, a scrolling slide on the laws he tried to pass, and a slide on the ragged schools he helped set up. Working in pairs, children use a printed, pre-cut Activity: The changes and impact card set (a support version is available) to sort statements into changes and impacts and match each to the other. The lesson closes with the final discussion on child work today and how Lord Shaftesbury might judge it, prompted by a presentation called Do children in Britain work today?.

How and why has children's leisure time changed? |  | LO: To explain how children's leisure time changed. | Recap and recall: in pairs, children use Presentation: Explain the answer to discuss what they learned last lesson about Lord Shaftesbury (the failed and later successful factory laws, ragged schools, the Coal Mines Act 1842, and the Chimney Sweepers Act 1875). Attention grabber: use Presentation: Leisure time to show children's leisure activities across time periods, reading fun facts, and discuss what 'leisure' means, what surprised them, and whether children still enjoy the same activities today. Main event: read the National Archives Tudor Entertainment source, Elizabeth I's 1590 order restricting theatre performances, and discuss which activities are mentioned, what she was trying to ban and why, what the source shows and does not show, and why so few sources record Tudor children's own view of leisure. Move to comparing leisure activities: look at an illustrated Tudor frost fair (made by artist Dan Escott around the mid-1900s) and discuss the activities shown, how today's fairs differ, why the image is a secondary source, and what that suggests about primary sources on Tudor children. Compare Tudor, Victorian and modern fairgrounds (entry methods and rides), then use Presentation: Statement slide 2 and a Venn diagram to sort statements by period and identify continuities. In pairs, children complete their own Venn diagram on Activity: Comparing leisure activities using Resource: Children's leisure activities, then discuss whether all leisure activities have changed and why. Wrapping up: in pairs, children discuss whether they agree or disagree with a statement about fairgrounds using Presentation: Statement, justifying their view with sources from the lesson. | Watch for dating Escott's frost-fair illustration to Tudor times because of what it depicts. Elizabeth's order reveals intended restrictions, not whether people obeyed or how children felt. Sparse children's accounts do not mean they had no leisure. Fairground technology could change while social purposes continued; children had unequal money and free time, so one fair cannot represent everyone's leisure or prove all change was improvement. | What do you think 'leisure' means? (Time spent relaxing, entertaining or pursuing hobbies.) / What Tudor or Victorian leisure activities surprised you? (Open discussion.) / Take a look at the other examples. Do children still enjoy these activities today? (Yes, many children today continue to play tennis, marbles and with dolls or visit the theatre.) / Which leisure activities are mentioned? (Plays, comedies and bear-baiting.) / What was Elizabeth I trying to ban? Why? (Plays on Sundays and Thursdays because she felt too many people were watching plays instead of her preferred sport of bear-baiting.) / What else does this primary source show? (The monarchy and authorities at the time wanted to protect Sunday from being disturbed by plays and other entertainment; how public entertainment was controlled by the government.) / Is there anything it does not show? (It does not share how Tudor children reacted to the activities Queen Elizabeth I was trying to ban; it does not show all of the leisure activities Tudor children may have enjoyed; it does not show if the ban successfully encouraged people to watch bear-baiting.) / Can you identify some of the activities at the Tudor frost fair? (Ice skating, eating, bowling, chatting with people, fishing, etc.) / What might be different in today's fairs? (The children may suggest: the type of food eaten; the types of rides and activities; the use of technology; disabled access; more safety regulations; height and age restrictions.) / What makes this a secondary source? (It is a secondary source as it is an illustrated image made by an adult in the 1900s, which was after the Tudor period.) / What does this suggest about the primary sources available about children from the Tudor period? (Adults often interpreted what children's childhood was like; many sources are not from children's viewpoints; some primary sources do not give all the information about children's leisure time.) / Did anything surprise you? (Open discussion.) / What leisure activities did Victorian children enjoy at fairs? (Various answers include: steam-powered rides, quoits/hoopla, coconut shy, helter-skelter, merry-go-round, browsing stalls, etc.) / What has continued in today's fairs? (Various answers include: ticket booths, food and craft stalls, merry-go-round rides, people enjoying themselves; people of all ages attending.) / Why do you think fairgrounds changed? (New technology made rides more exciting; what children enjoyed and ate changed over time; new ideas and materials made it possible to create new rides.) / Have all children's leisure activities changed? Why? (Most activities have changed because new technology and inventions have given children more options; child labour laws and changes in education during the Victorian period allowed children to have more free time; some activities have stayed the same like playing outside with friends, reading books and playing board games.) | Leisure time means ___, so a leisure activity could be ___. / Tudor children spent their free time ___, whereas children today ___. / I agree / disagree with this statement because the sources show ___. | compare - To identify similarities and differences between things. / leisure - Free time spent relaxing, entertaining or enjoying hobbies. / leisure activities - What people do in their free time for enjoyment and relaxation. / modern - Relating to the present or recent past. / Modern period - The current time of human history, commonly known from the 19th century until today. | The recap is structured by a Kapow presentation called Explain the answer, prompting pairs to talk through what they remember about Lord Shaftesbury. A presentation called Leisure time then walks through children's leisure activities period by period, with a question button on each slide that reveals a fun fact. An external National Archives page, Tudor Entertainment - Source five, gives Elizabeth I's real 1590 order restricting theatre performances for the class to read together. A presentation called Comparing a leisure activity carries an enlargeable Tudor frost fair illustration and, on its second slide, an interactive Venn diagram that children drag statements onto to sort them by period, revealing the answers afterwards. In pairs, children complete their own version on a printed Activity: Comparing leisure activities sheet (a support version is available), working from a printed Resource: Children's leisure activities showing images from all three periods, checked against a teacher-only Answer sheet: Comparing leisure activities. The lesson closes with a claim about fairgrounds for pairs to agree or disagree with, using what they have learned to justify their view, on a presentation called Statement.

What were the diseases children caught and how were they treated? |  | LO: To explain how diseases spread and were treated. | Recap and recall: in pairs, children take turns speaking as the expert on how children's leisure activities changed from the Tudors to today, using Presentation: Speak like an expert. Attention grabber: pairs discuss diseases and illnesses they know today, then Presentation: Diseases shows a Tudor scene (people unwell or dying, a doctor treating people) and a Victorian scene (a crowded, cramped slum), introducing the plague (Tudor) and cholera (Victorian). Slide two gives disease symptoms, then discuss why some diseases continued from the Tudor to the Victorian period and what could have caused them to spread. Main event: children use Activity: How did diseases spread in the past? to sort reasons for disease spread from most to least likely, then discuss the least and most likely reasons and whether diseases spread the same way today. Watch Pupil video: The plague doctor and discuss who the plague doctor was, why they dressed as they did, and whether the windowsill herb treatment would have worked. In pairs, children cut and share out Activity: Treatment cards (four each) and use Activity: Disease cards to match each disease to its two possible treatments, then discuss and record how effective the treatments were, checking answers on Presentation: Diseases and treatments. Wrapping up: discuss which treatment would have been most and least effective and why treatments today are so different, then explain that healthcare has improved through the development of scientists, doctors, nurses and surgeons. Optionally, children revisit the Skills catcher and Knowledge catcher in a different colour and complete the Unit quiz to end the unit. | Watch for calling Tudor treatments foolish using knowledge unavailable then, or assuming doctors already understood germs. Disease causes, contemporary explanations and attempted cures are different; matching a treatment card does not establish effectiveness. Avoid treating all diseases as spreading identically or blaming poverty on personal dirtiness. Check costume dates: the familiar beaked plague-doctor outfit is seventeenth-century, not typical Tudor dress. Medical understanding changed within periods as well as between them. | What is happening in the picture of the Tudor period? (People, including children, are unwell or dying; a doctor is trying to treat people.) / What is happening in the picture of the Victorian period? (People are crowded in a slum area; some are barefoot; the place looks cramped and damp, etc.) / Why do you think some diseases continued from the Tudor to the Victorian period?
