# How did cholera spread, and how did people try to stop it?

Year 4 History. Lesson 6 of "How have children's lives changed?" LO: To explain how diseases spread and were treated. 45 minutes.

## The journey

a smelly, crowded Victorian street and its pump → the bad-air idea and the burning tar → what a bad-air believer would and would not do → John Snow's dirty-water idea and his map → two true cases that only the water idea explains → the handle comes off and cholera stops → why the handle worked when the tar did not

## The retelling (the child who understands least, at home that evening)

> In Victorian London lots of people got really ill with cholera. Everyone thought the bad smell made them ill, so they burned tar to clean the air. A doctor called John Snow worked out it was the dirty water from one pump. The brewery men didn't drink the water and didn't get ill. They took the pump handle off and people stopped getting ill.

New things that child has to hold: disease, cholera, Soho in 1854, the street pump, bad air, burning tar, John Snow, his map, the brewery men, Susannah Eley, the handle coming off, treatment. Twelve, most of them told through one place and one problem. Victorians, poor crowded homes and Lord Shaftesbury are not new: the starter brings them back.

## The lesson, told

> Think back to our Victorian children. Who was queen? Did the Victorians live before or after the Tudors? What jobs did poor children do, and why did they have to work?
> (Children answer the four starter questions, then check.)
> Poor Victorian families lived squashed together in towns and cities. Today we're going to one part of London called Soho, in the year 1854. Look at this street. Lots of families lived in small houses, all crammed together. There were no toilets that flushed. Toilet waste and rotting rubbish piled up in the yards, and the whole place smelled terrible.
> Nobody had a tap at home. Families filled buckets at a pump in the street, and fetching the water was often a child's job. You pushed the handle up and down, and water came out.
> In 1854 a disease called cholera spread through Soho. A disease is an illness, and some diseases spread, which means they pass from one person to another. Cholera made people very ill in their stomachs, very fast. More than 500 people died in ten days, and many of them were children. Doctors had no treatment that worked.
> Why do you think people blamed the smell?
> (Children say what they think.)
> Victorians thought bad air caused diseases. It made sense to them. The illness was worst in the streets where the smell was worst, and the water looked clear. So they burned tar in the streets, because they thought the thick smoke would clean the air.
> Now be a Victorian. You blame the bad air. Here are five things a family could do.
> (Pairs sort five cards: would a bad-air believer try this, or not even think of it? Sweep the rubbish, wash the smelly yard with buckets of water and move to the fresh countryside go with bad air. Boil your drinking water and stop drinking from the street pump go with "wouldn't think of it".)
> Look at the two cards nobody tried. One doctor did think of them. His name was John Snow. He thought cholera came from dirty water, not bad air. He'd noticed that cholera made people ill in their stomachs, not their chests. When you breathe something bad in, it's your chest that suffers. So he thought people must be swallowing something.
> He found out where everyone who died had lived, and later he showed it on this map. Each little black mark is one person. Where are most of the marks? Packed round one water pump, on Broad Street.
> (Children find where the marks crowd.)
> Most doctors still didn't believe him. So let's look at evidence he found, the way a historian would.
> (Pairs sort three true cards from 1854 into "fits the bad-air idea", "fits John Snow's dirty-water idea" or "fits both ideas", and say what in the card shows it. The brewery men breathed the smelly air but drank beer, and none died: water. Susannah Eley lived where the air was fresh but drank Broad Street water and died: water. The smelly streets round the pump: both, so that card alone can't settle it.)
> John Snow told the people in charge what he'd found, and they took the handle off the Broad Street pump. Nobody could drink its dirty water, and soon people stopped catching cholera from it. John Snow showed that cholera spread through dirty drinking water. Today our tap water is cleaned before it reaches us, and the treatment for cholera is simple: lots of clean water with a little salt and sugar, to put back the water the body has lost.
> So why did taking the handle off work, when burning tar didn't? Here's a good answer and a not-so-good one.
> (Children see the two answers, find what the good one says that the other doesn't, tell a partner their own explanation, then write it.)

Read back as the child who understands least: "Soho" and "brewery" would stop them, so each is explained in the sentence that brings it in (a part of London; a place where they made beer). "Pump" is pictured working (push the handle, water comes out), because the handle story means nothing without it. "Tar" is said with what it does here (it burns with thick, smelly smoke).

Amount and feel: one story, one place and one year, with Tudor plague left for another lesson. Three short stretches of teaching: the street, cholera and the bad-air idea (the longest, about seven minutes, broken by a question to the class); John Snow and his map (about four); the handle and today (about four). Each of the first two ends with children sorting cards at their tables or from the board, so nobody sits listening for long. The third is the end of the story and runs straight into the final writing, which is its use.

## Teacher orientation

Teacher orientation: Children follow one true story, cholera in Soho, London, in 1854. They find out that Victorians blamed the bad smell and burned tar, decide what someone who blamed the bad air would and wouldn't do, then work like John Snow and decide which idea real evidence from 1854 fits, and finish by explaining why taking the handle off the Broad Street pump stopped cholera when burning tar did not. The tricky move is seeing that the brewery men breathed the same smelly air and still stayed well; it is taught in "Bad air or dirty water?". From the slides: both sorts run on the board, children putting each card's letter under its heading. To print: the two sort sheets, one between two, and the worksheet, which is the final task.

## The slides, in order

### Victorian children (starter)

- **On the board:** four short questions. Who was the queen in Victorian times? Did the Victorians live before or after the Tudors? Name one job a poor Victorian child might do. Why did poor Victorian children go out to work?
- **The thought:** what do I remember about poor Victorian children's lives?
- **What happens:** children answer, then the check slide shows the answers (Queen Victoria; after; a mine, a factory, sweeping chimneys; their families needed the money).
- **Why it is here:** today's story happens to poor Victorian families in a crowded city, and the opening board stands on that.

### Word card: disease, treatment

Both words are on the board of the next beat, and the objective names both ideas (spread and treated). The script says these words are about to come up; the story then explains them.

### Why did Victorians blame the smell? (Teach)

- **On the board** (paced over several slides, a picture for each moment): `Victorian London was crowded, dirty and very smelly.` / `Families lived squashed together in small houses. Toilet waste and rotting rubbish piled up in the yards, and it smelled terrible.` / `Nobody had a tap at home. Families got their water from a pump in the street, by pushing its handle up and down.` / `In 1854, a disease called cholera spread through Soho, a part of London. More than 500 people died in ten days. Doctors had no treatment that worked.` / the question `Why do you think people blamed the smell?` / `People thought the smell was making them ill, because the illness was worst where the smell was worst. So they burned tar in the streets, to make smoke they thought would clean the air.` / star line: `Victorians thought bad air caused diseases like cholera.` The script says the fuller version (a child's job to fetch the water, many of the dead were children, the water looked clear).
- **The thought:** why would people who couldn't see anything wrong with the water blame the smell?
- **What happens:** the teacher tells the scene and the outbreak, asks the class why people blamed the smell, then gives the Victorians' own reasons and the tar.
- **You land:** the star line, and that the Victorians weren't silly: they were reasoning from what they could smell and see.
- **Why it is here:** children now hold the bad-air idea and what it made people do, which the next sort uses on new actions and the final task needs for the tar.

### What would you do if you blamed the smell? (Do, a sort)

- **On the board:** five cards and two headings. Cards: Sweep the rotting rubbish out of the yard. / Boil your drinking water before you drink it. / Throw buckets of water over the yard to wash the dirt away. / Stop drinking water from the pump in the smelly street. / Move away to the countryside for a while. (The washing card carries the word water and the pump card the word smelly, so the words cannot do the sorting.) Headings: `If you blamed the bad air, you would try this.` / `If you blamed the bad air, you wouldn't think of this.` The question: `You blame the bad air. Which of these would you try?`
- **The thought:** does this get rid of the bad air, or is it about something else?
- **What happens:** pairs place each card, letter under heading. The teacher goes through it: three are about the smell; the two left over are about the water, and nobody who blamed the air would think of them.
- **Levels:** from the slides, letters under each heading. Printed: the sort sheet, one between two, letters in the boxes. No real things.
- **You land / listen for:** the washing card. A child who puts it with "wouldn't think of this" is going by the word "water", not by what the action is for: ask what the water is being used to get rid of.
- **What it develops:** explaining what people in the past did from what they believed, history's central move, on actions the class has not been shown.
- **Why it is here:** the two cards nobody tried are exactly John Snow's idea, which opens the next Teach.

### What did John Snow think? (Teach)

- **On the board:** `A doctor called John Snow thought cholera came from dirty water, not bad air.` beside his portrait / `He noticed that cholera made people ill in their stomachs, not their chests. So he thought they were swallowing something, not breathing it in.` / with the map: `He found out where everyone who died had lived, and later marked them on this map. Each little black mark is one person.` / the question `Where are most of the marks on the map?` / `Most of the marks are packed round one water pump, on Broad Street.`
- **The thought:** if the water from one pump was to blame, where would the deaths be?
- **What happens:** the teacher links back to the two cards nobody tried, tells who John Snow was and why he suspected the water, then the class finds where the marks crowd.
- **Why it is here:** children now know the dirty-water idea and that the deaths crowded round one pump, which the evidence sort needs to tell the two ideas apart.

### Bad air or dirty water? (Do, a sort)

- **On the board:** three cards, each a true piece of evidence from 1854, and three headings: `It fits the bad-air idea.` / `It fits John Snow's dirty-water idea.` / `It fits both ideas.` The question: `Which idea does each card fit? What in the card shows you?`
  - The brewery men. More than 70 men worked at a brewery, where beer was made, right next to the Broad Street pump. They breathed the same smelly air. They drank beer, not water from the pump. None of them died of cholera.
  - Susannah Eley. Susannah Eley lived miles away, where the air smelled fresh. She liked the taste of the Broad Street pump water, so a bottle of it was taken to her every day. She caught cholera and died.
  - The smelly streets. Most of the people who died lived in the crowded, smelly streets around Broad Street.
- **The thought:** if bad air caused cholera, what should have happened here, and what really happened?
- **What happens:** pairs place each card and tell each other the words in the card that decided it. The teacher goes through it, brewery men first.
- **Levels:** from the slides, letters under each heading. Printed: the evidence sort sheet, one between two, letters in the boxes. No real things.
- **You land / listen for:** the brewery men under bad air because "they were in the smelly street" means the child has seen the smell and stopped: ask what they drank. The smelly streets fit both, and that is why John Snow needed the other two.
- **What it develops:** using evidence to tell two explanations apart, the subject's own work: what each idea predicts, checked against what really happened.
- **Why it is here:** each child has now used evidence to show the water idea is the one that fits, which the final explanation turns into a because.

### Word card: no new word. The handle and today follow straight on.

### What stopped cholera at the pump? (Teach)

- **On the board:** `John Snow got the handle taken off the Broad Street pump, so nobody could drink its water.` beside the handle-less pump / `Soon, people stopped catching cholera from that pump.` / beside a picture of someone being helped to drink: `Today, the treatment for cholera is clean water with a little salt and sugar.` / star line `John Snow showed that cholera spread through dirty drinking water.` The script adds that he told the people in charge, that our tap water is cleaned today, and why salt and sugar water helps.
- **The thought:** why would taking the handle off stop people catching cholera?
- **Why it is here:** the end of the story. Children now hold why the handle worked, which with the tar from the first Teach is the whole of the final explanation.

### Why did the handle work when the tar didn't? (Practise, the final task)

- **On the board (launch):** a good answer beside a not-so-good one, and the difference.
  - Good: `Cholera spread through dirty water from the Broad Street pump. People caught it when they drank the water. Burning tar didn't stop it, because the smoke only changed the air, and cholera wasn't in the air. Taking the handle off worked, because nobody could drink the dirty water any more.`
  - Not so good: `The Victorians burned tar in the streets. Then they took the handle off the pump. After that, people stopped getting cholera.`
  - The difference: `The good one says why, using because, for the tar and for the handle.`
- **On the board (task):** `Why did taking the handle off the pump stop cholera, when burning tar didn't?` with the success criteria and the words `dirty water`, `the air`, `because`.
- **The thought:** what did the tar change, what did the handle change, and which one touched the thing that was really spreading cholera?
- **What happens:** the models come off. Each child tells a partner why the tar didn't work and why the handle did; the partner asks "Why did that stop people getting ill?" Then they write.
- **Levels:** the worksheet is this task printed, a question with room to write and the three words; children write there instead of in books. Board version: the question and criteria, answered in books.
- **Listen for:** the jump from "they took the handle off" to "people got better" with no water in between. Ask what the handle stopped people doing.
- **Why it is here:** it is the objective: how cholera spread and how people tried to stop it, as one explanation.

## Read-back

By the end, children will explain that cholera spread through dirty drinking water, so taking the Broad Street pump's handle off worked where burning tar did not, because the lesson gives them the Victorian bad-air idea and what it led people to do, John Snow's dirty-water idea, and the 1854 evidence that only the water idea explains, evidenced by each child's sort of the brewery men and Susannah Eley and their written explanation.

Read from the other end: a child who missed the water idea puts the brewery men with bad air and writes "they burned tar and took the handle off", so both checks catch them. A child could not have said the clause after "because" before the lesson.

## Decisions the walk-through does not show

- **The learning:** that a disease can spread in a way people at the time could not see, so what they did about it depended on what they believed caused it. Victorians blamed bad air and burned tar; John Snow's evidence showed cholera was in the water, so stopping people drinking it worked. Kind: factual knowledge of one well-developed case (cause and remedy in one story), with the historian's move of telling two explanations apart by evidence. No idea is named in concepts: there is one case, told properly, not an idea met across several cases.
- **Objective and boundary:** the objective is used as written. "Spread" is met by one disease told properly (preferences, How Much Fits: one story, and the plural met by that). "Treated" is met by what people did to stop cholera then (tar, the handle) and how it is treated now (clean water, salt and sugar). Germs and bacteria stay out: Victorians did not know them, and the lesson does not need them. Tudor plague, the plague doctor, smallpox and vaccination stay out.
- **Prior knowledge:** lessons 2 to 5 of the unit (Tudor and Victorian children's work, Lord Shaftesbury, leisure) are taken as taught; the starter brings back the Victorians and poor families' lives, and the opening Teach says again that poor families lived crowded together, so a child who was away can follow. Nothing about disease is assumed.
- **Structure:** content-based (Teach then Do per chunk, then the final explanation). The nearest alternative, a source-led lesson on Snow's map alone, would teach reading a map and leave the bad-air idea and the tar untaught, which the objective's "treated" needs.
- **Dominant sticking point:** the bad-air idea itself, which feels like common sense to children too (things that smell horrible make you ill). Exposed in "Why did Victorians blame the smell?", used in "What would you do if you blamed the smell?", broken by evidence in "Bad air or dirty water?", retested in the final task, where a child still holding it cannot say why the tar failed. A second, smaller one: that Victorians were silly. Met once, in the first Teach, where the board says it made sense to them and why.
- **Independent evidence:** "Bad air or dirty water?" is each pair's own decision on evidence the lesson has not interpreted for them; the brewery men cannot be placed by the word "water" (it is not in the card's decisive line) and the smelly streets card pulls towards bad air. The final writing is written with a good one in mind (the model of this very task is shown first, by the teacher's ruling), so it is not claimed as unaided.
- **Success criteria:** three steps for the explanation (how cholera really spread; why the tar didn't stop it; why taking the handle off worked), on the final task slide only. The worksheet is the final task printed.
- **Pictures:** each moment of the first Teach has its picture (the crowded street, the pump, a person ill in bed, tar burning), then John Snow, his real map, the pump without its handle, and someone being helped to drink. The map is load-bearing: children find where the marks crowd from it.
- **Lesson question:** None. The final task asks the question the story builds to, and a separate question slide would add nothing.
- **Deliberate omissions:** the plan's ranking of reasons for spread, the plague doctor video, and the treatment-card match (they would bring a second story and a third disease into one lesson); the leaking cesspit under the pump and the sewers (each a further new thing the final answer does not need); an Apply (the final explanation already draws the whole story together, and "Bad air or dirty water?" was the fresh-case reasoning).
- **Amount:** three chunks of teaching. The third ("What stopped cholera at the pump?") stays because the final task cannot be answered without the handle coming off, it is short (one board and one line about today), and it leads straight into the final writing, which is its use. Timed as taught, by asking about each slide, the beats come to about 47 minutes, so the lesson runs about 50 to 52 with books out. The boards were cut back to the sentences each idea needs (the fuller telling moved to the script) to get there; the rest is the final explanation, which needs its 12 minutes. A flag tells the teacher, and names the smelly-streets card as the cut if time is short.
- **Reasoning:** the reasoning shape is "Bad air or dirty water?", testing each piece of evidence against what each idea predicts.
- **Flags for the teacher:** Lesson 6 read as the first unit's Lesson 6 (the plan has three units); the plan's Tudor plague, plague doctor, ranking of reasons and treatment cards left for another day; the lesson runs about 50 minutes taught by asking.
