# Year 4 English: punctuating direct speech (episode, about 25 minutes)

**Journey:** Leo's speech bubble → inverted commas hold only the words he really said → the mark at the end of his words stays inside, and a full stop turns into a comma → Leo's shout and Mum's reply, together → Aisha and Grandad at the duck pond, on their own → Grandad's and Aisha's bubbles turned back into sentences

**Teacher orientation:** Children see Leo's speech bubble from the class story turned into a sentence, so the inverted commas visibly do the bubble's job, then pick out spoken words themselves before you model how the sentence ends. They write the three story lines (two with you modelling, one together), punctuate four fresh lines about a trip to the duck pond, and finish by turning two speech bubbles into sentences with nothing on the board to help. The tricky move is the end of the spoken words: the question mark, exclamation mark or comma goes inside the closing inverted comma, and a full stop becomes a comma because the sentence carries on to say who spoke. It's taught on the My Turn (slide 3) and caught deliberately in the last task, where Aisha's bubble ends in a full stop.

---

## Slide 1. What do inverted commas do?

**On the board**

- A picture of Leo looking worried, with a speech bubble: `I can't find my shoe.`
- `When we write a story, there's no room to draw a bubble. So writers use inverted commas instead.`
- The sentence, large, with the inverted commas in orange: `“I can't find my shoe,” said Leo.`
- `The words inside are the words that came out of Leo's mouth. He didn't say "said Leo", so that stays outside.`
- Star fact: `Inverted commas do the same job as a speech bubble: they go around the words someone really said.`

**The thought:** Which part of the sentence was in Leo's bubble, and which part wasn't?

**What happens**

Say to children: Look at Leo. He's in a bit of a panic, isn't he? He's saying, I can't find my shoe. You already know how a speech bubble works. Whatever's inside it came out of his mouth. But when we're writing a story, we can't stop and draw a bubble every time somebody talks. So writers use these instead. They're called inverted commas. You might have heard them called speech marks, and that's the same thing. Look at where they are in the sentence. They open just before "I", and they close just after "shoe". That's exactly what was in Leo's bubble. Now look at "said Leo". Was that in his bubble? No. Leo didn't say "said Leo". That's the writer telling us who's talking, so it stays outside. When you write them, the opening ones sit up high and look like a little 66. The closing ones look like 99. So, the important bit: inverted commas do the same job as a speech bubble. They go around the words someone really said. Some of you might have spotted that something else changed at the end of Leo's words. Hold on to that. We're coming straight back to it.

Every child points to (or tells their partner) the first and last word inside the inverted commas.

Teacher info: keep the class on which words sit inside. The comma after "shoe" is the next slide's teaching, so acknowledge it if a child notices and move on.

**What you land:** Inverted commas do the same job as a speech bubble: they go around the words someone really said. "said Leo" isn't in the bubble, so it's outside.

**Why it's here:** It gives children the rule for where the inverted commas open and close, and it turns the teacher's speech bubble link into the reason. Slide 2 needs this to find the spoken words, and the bubble comes back as the final task on slide 7. The full stop that became a comma is left as a noticed question for slide 3.

## Slide 2. Which words go inside?

**On the board**

- `More from Leo's busy morning.`
- `1. We're going to be late said Mum`
- `2. Mum said it was by the door groaned Leo`
- `Write the words each person really said. Put inverted commas around them.`

**The thought:** Which of these words came out of the person's mouth, and where do they stop?

**What happens**

Say to children: Two more lines from Leo's morning. For each one, ask yourself: which words actually came out of the person's mouth? Write just those words, with your 66 at the start and your 99 at the end. Don't worry about the rest of the sentence yet. Look carefully at number 2. Who's doing the talking?

Every child writes the two sets of spoken words in inverted commas. Then take the answers.

Say to children: Number 2 catches a lot of people out. It says "Mum said", so it's tempting to think Mum's talking. But who groaned? Leo did. He's telling us what Mum said earlier, so all of these words came out of Leo's mouth: Mum said it was by the door.

**What you look for**

- 1: `“We're going to be late”`
- 2: `“Mum said it was by the door”`
- "said Mum" or "groaned Leo" written inside: back to the bubble on slide 1 (was that in the bubble?).
- Number 2 closed after "Mum said" or started at "it was": the child is hunting for the word "said" rather than asking who's speaking. Ask them whose mouth the words came out of.

**Why it's here:** Every child uses the slide 1 rule on fresh lines, including one where "said" sits inside the spoken words, so finding the words is secure before the ending is taught. Their answers stop at the last spoken word with no mark, which is exactly the gap slide 3 fills.

## Slide 3. My Turn: how do the spoken words end?

**On the board**

- `I can't find my shoe said Leo`
- `Have you looked under the bed asked Mum`
- `Leo's sentence isn't finished when his words stop. It carries on to say who spoke, so his full stop becomes a comma.`
- Success criteria:
  1. Find the words the person really said.
  2. Write “ before the first word they said.
  3. After their last word, add ? for a question, ! for a shout, or a comma instead of a full stop.
  4. Write ” straight after that mark.
  5. Carry on with who spoke, starting with a small letter, then a full stop.
- Clear space beside the two lines for writing them out live.

**The thought:** None: children watch the teacher model.

**What happens**

Say to children: Here's Leo's line from our story, with no punctuation at all. Step 1: find the words Leo really said. I can't find my shoe. Step 2: my 66 goes before "I". Now step 3, and this is the bit that catches people out. If Leo's words were on their own, they'd end with a full stop. I can't find my shoe, full stop. But this sentence isn't finished yet. It carries on and tells us who said it. So I don't use a full stop. I use a comma instead, straight after "shoe". Step 4: my 99 goes straight after that comma. See where the comma is? Tucked inside, with Leo's words, because it belongs to them. Step 5: "said Leo", with a small s, because we're still in the same sentence. And now the sentence really is finished, so here's my full stop.

Now Mum. Have you looked under the bed. Is Mum telling Leo something, or asking him? Asking. So her words need a question mark. Same steps. 66 before "Have". After "bed", the question mark. Stop. Some of you will want to put a comma in as well, or put the question mark after the 99. Neither. The question mark does the comma's job here, and it goes inside, because it's part of what Mum said. 99 straight after it. Then "asked Mum", and "asked" gets a small a, even though there's a question mark just before it. The sentence still isn't finished. Full stop.

Children watch, then read the two finished sentences back with you, pointing at the mark before each 99.

Teacher info: the small letter after a question mark cuts against what children already know (a question mark usually ends a sentence), so expect "Asked" with a capital on the next few slides.

**What you land**

- `“I can't find my shoe,” said Leo.`
- `“Have you looked under the bed?” asked Mum.`
- The mark at the end of the spoken words goes inside the 99, and a full stop becomes a comma because the sentence carries on.

**Why it's here:** It answers the question slide 1 left open and gives children the whole method on two lines that differ only in their end mark, so the comma case and the question case are both seen worked. Slide 4 has the class run the same steps on the shout case and on a comma case of their own.

## Slide 4. Our Turn

**On the board**

- `It's not there shouted Leo`
- `Your shoe might be in the car said Mum`
- The same five success criteria.

**The thought:** Is this person asking, shouting or just saying it, so which mark do their words need, and does it go before or after the 99?

**What happens**

Say to children: Leo's still looking. Let's do his line together. What did Leo really say? Where does the 66 go? How did he say it? He shouted. So which mark do his words need? And does the exclamation mark go before the 99 or after it? Tell your partner. Now write the whole sentence.

Every child writes the first sentence; take it back and write it up.

Say to children: Now Mum's line. Have a go at this one by yourself first, then check with your partner. Is Mum asking, shouting or just saying it? So what happens to the full stop her words would have?

Every child writes the second sentence on their own before comparing.

**What you look for**

- `“It's not there!” shouted Leo.`
- `“Your shoe might be in the car,” said Mum.`
- A full stop inside Mum's line: the swap hasn't landed. Go back to Leo's line on slide 3 and ask what the sentence does after his words.
- The comma or exclamation mark after the 99: point at Mum's question mark from slide 3, inside the 99 because it belongs to her words, and say the comma belongs in the same way.
- "Shouted" or "Said" with a capital: the sentence isn't finished yet.

**Why it's here:** It gives children the shout case with support and has each child decide the comma case alone, which is the check that tells you whether the class is ready. Slide 5 mixes all three cases with nobody deciding for them.

## Slide 5. Your Turn

**On the board**

- `Aisha and her grandad have gone to feed the ducks.`
- `1. Can we feed the ducks now asked Aisha`
- `2. Bread isn't good for ducks said Grandad`
- `3. Grandad said not to get too close whispered Aisha`
- `4. A duck just took my hat shouted Aisha`
- `Write each sentence with inverted commas and the right punctuation.`
- The same five success criteria.

**The thought:** What did each person really say, how did they say it, and have I put that mark inside the 99?

**What happens**

Say to children: New story now. Aisha and her grandad are at the duck pond. Four lines, and they're mixed up: some questions, a shout, and some just said. For each one, work out which words the person really said, then how they said them. Use the steps if you get stuck.

Every child writes the four sentences.

**What you look for:** the mark after the 99 on 2 and 3; "Asked" with a capital on 1; number 3 closed after "Grandad said".

**Why it's here:** Children run the whole method on a new scene with all three endings mixed, including a line where "said" sits inside the spoken words, so each child shows the full procedure. Slide 7 then takes the method somewhere these lines didn't: bubbles whose own marks have to change.

## Slide 6. Your Turn - check

**On the board**

- `1. “Can we feed the ducks now?” asked Aisha.`
- `2. “Bread isn't good for ducks,” said Grandad.`
- `3. “Grandad said not to get too close,” whispered Aisha.`
- `4. “A duck just took my hat!” shouted Aisha.`
- The mark before each 99 in orange.

**The thought:** Is my mark in the same place as the one on the board, inside the 99, and did I start with the right words?

**What happens**

Say to children: Check yours one mark at a time. Look at the end of the spoken words first. Is your mark there, and is it inside the 99? Then check your small letter on "asked", "said", "whispered" and "shouted". Number 3 is the tricky one. It says "Grandad said", but Grandad isn't talking. Aisha's whispering. All of those words are hers. And number 4... so much for not getting too close.

Every child checks and corrects each sentence.

**What you look for:** children who had the right mark in the wrong place on 2 or 3 fixing the order, not just adding a comma.

**Why it's here:** It shows each child where their mark sat, so the last task tests the rule after any wrong placement has been corrected. It sits beside the spine as the answers to slide 5.

---

## Slide 7. Turn the bubbles into sentences

**On the board**

- `The duck has dropped Aisha's hat in the pond.`
- Grandad with a speech bubble: `Where's your hat now?`
- Aisha with a speech bubble: `It's floating in the middle of the pond.`
- `1. Write what Grandad said as a sentence. Say who said it.`
- `2. Write what Aisha said as a sentence. Say who said it.`
- No success criteria on this slide.

**The thought:** The words in Aisha's bubble end with a full stop. What happens to it when the sentence carries on to say who spoke?

**What happens**

Say to children: Remember the duck that took Aisha's hat? It's dropped it in the pond. Grandad and Aisha are talking in speech bubbles, like Leo was at the start. Now you're the writer. Turn each bubble into a sentence with inverted commas, and say who said it. Look carefully at what's in each bubble, because it tells you how they said it. This one is just you, so the steps aren't on the board.

Every child writes two sentences.

Teacher info: the success criteria come off for this task only, because it's the check on whether children hold the method. They're back for anything that follows.

**What you look for**

- `“Where's your hat now?” asked Grandad.`
- `“It's floating in the middle of the pond,” said Aisha.`
- Aisha's full stop copied inside the 99, or copied after it: the full stop-to-comma swap hasn't landed. Put Leo's line from slide 3 back up and ask what his full stop turned into and why.
- The comma or question mark after the 99: the mark hasn't been seen as part of the words. Put the bubble beside the sentence: everything in the bubble goes inside.
- "Asked" or "Said" with a capital after the bubble's mark.

**Why it's here:** It is the end check. The bubbles hand over the words but show the wrong mark for a sentence that carries on, so copying what's there gives the wrong answer, and only a child who holds the rule from slide 3 gets Aisha's sentence right. It also closes the loop on the teacher's speech bubble link from slide 1, run backwards.

## Slide 8. Turn the bubbles into sentences - check

**On the board**

- `1. “Where's your hat now?” asked Grandad.`
- `2. “It's floating in the middle of the pond,” said Aisha.`
- The question mark and the comma in orange.

**The thought:** What did I do with the full stop in Aisha's bubble?

**What happens**

Say to children: Look at Grandad's first. His question mark came straight across from the bubble, inside the 99, then "asked" with a small a. Now Aisha's. Her bubble had a full stop. In the sentence, it's a comma. Why? Because the sentence hasn't finished. It carries on to tell us who said it. If you wrote "replied Aisha" or "sighed Aisha", that's fine, as long as it starts with a small letter.

Every child checks both sentences and corrects Aisha's if needed.

**What you land:** The mark at the end of someone's words stays inside the inverted commas, and a full stop becomes a comma when the sentence carries on.

**Why it's here:** It shows each child the answer to the task that tests the lesson's sticking point, and it names the rule once more at the point children have just used it. It sits beside the spine as the answers to slide 7.

## Read-back

By the end, children will punctuate a line of speech followed by who said it, because the episode gives them the speech bubble's job for inverted commas (only the words really said go inside) and replaces the idea that the end mark goes after the closing inverted comma or that the full stop stays, with the rule that the spoken words' own mark goes inside and a full stop becomes a comma when the sentence carries on, evidenced by each child turning two speech bubbles, one ending in a question mark and one in a full stop, into sentences with no success criteria on the board.

## Decisions the walk-through doesn't show

- **The learning:** a method (punctuating speech that comes before who said it) resting on one idea children must hold, not recite: inverted commas mark exactly what came out of the speaker's mouth, and the end mark belongs to those words. A child can't say the full stop-to-comma swap before this episode; it's new.
- **Objective and boundary:** the objective as written, served through the authorised learning. Deferred: a new line for a new speaker (the end check numbers the two sentences so children write them separately and the rule never arises), speech after the reporting clause, speech split around "said", and commas inside the speech. The small letter on "said" and "asked" is included because it's the same fact as the comma: the sentence carries on.
- **Prior knowledge and structure:** capitals, full stops, question marks, exclamation marks and speech bubbles are the visible foundation (the bubble opens the episode; the end marks are named by what children already know). Inverted commas are treated as new. Skill-based, because this is a punctuation move children must perform reliably; the one idea underneath it gets a short Teach and its own Do before modelling. Content-based was the nearest alternative and would have treated the rule as knowledge to hold rather than a move to practise.
- **Case coverage:** the three supplied story lines give the three endings. Lines 1 and 2 (comma and question mark) are modelled on slide 3, changing only the end mark; line 3 (exclamation) and a second comma case are guided on slide 4, with the comma case, the hardest, decided by each child. Your Turn mixes all three plus the "said inside the spoken words" case first met on slide 2.
- **Dominant sticking point:** the end mark placed after the closing inverted comma, or a full stop kept where a comma belongs. Noticed on slide 1, taught on slide 3, checked on slide 4 (Mum's car line), practised on slide 5 and retested on slide 7, where the bubble's own full stop is the trap. Secondary: "said Leo" inside the inverted commas, and closing at the word "said" when it sits inside the speech (slides 1, 2 and Your Turn item 3). The capital after a question mark gets one line on slide 3 and a look-for after that, nothing more.
- **Independent evidence:** slide 7. A child who puts marks outside, keeps the full stop or capitalises "asked" writes a visibly different sentence from one who holds the rule, and the bubble's full stop means copying the marks produces the wrong answer. The success criteria come off for this task only, as it's the unaided check; the words are supplied, so every child can start by writing the inverted commas.
- **Success criteria:** five how-to steps in the child's words with the literal marks in them (“ and ”), identical on slides 3, 4 and 5, off the check slides and the end task. The mark before each 99 is the part picked out in orange on the answer slides. No worksheet in this episode.
- **Pictures and representations:** speech bubbles with the story characters (Leo, Mum) and the duck pond pair (Aisha, Grandad) are load-bearing: the bubble beside the sentence is how children see that inverted commas do the bubble's job, and the bubble is the source for the end check. No photographs are needed.
- **Teacher requirement:** "inverted commas do the same job as the bubble" is the star fact on slide 1 and is run in reverse on slide 7.
- **Supplied text:** all three story lines are used exactly as given. The extra lines for Leo and Mum stay general (a rushed morning) so they won't contradict the class story; the independent work moves to a new scene so it's genuinely fresh.
- **Reasoning:** the reasoning in a punctuation episode is deciding whose words are whose when "said" appears inside the speech (slide 2, Your Turn item 3) and deciding what a bubble's own mark must become (slide 7). No separate reasoning prompt, as a convince-me on top would add time without adding a decision.
- **Light moment:** the material handed over a small story with a turn in it: Grandad warns Aisha not to get too close, and in the next line a duck steals her hat. It's on the board as Your Turn items 3 and 4, with a callback line in the slide 6 script and the hat floating in the pond on slide 7.
- **Deliberate omissions:** no vocabulary slide, as "inverted commas" is taught in its own landed line on slide 1 and used on every slide after; no separate hinge slide, since Mum's car line on slide 4 does that job; no discussion of "speech marks" versus "inverted commas" beyond one spoken line.
- **Flags for the teacher:** the invented lines for Leo and Mum (slides 2 and 4) assume a rushed morning hunt for the shoe; swap them if they clash with what happens in the class story.
