# Year 4 English: inverted commas (episode, about 25 minutes)

**Journey:** a speech bubble shows Leo's exact words → in a story, inverted commas sit where the bubble's edges were → find exactly where someone starts and stops talking → when "said Leo" follows, the full stop turns into a comma, inside → a question mark or exclamation mark stays as it is, inside → mixed sentences alone, where the word after the speech won't tell you the mark → your own lines for Mum and Leo

**Teacher orientation:** This episode moves the class from speech bubbles to inverted commas. The first slide shows Leo's bubble beside the same words written with inverted commas, so children see the marks doing the bubble's job, and then they draw bubbles round the spoken words in two new sentences. You model one line from the class story, the class does the other two with you, and then children punctuate four mixed sentences on their own and finish by writing one thing Mum asks and one thing Leo says back. The tricky move is the mark at the end of the spoken words: it always goes inside the closing inverted comma, a full stop turns into a comma because the sentence carries on with "said Leo", and a question mark or exclamation mark stays as it is with no comma added. You model it on the My Turn and meet the question mark trap on the Our Turn. Nothing needs preparing.

---

## Slide 1: How does a story show what Leo says?

**On the board**

- A drawing of Leo on his hands and knees looking under his bed, with a speech bubble: *I can't find my shoe.*
- Beside the picture: `In a comic, a speech bubble shows us the exact words Leo says.`
- Underneath: `Story books don't have bubbles. So writers use inverted commas instead, where the edges of the bubble would be.`
- The same words written large: `“I can't find my shoe.”` with the two marks coloured. A label with an arrow to each mark reads `inverted commas`; a bracket under the words reads `direct speech`.
- Star fact: `Inverted commas do the same job as a speech bubble. They go around the words someone actually says.`

**The thought:** If there's no bubble, which bits of the written line are showing me where Leo's talking starts and stops?

**What happens**

Say to children: You all know speech bubbles. Look at Leo. He's lost his shoe, and his bubble shows us exactly what he says: I can't find my shoe. Now, our class story isn't a comic. There isn't a single bubble in it. So how does the writer show us which words Leo actually said out loud? Look at the line underneath the picture. Can you see these two little marks, one right at the start and one right at the end? They're called inverted commas. Point to them with me. Imagine somebody has rubbed out Leo's bubble and only the two edges are left. That's what these are. The first one goes just before Leo starts talking, and it looks like a tiny 66 up high. The last one goes just after he stops, and it looks like a tiny 99. Everything in between is exactly what Leo said, in his own words, and we call that direct speech. So inverted commas do the same job as the speech bubble. They go around the words someone actually says.

Children look from the bubble to the written line, point to the two inverted commas, and say with their partner where Leo starts talking and where he stops.

Teacher info: Keep the full stop in the bubble and in the written line on this slide. The next slides turn it into a comma, and seeing it change is the point.

**What you land:** Inverted commas do the same job as a speech bubble: they go around the words someone actually says.

**Why it is here:** Children now know what inverted commas are for and that they sit where the bubble's edges were. Slide 2 has them draw that bubble inside real sentences, and steps 1 and 2 of the method on slide 3 put the inverted commas exactly at its edges.

## Slide 2: Where does the talking stop?

**On the board**

- `Write each sentence. Draw a bubble around the words someone actually says.`
- `We're going to miss the bus shouted Mum from the front door`
- `Mum said no whispered Leo`

**The thought:** Who is doing the talking here, and which words actually came out of their mouth?

**What happens**

Say to children: These sentences have no punctuation yet, so you're going to be the bubble finder. Write each one out, then draw a bubble around just the words someone actually says. Careful with the second one. Read it twice. Ask yourself who is doing the talking first.

If a child is stuck on the second: Who is whispering? So what did Leo whisper?

Every child writes both sentences and draws a bubble in each.

**What you look for**

- Sentence 1: the bubble goes around `We're going to miss the bus` and stops before `shouted`. `from the front door` is outside, because it tells us where Mum was, not what she said.
- Sentence 2: the bubble goes around `Mum said no`. Leo is the one whispering, and what he whispers is "Mum said no".
- A bubble around just `no` tells you the child is hunting for the word *said* instead of asking who is talking. Ask them: who is whispering? Then everything Leo whispered goes in his bubble.
- A bubble that swallows `shouted Mum` means they haven't yet separated the talking from the telling. Point back to Leo's bubble on slide 1: it doesn't say "said Leo" in it.

**Why it is here:** Children can now find the exact edges of someone's words, even when the sentence carries on afterwards or the speech has the word *said* inside it. The My Turn puts the inverted commas exactly where these bubbles start and stop.

## Slide 3: My Turn: adding who said it

**On the board**

- The line from the story, with no punctuation: `I can't find my shoe said Leo`
- Leo's bubble from slide 1, small, beside it: *I can't find my shoe.*
- Clear space underneath for the sentence to be written live.
- Success criteria:
  1. Find the words the person actually says.
  2. Put {{inverted commas}} just before the first word they say.
  3. End their words with the right mark:
     - Is it a question? Use ?
     - Is it an exclamation? Use !
     - Neither? Use <<a comma, not a full stop>>.
  4. Close the {{inverted commas}} straight after that mark.
  5. Carry on with who said it. End with a full stop.

**The thought:** None. The class watches the whole method once.

**What happens**

Say to children: Here's the first line of our story with no punctuation at all. I can't find my shoe said Leo. Watch me. Step one. Which words does Leo actually say? The same as his bubble: I can't find my shoe. So step two, my first inverted commas go just before I, up high, like a little 66. Now I write his words. I can't find my shoe. Right, step three, and this is the important bit. In his bubble there's a full stop after shoe. But look at my sentence. It hasn't finished. It carries on: said Leo. A full stop means the sentence has ended, and this one hasn't, so the full stop turns into a comma. Is it a question? No. An exclamation? No. So it's a comma. And where does the comma go? It's the end of Leo's words, so it stays with his words, inside, just like the full stop was inside his bubble. Comma. Step four, now I close the inverted commas, up high, like a 99. Stop. This is where a lot of people slip. Some of you will want to close the inverted commas first and pop the comma after them, out here. But that comma belongs to Leo's words, so it goes in with them. Step five. Carry on with who said it: said Leo. Small s, because we're still in the same sentence. Now the sentence really has finished, so here's my full stop. Let's read it together.

The teacher writes the sentence live while the class watches.

**What you land:** `“I can't find my shoe,” said Leo.`

Look for: comma tucked inside the closing inverted comma; no full stop after *shoe*; small s on *said*.

**Why it is here:** Children have seen the whole method once, with the hardest new move named out loud: the full stop turns into a comma and stays inside. The Our Turn takes the other branch of step 3, where the mark doesn't change.

## Slide 4: Our Turn: which mark goes inside?

**On the board**

- `Punctuate each sentence.`
- `Have you looked under the bed asked Mum`
- `It's not there shouted Leo`
- The same success criteria as slide 3.

**The thought:** What kind of sentence did Mum say, and does her question mark stay with her words or go at the very end?

**What happens**

Say to children: Let's do the next line together. Have you looked under the bed asked Mum. Step one. Which words does Mum actually say? Tell your partner.

Yes, have you looked under the bed. So where do the first inverted commas go? Just before Have.

Step three. Is Mum's sentence a question, an exclamation or neither? How do you know?

It's a question. She's asking Leo something. So which mark? A question mark.

Now, here's where lots of people go wrong. A question mark usually sits at the very end of a sentence, so it's really tempting to put it after Mum. But who's asking the question? Mum. And which words are the question? Have you looked under the bed. So the question mark stays with her words, inside, where it would be in her bubble. Do we need a comma as well? No. The question mark is already doing that job. Close the inverted commas. Then carry on: asked Mum. Small a, even though there's a question mark just before it, because the sentence still hasn't finished. Full stop.

Now the last line of our story: It's not there shouted Leo. Have a go at this one while I watch. Which words does Leo say? What kind of sentence is it? How does shouted help you? Where does the mark go? Is the next word a capital?

Every child writes both sentences, the first alongside the teacher and the second on their own before it's shared.

**What you land:**

- `“Have you looked under the bed?” asked Mum.`
- `“It's not there!” shouted Leo.`

Teacher info: A question mark after *Mum* means the child is holding "question marks go at the end of the sentence"; point back to where it would be in Mum's bubble. `?,` means they've learned "always a comma before *said*"; the ? or ! replaces the comma. A capital A on *Asked* comes from knowing that ? ends a sentence, so say again that this sentence carries on.

Look for: ? or ! inside the closing inverted comma; no comma added after it; small a and small s. `“It's not there,” shouted Leo.` is also correct; the exclamation mark shows how he said it.

**Why it is here:** The class has now put each kind of end mark inside and seen that ? and ! don't change and don't get a comma added. The Your Turn mixes all three cases, so children have to decide the mark each time rather than repeat the last one.

## Slide 5: Your Turn: what happened next

**On the board**

- `Write each sentence with inverted commas and the right punctuation.`
- `Where did you last have it said Mum`
- `I had it in the garden said Leo`
- `The dog has got it shouted Leo`
- `Is it still a shoe asked Mum`
- The same success criteria.

**The thought:** Is what this person says a question, an exclamation or neither, even when the word after it is just *said*, and so which mark goes inside?

**What happens**

Say to children: I've made up what might happen next in Leo's morning. Four sentences, all mixed up. For each one, read the words the person actually says and decide what kind of sentence it is before you choose the mark. The steps are there if you get stuck.

Every child writes all four sentences on their own.

Look for: a comma in the first sentence (choosing the mark from *said* rather than from what Mum says); a full stop kept after *garden*; any mark sitting outside the closing inverted comma.

**Why it is here:** Each child uses the whole method alone on cases in no pattern. The first two both use *said* but need different marks, so the teacher can see who is deciding from the spoken words. The check slide gives everyone the right version before they write their own lines.

## Slide 6: Your Turn: what happened next - check

**On the board**

- `“Where did you last have it?” said Mum.`
- `“I had it in the garden,” said Leo.`
- `“The dog has got it!” shouted Leo.`
- `“Is it still a shoe?” asked Mum.`

**The thought:** Is each of my marks the right one, and is it tucked inside my inverted commas?

**What happens**

Say to children: Check your marks one at a time. First, is it the right mark? Then, is it tucked inside the inverted commas? Number one says said Mum, but what Mum says is a question, so it gets a question mark. If you put a comma there, that's the one to fix. And number four. Honestly, Mum, I'm not sure it is still a shoe.

Children tick or correct each sentence.

Teacher info: `“The dog has got it,” shouted Leo.` is also correct. A comma in number one means the child chose the mark from the word *said*; send them back to step 3 and ask what Mum actually says. A full stop in number two means they still treat the spoken words as a finished sentence; ask whether the sentence has ended yet.

**Why it is here:** The class has the right version of every case before writing their own, and the teacher knows which of the three slips to watch for in the last task.

---

## Slide 7: What do Mum and Leo say next?

**On the board**

- A drawing: Leo holding up a soggy, chewed trainer, the dog wagging its tail, Mum in the doorway.
- `Leo has got his shoe back. It's soggy and chewed.`
- `What does Mum ask?`
- `What does Leo say back?`
- `Write one sentence for each, with inverted commas and who said it.`
- The same success criteria.

**The thought:** Is the sentence I've made up a question, an exclamation or neither, so which mark goes inside my inverted commas?

**What happens**

Say to children: Good news. Leo's got his shoe back. Bad news. Look at it. This time nobody's giving you the words, you're making them up. First, picture Mum's speech bubble. What would she ask? Now picture Leo's. What would he say back? Tell your partner. Then write each one the way a story would, with inverted commas and who said it. Keep each person to one sentence. And before you add the mark at the end of their words, ask yourself: is this a question, an exclamation or neither?

Every child writes two sentences of their own.

Teacher info: Keep the speech first and who said it afterwards. If a child writes `Mum asked, ...` or splits one person's words in two, that's a later lesson, so steer them to one sentence with the words first. A child who starts Mum's line with Leo's name (`Leo, what happened?`) is also reaching past today; suggest a sentence without the name.

**What you look for:** A model if you want one: `“Why is it all wet?” asked Mum.` and `“The dog was using it as a pillow,” said Leo.` Look for: the mark inside the closing inverted comma; a comma, not a full stop, when Leo's line is neither a question nor an exclamation; small letters on *asked* and *said*.

**Why it is here:** This is the final performance. Each child runs the whole method on words they chose, so the decision about the mark comes from their own sentence rather than one handed to them.

---

**Read-back:** By the end, children will punctuate a line of speech followed by who said it, because the lesson gives them the idea that inverted commas do a speech bubble's job around only the words spoken, and replaces the wrong rules that the end mark goes outside, stays a full stop, or waits until the end of the whole sentence with one decision (a ? or ! stays, anything else becomes a comma, and it goes inside), evidenced by four mixed sentences where two use *said* but need different marks, then two lines of their own speech.

## Decisions the walk-through does not show

- **The learning:** children will know that inverted commas replace a speech bubble around exactly the spoken words, and be able to decide the mark that ends those words and put it inside before who said it. It is a method with one decision in it; the bubble is the idea that makes the method make sense rather than a rule to remember.
- **Objective and boundary:** the objective as written. The end performance is a speech-first sentence with who said it afterwards. Deferred, as the brief says: a new line for a new speaker and who-said-it before the speech. Also kept out: speech split in two, and commas before a name inside speech (steered away from in the last task).
- **Prior knowledge and structure:** capital letters, full stops, question marks, exclamation marks and speech bubbles are the foundation; inverted commas are treated as new. Skill-based with one cycle, because every case runs the same five steps and only the choice in step 3 changes. Two cycles (comma case, then ? and ! case) was the nearest alternative, but it would split one method in half; instead the My Turn models the comma case and the Our Turn carries the question, the case with the strongest wrong rule. A first try before modelling was rejected because a wrongly punctuated sentence looks fine to a child and would only rehearse the error.
- **Sticking point:** the mark at the end of the spoken words. Named out loud on slide 3 (comma outside the inverted commas), met on slide 4 (question mark at the very end, a comma added after ?, a capital after ?), retested on slide 5 (sentences one and two) and slide 7.
- **Independent evidence:** slide 5 cannot be passed by reading the word after the speech, because sentences one and two both say *said* and need different marks, and the four cases come in no pattern. Slide 7 gives no start form at all.
- **Success criteria:** five how-to steps with the three-way choice written as questions inside step 3, identical from slide 3 to slide 7. Worth drawing live and keeping on the wall, because later direct speech lessons add to this same method.
- **Pictures:** the drawing of Leo and his bubble carries the teacher's requirement that children see inverted commas doing the bubble's job, and comes back small on the My Turn. The chewed-shoe drawing on slide 7 gives children something to make up speech from. No photograph is needed.
- **Left out on purpose:** a separate vocabulary slide, because *inverted commas* and *direct speech* are labelled on the example on slide 1 where they mean something; the term *reporting clause*, which children meet as "who said it"; a judge-the-claim beat about the question mark, because slide 4 already confronts it and a third pass would rehearse the same sentence.
- **Reasoning:** the reasoning in this objective is the step 3 decision made against a misleading cue, which is sentence one on slide 5; a separate convince-me task would add time without a new decision.
- **Light moment:** the chewed shoe. `Is it still a shoe asked Mum` as the last Your Turn sentence, one aside on the check slide, and the soggy shoe on slide 7.
- **Flags for teacher:** slides 5 and 7 make up what happens next in the class story (the dog has the shoe). If that clashes with the real book, swap the words, keeping sentence one a question with *said* and sentence two a statement with *said*.
