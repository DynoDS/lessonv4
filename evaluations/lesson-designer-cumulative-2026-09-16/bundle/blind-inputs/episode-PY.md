# Evaporation and temperature: episode walk-through (Year 4 Science)

**Journey:** 40 ml has gone from a saucer → it went into the air as water vapour → a tight lid would keep it in → three saucers, warm to cold → how much water each one really lost → which sentence the numbers prove → same two days, so the warmest was fastest → Leo's towels

**Teacher orientation:** The class meets one saucer on a sunny windowsill that lost 40 ml of water in two days, finds out that the water turned into water vapour and went into the air (that's evaporation), and predicts what a tight lid would change. They then work out how much water evaporated from three saucers left somewhere warm, somewhere in between and somewhere cold, decide which sentence those numbers prove, and finish by judging Leo's claim about two wet towels in writing. The tricky move is reading the results: the table shows the water left in each saucer, not the water that evaporated, so the fridge saucer's 48 ml means only 2 ml went into the air. That move is taught on slide 5, with the windowsill row already worked. Nothing needs preparing beyond something for children to write on; the written end check is slides 8 and 9.

## Slide 1: Where did 40 ml of water go?

**On the board**
- A photograph of a saucer of water on a sunny windowsill.
- `This saucer had 50 ml of water in it. It was left on a sunny windowsill for two days.`
- `After two days, there was only 10 ml left. Nobody touched it.`
- Question: `Where did the other 40 ml of water go?`

**The thought:** Nobody touched the saucer, so where could 40 ml of water have gone?

**What happens**
Say to children: Have a look at this saucer. It's sitting on a sunny windowsill. At the start, somebody measured 50 ml of water into it. That's about ten teaspoons. Then they left it alone for two whole days. Nobody knocked it, nobody poured any out, and nobody drank it. After two days they measured it again, and there was only 10 ml left. So 40 ml of water has gone from that saucer. That's about eight teaspoons. Where did it go? Have a think on your own first. Got an idea? Now tell your partner where you think the 40 ml went.

Every child settles an idea, then tells their partner. Take two or three ideas and don't correct them yet: "Hold on to your idea. We're about to find out."

Teacher note: a china saucer doesn't soak up water, so if a child says it soaked in, that's a sensible guess you can rule out straight away.

**What you look for:** Ideas like `it disappeared`, `the sun dried it up`, `it soaked into the saucer`. Each one is the idea slide 2 has to replace. `It dried up` is the everyday word for what happened, so ask "dried up to where?". A child who says `into the air` already has the idea; bring them in on the next slide.

**Why it's here:** It opens on a real result that makes the question worth asking before anything is explained. Slide 2 answers this exact question, and the 40 ml comes back as the worked row on slide 5.

## Slide 2: The water went into the air

**On the board**
- The same saucer photograph.
- `You know that when water boils, it turns into a gas. Water can also turn into a gas without boiling. It just happens much more slowly.`
- `This gas is called water vapour. You can't see it, but it's in the air all around us. So the 40 ml of water hasn't disappeared.`
- Question: `Look at the saucer. Which part of the water is touching the air?`
- Star fact: `Evaporation is when liquid water turns into water vapour, an invisible gas in the air.`

**The thought:** If I can't see it, where has the water gone, and how did it get out of the saucer?

**What happens**
Say to children: You already know that when water boils, it turns into a gas. Water can also turn into a gas without boiling. It just happens much, much more slowly, a tiny bit at a time. The gas it turns into is called water vapour. You can't see water vapour. It's invisible. But it's there, mixed into the air all around us, including the air in this room right now. So that 40 ml of water didn't disappear. Water can't just stop existing. It turned into water vapour and went into the air. Look at the saucer. Which part of the water is touching the air? Yes, the top. That's where the water escapes from, a little bit at a time. This change has a name. When liquid water turns into water vapour, we call it evaporation. The water in the saucer evaporated. Say it with me: evaporated.

Every child looks for the top of the water on the photograph and answers the question; the class says the new word.

Teacher note: the white cloud above a boiling kettle isn't water vapour itself; it's tiny drops of water forming as the vapour cools. Water vapour is always invisible. If a child says "steam is water vapour you can see", tell them the gas itself is invisible and the cloud is tiny drops. Keep it to that; how vapour turns back into water is later learning.

**What you land:** `Evaporation is when liquid water turns into water vapour, an invisible gas in the air.` The class leaves the slide able to say where the 40 ml went: into the air, as water vapour.

**Why it's here:** It gives the class the idea the lid prediction on slide 3 runs on, and it teaches evaporation and water vapour, the words the objective and every later slide use. Part (b) of the end check on slide 9 asks for this idea again on a new case.

## Slide 3: What if the saucer had a lid?

**On the board**
- A picture of the same saucer of water on the sunny windowsill, with a clear lid fitted tightly over it.
- `Imagine the same saucer with 50 ml of water, on the same sunny windowsill, for two days. This time, a clear lid fits tightly over the top.`
- Question: `After two days, what will you find?`
- `A: There's a lot less water, just like before.`
- `B: Most of the water is still there.`
- `Choose A or B. Then explain why.`

**The thought:** If the water goes into the air, what happens when a lid stops it getting out?

**What happens**
Say to children: Same saucer. Same 50 ml of water. Same sunny windowsill, same two days. But this time we put a clear lid over it, and it fits tightly, so no air can get in or out. The lid's see-through, so the sun still shines on the water. After two days, what will you find? A, there's a lot less water, just like before. Or B, most of the water is still there. Decide on your own first. Write your letter, and underneath it, why you think that. ... Now tell your partner your letter and your reason.

Every child commits to A or B with a reason before talking. Take reasons for both letters. Then say: If you tried this, you'd find almost all the water still in the saucer. The water vapour had nowhere to go. It was stuck under the lid.

Teacher note: children who try this at home will often see little drops on the inside of the lid. That's the trapped water vapour turning back into water. If someone mentions it, a quick "yes, that's the water vapour, caught" is enough.

**What you look for:** B, with a reason like `the water vapour can't get out into the air` or `it's trapped under the lid`. A child who picks A because `the sun still dries it up` is still thinking the water vanishes, so ask them where the water goes when it evaporates. A child who picks B because `the sun can't get to it` has the right letter for the wrong reason; point out that the lid is see-through and ask what the lid actually stops.

**Why it's here:** It's the first time children use the idea on a case they haven't been shown, and a child who thinks evaporated water vanishes predicts A, so the answers show who has the idea. The same idea, that the water is in the air, is what part (b) of the towel check on slide 9 needs.

## Slide 4: Three saucers, three places

**On the board**
- Three pictures in a row: a saucer on a sunny windowsill, a saucer on a classroom shelf, a saucer in a fridge.
- `Three saucers were set up the same way. Each one had 50 ml of water and was left for two days.`
- `What changed was where each saucer was left: somewhere warm, somewhere in between, and somewhere cold.`
- Results table:

| Where the saucer was | Water at the start | Water left after two days |
|---|---|---|
| Sunny windowsill | 50 ml | 10 ml |
| Classroom shelf | 50 ml | 35 ml |
| Fridge | 50 ml | 48 ml |

- Question: `Which place was the warmest? Which was the coldest?`

**The thought:** What was the same for all three saucers, and what was different?

**What happens**
Say to children: Our windowsill saucer wasn't the only one. Three saucers were set up at the same time. They were the same saucers, with the same 50 ml of water in each, and they were all left for the same two days. The one thing that was different was where they were left. One went on the sunny windowsill, one on a shelf in a classroom, and one in a fridge. Here's how much water was left in each after two days. Which of these three places was the warmest? And which was the coldest? ... Yes. The sunny windowsill was the warmest, the fridge was the coldest, and the classroom shelf was somewhere in between.

Every child answers the question about warmest and coldest.

Teacher note: the windowsill saucer also had sunshine falling on it, and the air in a fridge is very still. The sunshine is what makes the windowsill the warmest place, so it's part of the same story. If a child spots it, that's good thinking about a fair test, and it doesn't change today's conclusion.

**What you land:** Same saucers, same amount of water, same two days. The windowsill was warmest and the fridge was coldest.

**Why it's here:** It puts the evidence in front of the class and fixes what all three saucers had in common. Slide 7 depends on that: because they all had the same two days, the saucer that lost the most water was evaporating the fastest.

## Slide 5: How much water went into the air?

**On the board**
- The results table with a new column:

| Where the saucer was | Water at the start | Water left after two days | Water that evaporated |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sunny windowsill | 50 ml | 10 ml | 50 ml - 10 ml = 40 ml |
| Classroom shelf | 50 ml | 35 ml | ? |
| Fridge | 50 ml | 48 ml | ? |

- `Watch out! The table shows the water left in each saucer. We want the water that went into the air.`
- Task: `Work out how much water evaporated from the saucer on the shelf and the saucer in the fridge.`

**The thought:** How much of this water went into the air, not how much stayed in the saucer?

**What happens**
Say to children: Right, the important bit. This table tells us how much water was left in each saucer. But we want to know how much water evaporated, how much went into the air. They're not the same thing. We already did the windowsill one. It started with 50 ml and had 10 ml left, so 50 take away 10 is 40 ml that evaporated. Watch out, because this catches a lot of people out: the fridge saucer has the biggest number, 48 ml, but that's the water that stayed. Now you do the other two. How much water evaporated from the saucer on the shelf, and how much from the saucer in the fridge?

Every child works out and writes both amounts.

Teacher note: go through 15 ml and 2 ml with the class before slide 6, because the sentences there are judged from this column. A class that carries 35 ml and 48 ml forward will reach the opposite conclusion.

**What you look for:** Shelf 15 ml, fridge 2 ml. A child who writes 35 ml and 48 ml has copied the water left; point at the windowsill row, where 10 ml was left but 40 ml evaporated.

**Why it's here:** It turns the results into the amount of water that went into the air from each saucer. Slide 6 asks which sentence those amounts prove, and the end check on slide 8 asks children to use them.

## Slide 6: What do our results show?

**On the board**
- The completed table: water that evaporated is 40 ml from the sunny windowsill, 15 ml from the classroom shelf and 2 ml from the fridge.
- Question: `Which sentence do our results show?`
- `A: Water only evaporates in warm places.`
- `B: The warmer the place, the more water evaporated.`
- `C: The colder the place, the more water evaporated.`
- `Choose one of the other sentences. Which saucer shows it's wrong?`

**The thought:** Does this sentence fit every saucer, or does one saucer break it?

**What happens**
Say to children: Here are our amounts: 40 ml from the windowsill, 15 ml from the shelf and 2 ml from the fridge. Here are three sentences about what these results show. Only one of them fits every saucer. Check each sentence against the table, one saucer at a time. Write the letter of the sentence our results show. Then choose one of the other sentences and write which saucer shows it's wrong.

Every child writes a letter, then names a sentence and the saucer that breaks it.

**What you look for:** B. A is wrong because the fridge was the coldest place and its saucer still lost 2 ml. C is wrong because the fridge was the coldest and lost the least, while the windowsill was the warmest and lost the most. A child who chooses A thinks cold water doesn't evaporate at all, often because they link turning into a gas with boiling; point them to the fridge row. A child who chooses C is usually reading the water left column, so go back to the water that evaporated. For A, the fridge is the clean answer; a child who names the classroom shelf has a point, but it's arguable whether a classroom counts as warm.

**Why it's here:** Children reach "warmer, more evaporated" from their own numbers before anyone says it, which is what the teacher asked for, and they rule out "only in warm places" with the fridge saucer. Slide 7 takes their sentence B and turns "more water in the same time" into "faster".

## Slide 7: Which saucer was evaporating the fastest?

**On the board**
- The completed table, smaller.
- `All three saucers had exactly the same two days. So the saucer that lost the most water was evaporating the fastest.`
- `Even the fridge saucer lost 2 ml. Cold water still evaporates. It just happens very slowly.`
- Star fact: `The warmer the water, the faster it evaporates.`

**The thought:** They all had two days, so what does losing the most water tell me about speed?

**What happens**
Say to children: You chose B: the warmer the place, the more water evaporated. Now think about the time. All three saucers had exactly the same two days. So if the windowsill saucer lost 40 ml and the fridge saucer lost only 2 ml in the same time, which one was evaporating faster? The windowsill, by miles. The water there was warmer, so it was turning into water vapour much faster. And look at the fridge. Even in there, 2 ml got out into the air. Not exactly a quick escape, but it still happened. So water doesn't have to boil, and it doesn't even have to be warm, to evaporate. Cold water evaporates too. It just happens very slowly. So here's the rule our saucers showed us: the warmer the water, the faster it evaporates.

Every child listens and answers "which one was faster?" as you ask it.

**What you land:** `The warmer the water, the faster it evaporates.` Cold water still evaporates, slowly.

**Why it's here:** It turns the amounts into speed, which is what "how quickly" in the objective means, and it lands the rule the end check on slide 8 asks children to back up with the saucer numbers.

## Slide 8: Is Leo right about the hot day?

**On the board**
- A photograph of towels hanging on a washing line.
- `Two towels are just as wet as each other. One is hung outside on a hot, sunny day. The other is hung outside on a cold, cloudy day.`
- Leo says: `The towel on the hot day will dry quicker, because the sun makes the water disappear faster.`
- Question: `Is Leo right that the towel on the hot day will dry quicker? Use our saucer results to explain.`
- Sentence starter: `Our saucer results show...`
- The completed saucer results table, as a reference.

**The thought:** What do the windowsill and fridge saucers tell me about a towel on a hot day and a towel on a cold day?

**What happens**
Say to children: Leo's thinking about two wet towels on a washing line. One goes out on a hot, sunny day and one goes out on a cold, cloudy day. Leo's said two things, and we're going to check them one at a time. First, is he right that the towel on the hot day will dry quicker? Don't just tell me what you think. Use our saucer results to show it. The table's there to help you. If you're not sure how to start, begin with "Our saucer results show..." This one is on your own.

Every child writes their answer independently.

Teacher note: keep the two star facts off this slide and the next; the table stays because using it is the task.

**What you look for:** See slide 10 for the model. Look for 40 ml and 2 ml used as water that evaporated, not water left, and "warmer water evaporates faster" in the child's own words. `Yes, because the sun dries things` with no numbers is everyday sense rather than today's lesson, so ask which saucer it matches.

**Why it's here:** It's the end check on the rule, on a case the lesson never showed, and it can only be answered well by using the evidence from slides 5 to 7.

## Slide 9: Is Leo right about the water?

**On the board**
- The same towels photograph.
- Leo's speech bubble again: `The towel on the hot day will dry quicker, because the sun makes the water disappear faster.`
- Question: `Leo says the sun makes the water disappear. Is he right? Explain your answer.`

**The thought:** When a towel dries, where does the water actually go?

**What happens**
Say to children: Now the second thing Leo said. He says the sun makes the water disappear. Is he right? Explain your answer. If you're stuck, think back to the saucer with the lid.

Every child writes their answer independently.

**What you look for:** See slide 11 for the model. Look for `turns into water vapour` and `goes into the air`. `Yes, it dries up and it's gone` means the child still thinks the water vanishes; take them back to where the 40 ml went on slide 2.

**Why it's here:** It retests the lesson's main sticking point once, at the end, on a new case. Leo's claim mixes a right prediction with the wrong idea, so a child can't pass slides 8 and 9 by simply agreeing or disagreeing with him.

---

## Slide 10: Is Leo right about the hot day? - check

**On the board**
- `Yes. Warmer water evaporates faster. The saucer on the sunny windowsill was the warmest, and 40 ml of its water evaporated in two days. The fridge saucer only lost 2 ml. So the towel on the hot day will dry quicker.`

**The thought:** Did I use the water that evaporated, and did I say why warm makes it quicker?

**What happens**
Say to children: Here's a strong answer. Look at what it does. It says yes, it gives the rule, and then it proves it with two saucers, the warmest and the coldest. Check yours. Did you use the water that evaporated, 40 ml and 2 ml, and not the water left?

Every child checks their own answer against the model.

**What you look for:** Answers that used 10 ml and 48 ml as if they were the water that evaporated; that's the slide 5 mix-up still there.

**Why it's here:** It shows what using the evidence looks like, so children can see the difference between "the sun dries things" and an answer the saucers back up.

---

## Slide 11: Is Leo right about the water? - check

**On the board**
- `No. The water doesn't disappear. It evaporates, which means it turns into water vapour. Water vapour is a gas you can't see, and it goes into the air.`

**The thought:** Did I say where the water goes?

**What happens**
Say to children: And the second part. Leo's wrong about this bit. The water from the towel doesn't disappear. It turns into water vapour and goes into the air, just like the 40 ml from our saucer. You can't see it, but it's there. Check yours. Did you say where the water goes?

Every child checks their own answer against the model.

**What you look for:** Answers that say "it dries up" without saying where the water goes; ask "dried up to where?", the same question as slide 1.

**Why it's here:** It closes the episode on the idea the teacher most wanted secured: the water goes into the air, it doesn't vanish.

---

**Read-back:** By the end, children will explain, using the saucer results, that warmer water evaporates faster and that evaporated water goes into the air as water vapour, because the lesson gives them what evaporation is (liquid water turning into an invisible gas in the air) and how to read how much water each saucer lost in the same two days, replacing the idea that dried-up water has simply vanished, evidenced by the towel check on slides 8 and 9, where Leo's claim mixes a right prediction with that wrong idea and children must use the saucer amounts for one part and water vapour for the other.

## Decisions the walk-through doesn't show

- **The learning.** Children will know that when water dries up it hasn't vanished: it has turned into water vapour, an invisible gas in the air, and this is evaporation. They will understand that warmer water evaporates faster, and be able to show it from results by working out how much water was lost in the same time. The first is a fact about a process, taught once on a well-developed case (the windowsill saucer) and used on the lid. The second is an idea, a pattern that transfers, met on two instances with different evidence: the three saucers (slides 4 to 7) and the two towels (slide 8).
- **Objective and boundary.** Objective used as written: To explain how temperature affects how quickly water evaporates. End performance: judge Leo's claim, backing the rate with the saucer amounts and saying where the water goes. Deferred: why warmth speeds evaporation (particles are later learning), condensation and the water cycle (only touched in teacher notes about drops on a lid and the cloud above a kettle), and other things that change drying speed, such as wind and surface area.
- **Prior knowledge and structure.** Built on what the brief says the class holds: solids, liquids and gases, and water turning to gas when it boils. Slide 2 hangs evaporation on that ("water can also turn into a gas without boiling"). Content-based rather than Discovery: children can't discover an invisible gas or the word for it from results, so evaporation is taught directly first, and the discovery-like part, drawing the pattern from the numbers, sits inside the route as slides 5 and 6, which is how the teacher's "use the results rather than just be told" is honoured.
- **Sticking point.** The dominant one is "the water has disappeared". It's exposed on slide 1 (children's own ideas), taught on slide 2, checked on slide 3 (the lid), and retested once on slide 9. The secondary one is reading water left as water evaporated, which would turn the conclusion upside down; it's taught on slide 5 and the table stays in view through slide 8. A third, "water only turns into a gas when it's hot or boiling", gets one clean touch: sentence A on slide 6, ruled out by the fridge saucer, and one line on slide 7.
- **Independent evidence.** Slides 8 and 9, written alone. It can't be passed by a surface cue: agreeing with Leo gets part (b) wrong, disagreeing gets part (a) wrong, the star facts are kept off both slides, and part (a) asks for the saucer amounts rather than an everyday "the sun dries things".
- **Success criteria.** No separate criteria panel. The end check is two short questions whose instruction already says what a good answer uses ("Use our saucer results"), the table is the tool, and the sentence starter gives the weakest child a way in without the verdict.
- **Representations and pictures.** The saucer results table is load-bearing and grows across slides 4 to 8 (water left, then the water that evaporated column with the windowsill row worked, then complete). Pictures: the saucer on a sunny windowsill (slides 1 and 2), the saucer with a clear, tight lid (slide 3; the lid must look see-through, because that holds the sunshine the same and leaves only "can the vapour get out?" to change), the three places (slide 4), towels on a washing line (slides 8 and 9).
- **Reasoning.** Slide 6 is the reasoning beat: deciding which sentence the evidence supports and naming the saucer that rules another out. The end check carries it on as judging a claim that is half right.
- **Light moment.** The fridge saucer losing only 2 ml in two days: "Not exactly a quick escape, but it still happened", in the slide 7 script only.
- **Deliberate omissions.** No separate vocabulary slide: evaporation and water vapour are taught in the slide 2 sentences at the moment they're needed and used on every slide after. No live wet-streak demonstration: the saucer result already makes the case, and a streak that doesn't dry in time would argue against the lesson. No prediction of which saucer would lose most before the results: children would answer from everyday sense, not from anything taught. No extra "cold room" prediction after slide 7: it would ask the same thing as the towel check.
- **Flags for the teacher:** None.
