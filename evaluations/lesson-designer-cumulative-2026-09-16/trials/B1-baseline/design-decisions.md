# Design decisions: Year 4 Science, evaporation and temperature (episode, about 25 minutes)

**Objective:** To explain how temperature affects how quickly water evaporates.

**Journey:** a saucer that lost 15 ml with nobody touching it → water can turn into a gas without boiling → it's in the air as invisible water vapour → even in a fridge? → three saucers side by side: the warmest lost the most → same two days, so warmer means faster → working backwards from two cups → two puddles, on their own

**Teacher orientation:** Children use one set of results, three saucers that each started with 50 ml of water and sat for two days on a sunny windowsill, on a classroom shelf and in a fridge, to work out what evaporation is and how warmth changes it. On screen they meet the shelf saucer, then a results table they finish themselves, then two short cases to decide; they record a reason about Ben's claim (slide 4), the missing column of the results table (slide 5), a letter and a reason about two cups (slide 7), and three short answers about two puddles as the end check (slide 8). The tricky move is that water can turn into a gas without boiling, so the water hasn't vanished: it's in the air as invisible water vapour. That is taught on slides 2 and 3, tested straight away on Ben's fridge claim on slide 4, and checked again, with the facts off the board, on slide 8.

---

## Slide 1: What happened to this saucer?

**On the board:**
- The shelf saucer shown twice, side by side: `Monday: 50 ml` and `Wednesday: 35 ml`.
- `This saucer of water sat on a classroom shelf for two days. Nobody touched it.`
- Question (blue): `Where did 15 ml of water go?`

**The thought:** Nobody touched it, so where could that water have gone?

**What happens:**
Say to children: Have a look at this saucer. On Monday it had 50 ml of water in it, and it sat on a shelf in a classroom for two days. By Wednesday there was only 35 ml left. So 15 ml of water has gone somewhere. Nobody touched it. Nobody knocked it over. And before anyone asks, there isn't a class cat. So where did that water go? Tell your partner your best idea. You don't have to be sure, I just want to know what you think.

Every child tells their partner one idea. The teacher takes three or four ideas from around the room and writes nothing down as right or wrong yet.

Teacher info: Don't settle it here. The point is to get the class's own explanations out loud so the next two slides answer them.

**What you look for:** `It disappeared`, `it dried up`, `the saucer soaked it up`, `it went into the air`, and possibly `it evaporated`. `Disappeared` and `dried up` are the idea slides 2 and 3 replace. A child who says `evaporated` often can't say where the water went, so ask them.

**Why it's here:** It puts the wrong idea, that the water simply vanished, into the room in the children's own words, so slides 2 and 3 are answering a question the class has already asked.

---

## Slide 2: Where did the water go?

**On the board:**
- The same shelf saucer picture (`Monday: 50 ml`, `Wednesday: 35 ml`).
- Lead line: `You already know that when water is heated a lot, it boils and turns into a gas.`
- Card: `The water in this saucer never got anywhere near boiling. But some of it still turned into a gas, a tiny bit at a time, from the top of the water.`
- Question (blue): `So where did that gas go?`

**The thought:** If some of the water turned into a gas, where would a gas go?

**What happens:**
Say to children: You already know water can be a liquid or a gas. When we heat water a lot, like in a kettle, it boils, and it turns into a gas. Now, was this saucer on the shelf anywhere near boiling? No. It was just sitting in a classroom. But here's the important bit. Some of that water still turned into a gas. Not all at once, and not with bubbles like a kettle. Just a tiny bit at a time, from the top of the water, all through those two days. That's where our 15 ml went: it turned into a gas. So think about this. If the water turned into a gas, where did that gas go?

Children answer the question on the board with a partner, a few words each.

**What you land:** `It went into the air` (some will say `up`). The class leaves this slide knowing water doesn't have to boil to turn into a gas.

**Why it's here:** It takes what children already hold (boiling turns water into a gas) and changes it: water can become a gas slowly, without boiling. Slide 4 depends on exactly this, when Ben says the fridge is too cold.

---

## Slide 3: Where did the water go?

**On the board:**
- The saucer drawn with faint arrows rising from the water into the air around it, the arrows labelled `water vapour`.
- Card: `The gas went into the air around the saucer. Water as a gas is called water vapour.`
- Question (blue): `If the water is in the air, why couldn't we see it go?`
- Sticky fact (starred): `Evaporation is when liquid water turns into water vapour, an invisible gas in the air.`

**The thought:** If the water's still there in the air, why does it look as if it vanished?

**What happens:**
Say to children: It went into the air. Into the air around the saucer, and out into the classroom. When water is a gas, it has its own name. It's called water vapour. And here's why it looked as if the water had vanished. Water vapour is invisible. You can't see it at all. There's water vapour in the air in this room right now, all around you, and you can't see a drop of it. So the water in the saucer didn't disappear. It changed from a liquid into a gas and went into the air. That change has a name too. It's called evaporation. Say it with me: evaporation. The arrows on this picture are only there to show us where the water went. You'd never see arrows over a real saucer. So, if the water is in the air, why couldn't we see it go?

Children answer the key question with their partner, then the teacher takes one or two answers.

Teacher info: The white cloud above a kettle isn't water vapour. It's tiny drops of liquid water that have cooled down, which is why you can see it. Water vapour itself is invisible. If a child brings up steam, that's the answer to give.

**What you land:** `Because water vapour is invisible.` The sticky fact is the sentence the class leaves holding.

**Why it's here:** It names what children now understand (evaporation, water vapour, invisible, in the air) and replaces `it vanished`. Slide 6's question about where the 40 ml went, and question 3 at the end, both need it.

## Slide 4: Will the fridge saucer lose any water?

**On the board:**
- A picture of a fridge with a saucer of water on a shelf inside, and Ben beside it.
- `Another saucer with 50 ml of water spent the same two days in a fridge.`
- Ben says: `The fridge saucer won't lose any water. It's far too cold in there for water to turn into a gas.`
- Question (blue): `Is Ben right? Explain your answer.`

**The thought:** Does water have to be warm to turn into a gas?

**What happens:**
Say to children: Now, there was a second saucer. It had 50 ml of water in it too, and it spent the same two days in a fridge. Ben's got a strong opinion about this one. He says the fridge saucer won't lose any water, because it's far too cold in there for water to turn into a gas. Is Ben right? Decide on your own first. Write down whether you think he's right or not, and your reason. Then tell your partner. If you're stuck, think back to the saucer on the shelf. Did that water have to boil?

Every child writes a verdict and one reason, then shares it with a partner. The teacher doesn't give the answer: slide 5 does.

Teacher info: A few children may say the closed fridge door stops the water getting out. There's air inside a fridge, and water vapour can go into that air.

**What you look for:** `Not right, because the shelf water didn't boil and still turned into a gas, so some of the fridge water can too.` A child who says `he's right, it's too cold` still thinks water needs heat to become a gas; leave it, the results on slide 5 will show them. A child who says `not right, it just disappears anyway` has the right verdict for the wrong reason, so ask them where it would go.

**Answer (for the teacher, revealed by slide 5):** Ben isn't right. Water doesn't have to boil, or even be warm, to turn into a gas. A little of the fridge water can still evaporate into the air, just very slowly.

**Why it's here:** It makes every child use slide 2's idea (no boiling needed) on a colder case the teaching didn't cover, and it makes the class want to see the fridge result, which is how slide 5 opens.

## Slide 5: What do the three saucers show?

**On the board:**
- `Three saucers each started with 50 ml of water. After two days:`
- Results table, with a small photograph beside each place:

| Place | Water left | Water that evaporated |
|---|---|---|
| Sunny windowsill | 10 ml | |
| Classroom shelf | 35 ml | |
| Fridge | 48 ml | |

- Question (blue): `How much water evaporated from each saucer?`
- Question (blue, after a break): `Look at the windowsill and the fridge. What was different about those two places?`

**The thought:** Why would the windowsill saucer lose so much more water than the fridge one?

**What happens:**
Say to children: Let's check Ben first. Here's the fridge saucer. It started with 50 ml, and after two days it had 48 ml left. So how much evaporated? 2 ml. Only a tiny bit, but some of that water still turned into water vapour, even in a fridge. So Ben wasn't right. I'll write 2 ml in the fridge row. Now it's your turn. Work out how much water evaporated from the windowsill saucer and from the shelf saucer. Remember, they all started with 50 ml. Then look at the windowsill and the fridge. Which one lost the most water, and which lost the least? Talk to your partner: what was different about those two places?

The teacher fills the fridge row live (2 ml). Every child works out the other two rows, then tells their partner what was different about the windowsill and the fridge.

Teacher info: A child may say the windowsill had sunshine or was near the window, or that the fridge was closed. Accept it and ask, `And how warm was it there?` The difference to draw out is how warm each place was.

**What you look for:** Windowsill 40 ml, shelf 15 ml. `The windowsill was warm and sunny, and the fridge was cold.` A child who writes 10 ml for the windowsill has copied the water left, not worked out what evaporated.

**Why it's here:** Children read the pattern out of the results themselves, as the teacher asked, instead of being told it. Slide 6 explains the pattern they have just found.

## Slide 6: Why did the windowsill saucer lose the most?

**On the board:**
- The completed results table (40 ml, 15 ml, 2 ml in the last column), with the rows marked `warmest`, `in between` and `coldest`.
- Card: `All three saucers had the same two days. So the saucer that lost the most water was evaporating the fastest.`
- Card: `Heating water makes it turn into a gas faster. That's why really hot water boils. Cooler water still evaporates, just more slowly.`
- Question (blue): `Where did the 40 ml from the windowsill saucer go?`
- Sticky fact (starred): `The warmer the water, the faster it evaporates.`

**The thought:** Why would being warmer make water leave the saucer faster?

**What happens:**
Say to children: Let's look at what you found. The windowsill lost 40 ml. The shelf lost 15 ml. The fridge lost just 2 ml. And the windowsill was the warmest place, the fridge was the coldest, and the shelf was in between. Now, all three saucers had exactly the same two days. So if one saucer lost much more water in the same time, its water must have been evaporating faster. Here's why. Heating water makes it turn into a gas faster. You already know that really hot water in a kettle boils. That's water turning into a gas so fast you can see the bubbles. Cooler water still evaporates, just more slowly. And cold water in a fridge evaporates very, very slowly. So the warmer the water, the faster it evaporates. One more question, and think carefully. That 40 ml from the windowsill saucer. Where is it now?

Children answer the key question with a partner.

Teacher info: The windowsill also had sun shining on it and the fridge was shut, so the places weren't different in only one way. At this age the difference to teach, and the one these results support, is how warm the water was.

**What you land:** The sticky fact. For the key question, listen for `in the air, as water vapour`. A child who says `it's gone` or `it dried up` is still holding the vanishing idea.

**Why it's here:** It turns the pattern children found into the objective's relationship (warmer, faster) and gives it a reason they can connect to boiling. Slide 7 needs the `same time, so faster` link, and question 2 at the end needs the relationship itself.

---

## Slide 7: Which cup was somewhere warmer?

**On the board:**
- Two identical cups drawn side by side, labelled A and B.
- `Two cups each had 100 ml of water. They were left in two different places for one day.`
- `5 ml evaporated from cup A.` / `20 ml evaporated from cup B.`
- Question (blue): `Which cup was most likely somewhere warmer? How do you know?`

**The thought:** Cup B lost more in the same day, so what does that tell me about where it was?

**What happens:**
Say to children: Two cups this time, each with 100 ml of water, left in two different places for one day. 5 ml evaporated from cup A. 20 ml evaporated from cup B. Which cup was most likely somewhere warmer? Write A or B, and how you know. Think about how long they each had.

Every child writes a letter and a short reason. The teacher takes a couple of reasons.

Teacher info: `Most likely` is honest: wind can speed evaporation up too. Today we're only thinking about warmth.

**What you look for:** B, with `they had the same time and more evaporated from B, so it was evaporating faster, so it was warmer`. A child who picks B but can't say why may be guessing from the bigger number, so ask them what the day has to do with it.

**Answer (teacher only):** Cup B. Both cups had one day, and more water evaporated from cup B, so its water was evaporating faster. Warmer water evaporates faster, so cup B was most likely somewhere warmer.

**Why it's here:** It is a quick check that children can use the relationship on cups they haven't seen, running it backwards from results to temperature. It feeds question 2 of the end check, which runs it forwards.

## Slide 8: Which puddle will dry up first?

**On the board:**
- A picture of a playground after rain, with two puddles the same size: one in sunshine, one in the shade of a wall.
- `After a rainy morning, there are two puddles in the playground, the same size. One is in the sunshine. The other is in the shade by the wall.`
- Questions (blue), each with its own space:
  - `Which puddle will dry up first?`
  - `Why will that puddle dry up first?` with the smaller question under it: `Which puddle's water will be warmer?`
  - `Where does the water from the puddles go?`
- No sticky facts on this slide.

**The thought:** Which puddle is warmer, what does that do to how fast it evaporates, and where does the water actually end up?

**What happens:**
Say to children: This time you're on your own, and the facts are off the board. After a rainy morning, there are two puddles in the playground, both the same size. One's out in the sunshine. The other one's in the shade, by the wall. There are three questions. Answer all three on your own. If you get stuck on the second one, look at the smaller question under it: which puddle's water will be warmer?

Every child answers all three questions independently.

Teacher info: The sticky facts are left off on purpose: this is the check of what children can do without them. On a real playground some water can also soak into cracks or run into a drain; accept that as extra, but question 3 needs the air.

**What you look for:** Question 2 names warmth and speed (`the sun makes its water warmer, so it evaporates faster`), not only `because it's sunny`. Question 3 says `into the air` or `water vapour`. `It disappears` or `it dries up`, on their own, mean the vanishing idea is still there.

**Why it's here:** It is the end check. It needs both things the episode taught, on a case the lesson never showed: the relationship for questions 1 and 2, and where evaporated water goes for question 3.

---

## Slide 9: Which puddle will dry up first? - check

**On the board:**
1. `The puddle in the sunshine.`
2. `The sun makes the water in that puddle warmer, and warmer water evaporates faster.`
3. `It turns into water vapour, an invisible gas, and goes into the air.`

**The thought:** Did my answers say why, and where the water went, or just which puddle?

**What happens:**
Say to children: Let's check. Question 1: the puddle in the sunshine. I think most of you got that one. Question 2 is the one to look at carefully. It isn't enough to say it's sunny. The sun makes that puddle's water warmer, and warmer water evaporates faster. If you've got warmer and faster, give yourself a tick. Question 3. Where does the water go? It turns into water vapour, an invisible gas, and goes into the air. If you wrote that it disappears, change it now, because that's the one thing today was about. The water never disappears. Next time you walk past a puddle that's shrinking on a sunny day, you'll know where it's going.

Children check their own three answers against the board and correct question 3 where needed.

**What you look for:** How many children needed to change question 3, and how many had `sunny` without `warmer` in question 2.

**Why it's here:** It closes the episode on the two sentences it was built to land, and shows the teacher, from what children change, which half of the learning is still shaky.

---

## Read-back

By the end, children will explain which of two puddles dries up first and where its water goes, because the lesson gives them evaporation as liquid water turning into invisible water vapour in the air (replacing `the water vanished` and `water has to boil to become a gas`) and the relationship, read from the saucer results themselves, that warmer water evaporates faster in the same time, evidenced by the independent puddle check on slide 8 with the sticky facts off the board.

A child who missed the teaching can still pick the sunny puddle from everyday sense, but not answer question 3 with water vapour in the air, and not answer question 2 with warmth and speed, so the check catches what the `because` clause names.

## Closing decisions

- **The learning:** children leave knowing that water can evaporate without boiling, that evaporated water is water vapour in the air and hasn't disappeared, and that warmer water evaporates faster. The first two are facts; `warmer water evaporates faster` is an idea (a pattern with a reason) met on three different sets of evidence: the saucers (taught, slides 5 and 6), the cups (slide 7, backwards) and the puddles (slide 8, forwards, new).
- **Objective and boundary:** the objective is used as written. Today stays with evaporation and warmth. Condensation, the water cycle, particles and wind as a variable are left out; the kettle-steam point lives in teacher info only.
- **Prior knowledge and structure:** children know solids, liquids and gases and that water boils when heated a lot, so the teaching starts from boiling and moves to `a gas without boiling`. Content-based rather than Discovery, because the results are supplied rather than a live phenomenon and the meaning of the result (where the water went) can't be seen in any data; slide 5 is still a bounded look at the evidence before slide 6 explains it, so the class uses the results first, as the teacher asked.
- **Sticking point:** the dominant wrong idea is that the water vanished, with `water has to boil to turn into a gas` holding it up. Exposed on slide 1, taught on slides 2 and 3, tested on slide 4 (Ben), met again by the key question on slide 6, retested on slide 8, question 3.
- **Independent evidence:** slide 8, with sticky facts hidden. A surface cue can't pass it: question 1 is answerable by everyday sense, so the evidence sits in questions 2 and 3, and nothing on the board says `water vapour`, `air` or `warmer water evaporates faster`.
- **Success criteria:** none as a separate panel. The end check's three questions carry the structure, and a criteria panel listing `say where the water goes` would give question 3 away.
- **Representations and pictures:** the results table is load-bearing (children complete its last column on slide 5, and slide 6 marks warmest to coldest on it). The saucer diagram's arrows are a drawn model, and the script says so. Photographs of the three places, the fridge, and the two-puddle playground are the things the class looks at while the teacher talks; the puddle picture must show clearly which puddle is in sun and which in shade, and nothing else about them differs.
- **Reasoning:** judging Ben's claim before the evidence settles it (slide 4), inferring temperature backwards from results (slide 7), and explaining a new case (slide 8, question 2).
- **Light line:** the saucer nobody touched offered the wrong idea taken seriously: `before anyone asks, there isn't a class cat`, in the slide 1 script.
- **Deliberate omissions:** no vocabulary slide (evaporation and water vapour are taught in the slide 3 teaching and sticky fact, then used on slides 5 to 9); no covered-bowl demonstration, because the droplets on the lid bring in condensation; no second practical, because the supplied results already carry the comparison.
- **flagsForTeacher:** None.
