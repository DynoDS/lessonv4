# Sixteen tasks to judge

Each case gives the year group and objective, what was taught before the beat, the stimulus, the task, what the design claims for it where it says so, and one response to judge.


## C01. History: a printed cross recorded as understanding

Year 4, `To explain why Tudor children worked`. Taught before this beat: an apprentice worked for a master; the master gave food, a bed and training in a trade; being trained meant the apprentice could earn a living as a grown-up. Stimulus: a slide showing Will's case (`Will's master gave him meals and a bed. Will only ever swept the floor.`) beside a table headed `The deal` with three rows: `food ✓`, `a bed ✓`, `training ✗`. Task: `What was missing from Will's apprenticeship?` The design records this beat as the evidence that children understand why training mattered to a Tudor family, and nothing later asks about the consequence. Response to judge: "training".

## C02. History: organise the arrangement, then use it

Same lesson and teaching as C01. Beat 1: children sort five cards under `What the apprentice gave` and `What the master promised`: `worked for the master`, `obeyed the master`, `food`, `a bed`, `training in a trade`. The teacher confirms the sort with the class before moving on (the expected placement and the one correction to make are in the teacher notes). Beat 2: Will's case as in C01, with no crosses or ticks on the board. Task: `Put a finger on the promise Will's master broke. Then finish the sentence: Will's family were worried, because when Will grew up...` The design records beat 1 as consolidation and beat 2 as evidence of understanding why training mattered. Response to judge: beat 1 sorted correctly; beat 2 "training in a trade... he wouldn't know how to make shoes, so he couldn't earn his own money."

## C03. History: made deeper with rules nobody taught

Same lesson and teaching as C01. Task: `Under the guild rules, could Will's master be punished for not training him? Use the Statute of Artificers in your answer.` Neither guilds nor any statute was mentioned in the lesson. The answer key reads: "Yes. The Statute of Artificers of 1563 required every master to train his apprentice fully, and the guild would fine a master who did not." The design records this as the lesson's most demanding historical reasoning. Response to judge: "I don't know what that is."

## C04. History: the hardship the task needs was never taught

Objective as approved: `To explain why Tudor families sent children to work, even when the work was hard`. Taught in the lesson: the deal (food, a bed, training), and that training helped the apprentice earn a living later. Nothing in the lesson describes long days, strict masters or hard conditions. Final task: `Why did Tudor families still choose apprenticeships, even though the conditions were hard?` Response to judge: "Because they got food and a bed and learned a job."

## C05. History: a family with money keyed as needing nothing

Same teaching as C01. Stimulus: `Imagine Will's family had plenty of food and money.` Task: sort `food`, `a bed`, `training in a trade` into `Still useful to Will` and `Not useful any more`. Key: food and a bed under `Not useful any more`; training under `Still useful to Will`. Acceptance condition: none. Response to judge: a child puts all three under `Still useful to Will` and says "you still need food even if you're rich, but training was the big thing."

## C06. Science: a results table tells children something new

Year 4, `To compare how well materials keep water warm`. Taught before this beat: how to read a simple results table, that warm things cool down over time, and that a higher temperature after the same time means the water stayed warmer. Children were not told which material works best. Stimulus: a supplied table for three cups of the same warm water after 20 minutes: `wrapped in wool 41 °C`, `wrapped in foil 33 °C`, `no wrapping 30 °C`. Task: `Which cup stayed warmest? What does the table tell you about wool?` Response to judge: "The wool one, it was 41. The wool kept the heat in better than the foil and nothing."

## C07. History: a spoken reason as the whole task

Same teaching as C01, plus the changed-circumstance idea not yet raised. Task on the board: `A Tudor family had enough food and money. They still sent their son to be an apprentice. Tell your partner: what might they have been hoping for? Then be ready to say your partner's reason.` No cards, no writing. The design records it as the beat where children first use the idea that training mattered for the future on a case where hardship is not the reason. Response to judge (spoken, reported by the partner): "She said they wanted him to have a proper job he could do when he's grown up, like his own shop."

## C08. History: the same question twice, called deeper

Same teaching as C01. Slide 12 task: `Why did Will's family send him to be an apprentice?` Slide 15 task, after a vocabulary slide and a picture of the same workshop: `Why did Will's family want him to be an apprentice?` Slide 15's `unlocks` reads "they can now explain the family's choice more deeply". Nothing new was taught between the two. Response to judge (slide 15): "So he could get food and a bed and learn to make shoes."

## C09. History: two sources that feed a later comparison

Year 4, `To identify changes and continuities in Victorian and modern schools`. Beat A: a photograph of a Victorian classroom; children write one thing they notice about how the room is set out. Beat B, separately: an extract from a Victorian school timetable; children underline one subject that is still taught today. Neither beat uses the other's answer. The final task: `Using the photograph and the timetable, write one thing that has changed and one that has stayed the same.` The design says beats A and B each gather evidence for the final comparison. Response to judge: "Changed: the desks were all in rows facing the front. Same: they had arithmetic like we have maths."

## C10. Maths: fresh practice of the taught method

Year 4, `To subtract three-digit numbers using the column method with exchange`. Modelled and guided: the column method with one exchange from tens to ones. Task: eight fresh subtractions in no pattern (e.g. 632 − 418, 751 − 326), recorded as independent practice of the method, steps visible beside them. Response to judge: all eight correct, exchanges shown.

## C11. Science: a material nobody has met, judged from its name

Year 4, `To identify conductors and insulators`. Taught: a complete circuit lights a lamp; metals tested in class (a paper clip, a key) let it light; plastic and wood did not. Task: `Will a strip of feltine let the bulb light? Explain your answer.` Feltine is an invented name that was never shown, described or tested. Key: "No. Feltine is an insulator." Teacher note: "Children who say yes still hold the misconception that all materials conduct." Response to judge: "Yes, because it might be a metal."

## C12. History: an exclusive key on a card that fits both groups

Same teaching as C01. Task: sort `a hot dinner every day`, `a straw mattress by the oven`, `knowing when bread is baked just right`, `his own bakery one day` under `Helped him straight away` and `Helped him when he grew up`. Key: the baking-skill card under `Helped him when he grew up` only; acceptance condition: none. The design records it as a quick check after the deal is taught. Response to judge: a child places the baking card under `Helped him straight away` and says "because he was learning it right then, and it helped him do his job in the bakery."

## C13. Geography: trace a network, then use the tracing

Year 4, `To explain how water flows through a river system`. Taught: on a labelled schematic map, arrows show which way water flows and water always flows downhill from source to mouth; the map is marked as a simple model, not a real river. Beat 1: children trace with a pencil where water from three marked streams goes; the teacher confirms the traced routes. Beat 2: `Oil is spilled into the river at point X. Which of the villages A to E could the oil reach? Use your traced routes.` Response to judge: "C and E, because they're downstream of X on my arrows. A and B are upstream so it can't get there."

## C14. English: a count relabelled as purpose

Year 4, `To choose expanded noun phrases that create an atmosphere`. Taught: a model phrase for a spooky setting (`the crumbling house with its dark, empty windows`) beside a plain one, with the difference named as choosing details that make the reader feel something. Task: `Improve "the house". Add two adjectives.` The design records this as children revising a phrase to create a chosen atmosphere. Response to judge: "the big old house".

## C15. RE: a belief read from a photograph

Year 4, `To understand what light means to Hindus at Diwali`. Taught: nothing yet about Diwali beliefs; the slide is the lesson's first. Stimulus: a photograph of a family lighting diyas in a doorway, with no caption beyond `Diwali`. Task: `What does this family believe about light and darkness?` Key: "They believe light wins over darkness and good wins over evil." Response to judge: "They like pretty lights."

## C16. PSHE: rehearse a taught strategy, with a way out

Year 4, `To practise a calm way to sort out a disagreement`. Taught and modelled: stay calm, say what you would like, suggest a fair way, and fetch an adult if it keeps happening or someone is hurt. Stimulus: a fictional playtime disagreement over whose turn it is with a ball. Task: `With your partner, try one line Mia could say. You can say it, write it on your whiteboard, or watch and tell your partner which part of the plan they used. You can pass.` Nobody is asked about their own experience. Response to judge: "Can we take turns? You have it till the whistle and then me."
