# Review

## PART A

CASE C01
Verdict: REVISE
Reason: The board prints `training ✗`, so "training" is read off the table, not worked out. The design records this as evidence of why training mattered to a family, yet nothing asks about the consequence (what Will would miss as a grown-up). A child who thinks "he was fed, so it was fine" still passes. The repair changes the stimulus and the thinking demand, so it goes back to the designer.

CASE C02
Verdict: PASS
Reason: The sort is honestly named as consolidation, and the teacher checks it before anyone builds on it. Beat 2 takes the crosses away and asks for the consequence ("when Will grew up..."), which a child who thinks food and a bed were enough cannot finish. The response uses the taught link between training now and earning later.

CASE C03
Verdict: REVISE
Reason: The task depends on guilds and a statute the lesson never taught, so it tests untaught content and only looks deeper. "I don't know what that is" is the fair answer. The key also states specific legal and guild consequences as fact without anything in the lesson to support them. The fix is a different task that uses the taught deal, which is the designer's job.

CASE C04
Verdict: REVISE
Reason: The approved objective and the final task both depend on hard conditions, but the lesson never taught what was hard. So the response only restates the deal and never weighs it against hardship, and nobody could do more from what was taught. Either the hardship gets a place in the teaching or the task changes, and both are design changes.

CASE C05
Verdict: REVISE
Reason: The key marks food and a bed `Not useful any more` for a well-off family, which is false: the child's "you still need food even if you're rich, but training was the big thing" is right and gets marked wrong. Just adding an acceptance condition doesn't fix it, because once all three cards can go under `Still useful`, the sort no longer makes anyone decide anything. The groups need to change, for example to "which one was the family really sending him for?", so it goes back to the designer.

CASE C06

## PART B

### Pair P (science: evaporation and temperature)

**1. Learning: Y**
Both teach clearly that the water goes into the air. PX: "The gas went into the air around the saucer. Water as a gas is called water vapour." PY: "It turned into water vapour and went into the air."
The difference is how the relationship is reached and tested. In PX the children fill in the column and talk about "what was different about those two places?", then the relationship is told on slide 6: "Heating water makes it turn into a gas faster... The warmer the water, the faster it evaporates."
PX's end check never uses the results. Misunderstanding tried: "the sun just dries things up, and it vanishes". Question 1 passes on everyday sense. On question 2, the prompt printed under it, "Which puddle's water will be warmer?", leads to "the sunny one is warmer so it dries faster" without any evaporation thinking. Question 3, "Where does the water from the puddles go?", catches the vanishing idea, but only as recall of the starred sentence.
In PY children decide from their own numbers: "Which sentence do our results show?... Choose one of the other sentences. Which saucer shows it's wrong?" This means "Water only evaporates in warm places" has to be ruled out with the fridge saucer. The end task requires the results: "Use our saucer results to explain." Leo's half-right claim stops a simple agree or disagree from passing, and "because the sun makes the water disappear faster" catches the vanishing idea.
PY's one weakness is that slide 2 packs several ideas into one teach (no boiling, water vapour, invisible, top surface, the word).
Neither task relies on anything untaught.

**2. Development: Y**
Both have real links.
In PX, Ben's fridge claim (slide 4) is settled by the fridge row on slide 5 ("Let's check Ben first"), and the relationship carries into the cups and the puddles. But the column children work out on slide 5 is used only by the teacher on slide 6. The cups and puddles are fresh cases that don't use the saucer results.
In PY, children's own amounts feed each later beat. The teacher checks them first ("go through 15 ml and 2 ml with the class before slide 6... A class that carries 35 ml and 48 ml forward will reach the opposite conclusion"). Those amounts decide slide 6, which becomes "faster" on slide 7 ("You chose B"), and they are the evidence required on slide 8 ("Did you use the water that evaporated, 40 ml and 2 ml").
PY's lid beat is picked up again on slide 9 ("think back to the saucer with the lid").

**3. Teacher use and voice: no difference**
Both scripts sound warm and plain. PX: "And before anyone asks, there isn't a class cat." PY: "Not exactly a quick escape, but it still happened."
Both boards carry the essential meaning.
PX slide 6 is crowded (a marked table, two cards, a question and a star fact). So is PY slide 2 (three teaching sentences, a question and a star fact), and PY slide 8 (photo, scenario, Leo's bubble, question, sentence starter, table).
Both are followable from the slides as written.

**4. Pupil experience and preservation: Y**
The writing load is similar: PX asks for a verdict and reason, two amounts, a letter and reason, and three answers; PY asks for a letter and reason, two amounts, a letter and a named saucer, and two short explanations. PY runs to more slides because of its two check slides, and its end task is a slightly heavier write, but both fit about 25 minutes.
PY keeps the teacher's requirement through to the end ("Use our saucer results to explain").
PX tells the relationship, and its slide 6 board adds a shaky explanation: "Heating water makes it turn into a gas faster. That's why really hot water boils." That treats boiling as fast evaporation, which is not accurate.

**Rather teach P: Y** - children decide what the saucer results prove and must use those amounts in the end task, which is what the teacher asked for.

### Pair Q (English: inverted commas)

**1. Learning: X**
Both make the bubble idea clear. QX: "Imagine somebody has rubbed out Leo's bubble and only the two edges are left." QY: "Leo didn't say 'said Leo'. That's the writer telling us who's talking, so it stays outside."
Misunderstanding tried: "pick the end mark from the reporting verb (said = comma, asked = ?, shouted = !)".
QY's practice lets this pass. Its Your Turn pairs every verb with its matching mark ("asked Aisha" question, "said Grandad" and "whispered Aisha" statements, "shouted Aisha" exclamation), and in the end task Grandad's bubble supplies the "?". QY also teaches the verb cue directly: success criteria "! for a shout", and in the script "How did he say it? He shouted. So which mark?".
QX blocks it. Slide 5 has "Where did you last have it said Mum" beside "I had it in the garden said Leo", and the check slide says "Number one says said Mum, but what Mum says is a question". QX also accepts "`“It's not there,” shouted Leo.` is also correct".
Second misunderstanding tried: "the full stop stays". QY's end task traps it more sharply (Aisha's bubble ends in a full stop). QX catches it on slide 5 item 2, but its own-lines final task can be dodged by writing only questions or exclamations.
On balance QX makes the key decision one children can't bypass.

**2. Development: Y (slight)**
Both link real learning.
In QX the bubbles children draw on slide 2 become steps 1 and 2 of the method ("The My Turn puts the inverted commas exactly where these bubbles start and stop"), and the bubble comes back small beside the My Turn.
QY does the same (slide 2 answers "stop at the last spoken word with no mark, which is exactly the gap slide 3 fills"). It also brings the teacher's bubble idea back as the thing the end task works on: "Turn each bubble into a sentence with inverted commas", with the bubble's full stop as the trap.
QX's final task uses the bubble only as a prompt to imagine ("picture Mum's speech bubble").

**3. Teacher use and voice: no difference**
Both are warm and plain. QX: "Honestly, Mum, I'm not sure it is still a shoe." QY: "And number 4... so much for not getting too close."
Both put the steps on the board.
QX's Our Turn script is mostly teacher talk ("So the question mark stays with her words, inside... Close the inverted commas"), which is close to a second My Turn. QY's slide 1 board holds five pieces of text.
Neither is hard to follow from the slides.

**4. Pupil experience and preservation: no difference**
QX has higher copying and composing costs: "Write each sentence. Draw a bubble around the words" on slide 2, then making up two lines on slide 7. QY writes only the spoken words on slide 2, and its end-task words are supplied.
QY has the accuracy slip: its success criteria say "! for a shout", and its script ties the mark to "shouted", while QX accepts a comma after a shouted line.
Both stay within scope: speech first, no new line for a new speaker, no split speech.
These trade-offs roughly cancel out.

**Rather teach Q: X** - children decide the end mark from what the person actually says, not from "said", "asked" or "shouted", and QY's practice lets them skip that decision.
