# Evidence Synthesis for UK Primary Lesson Design

Purpose: a decision-support document for an AI agent designing lesson PowerPoints. Organised by lesson component. Each section gives the principle, the cognitive mechanism, classroom implications, common failure modes, and key sources.

This file supplies conditional evidence and possible mechanisms. It does not automatically govern a lesson or override `preferences.md`. The lesson designer actively uses the evidence where it is relevant and its conditions fit, and follows the teacher's settled classroom decisions where the two differ.

A recurring lens across the components is cognitive load theory (Sweller): working memory is tiny (roughly 4 elements, seconds of duration) and is the bottleneck through which every new idea must pass before reaching long-term memory. Every design decision either protects that bottleneck or wastes it. Willingham's corollary — "memory is the residue of thought" — means pupils remember what their working memory actually processed, not what the teacher intended.

## Contents — read what this design touches

The purpose note and governing idea above, and the Cross-Cutting Principles at the end, are the core: read them on every run. The sections below map to lesson components, and most lessons touch most components, so expect to read most of them most runs. The list is here so the sections this lesson genuinely does not need can stay unread, not to make the file a lucky dip: when a component is in the design, read its section.

- **§1 Opener / Retrieval** — why retrieval supports learning, when focused or mixed retrieval fits, and what to avoid. Read when designing the starter (nearly every lesson).
- **§2 Teaching / Modelling** — the worked-example effect, the menu of enabling inputs and the conditions for each, the move-versus-instance rule. Read when deciding how to ready children for the work (nearly every lesson with new material).
- **§3 Guided Practice** — supported rehearsal and plausible release for a defined procedure or answerable repeatable skill. Read when that learning must cross from modelling to independence; other structures use checks suited to their own shape.
- **§4 Independent Practice** — generative practice, the structure-to-retrieve-into rule, and the per-lesson-type paragraphs (rule-practice, content, writing). Read when designing the practice (every lesson), plus the paragraph matching this lesson's type.
- **§5 Misconceptions** — planning for predictable misconceptions and building clean two-option discriminations. Read when naming the lesson's misconceptions or designing any spot-the-mistake, hinge or diagnostic contrast (nearly every lesson).
- **§6 Checking for Understanding** — checks that can reveal class understanding and inform a real next decision, without prescribing the response-gathering routine. Read when placing the lesson's checks.
- **§7 Cognitive Load in Practice** — split attention, redundancy, coherence, pacing. Read when a decision is about presentation load: what sits beside what on a slide, how long a teach runs before a check.
- **§8 Purposeful Lesson Closure** — possible same-session retrieval and other endings suited to the lesson, with later-lesson action only from real linked context or evidence. Read when designing the ending.
- **§9 Lesson Structures** — the use-when conditions and evidence for Skill-based, Content-based, Discovery, Dialogic and Task-Centred lessons. While choosing the structure, read each subsection's "Use when" line; once chosen, read only that structure's full subsection — plus the discovery caveats whenever an investigation is on the table.

## 1. Opener / Retrieval Practice

**Principle.** Start every lesson by forcing pupils to pull prior knowledge out of long-term memory. Rosenshine #1 (daily review) and #10 (weekly/monthly review).

**Why it works.** Retrieval is a storage event, not just a test. Each act of retrieval strengthens the memory trace and the cues that lead to it (testing effect, Roediger & Karpyne). Spacing retrieval across days produces more durable learning than massed practice. Interleaving topics forces discrimination — pupils have to decide *which* method applies, not just execute the one just shown. Retrieval also activates schemas that upcoming teaching will extend, reducing intrinsic load for the new content.

**Classroom implications.**
- Normally retrieve the previous lesson when it is known; otherwise retrieve the prerequisite for today's objective.
- Keep the starter focused. Mixed recent and older retrieval is a sequence-level option when the teacher requests it or the wider sequence gives it a clear purpose and relevant methods or knowledge are secure enough to discriminate.
- Use the simplest form that retrieves the intended learning accurately. A short question set, matching, sorting, correction, labelling or one rich problem may each be right.
- For answerable retrieval, plan for high success. An open values or discussion prompt instead needs an accessible entry point.
- Retrieval strengthens memory when pupils make a genuine attempt and then receive the answer, correction or support needed to complete the memory accurately. Repeated uncorrected failure does not carry the same benefit.

**Avoid.** Long "warm-ups" that become mini-lessons. Silent copying. Questions so hard that they re-teach rather than strengthen. Mixing older material without a sequence purpose or before pupils have enough security to choose productively.

**Sources.** Rosenshine, *Principles of Instruction* (2012); Dunlosky et al., *Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques* (2013); EEF Teaching & Learning Toolkit — "homework" and "metacognition".

## 2. Teaching / Modelling

**Principle.** Novices learn procedures and concepts fastest from fully worked examples and explicit modelling, not from solving problems unaided. Rosenshine #4 (models).

**Why it works.** The **worked example effect** (Sweller, Cooper 1985): showing the full solution lets the learner devote working memory to understanding the *structure* of the solution rather than burning it on means-ends search. Search generates load that produces no learning. A clear model with teacher narration externalises expert thinking — pupils hear the decisions a competent solver makes, which is invisible in the finished answer alone.

Willingham: children are not miniature adults; they think concretely and remember what they thought about. A model that voices the thinking ("I notice… I need to… I'll check…") directs their thought at the *reasoning*, not just the surface form.

**Modelling is the strongest default for genuinely new material — but it is one of several ways to ready learners, and which one fits is a decision to make each lesson, not a reflex.** The best move depends on what the children already hold and what the lesson is *for*; a fixed default is right some lessons and wrong the rest, so only reading the situation lands it each time. The menu, each with the condition it suits:

- **Model it and narrate the thinking** — the default for a genuinely new procedure or concept (worked-example effect), and the only real option for a physical skill that must be *shown* (handwriting, apparatus, a technique). Best when pupils lack the schema and the expert thinking is invisible in the finished answer.
- **A worked example to study** (and the faded sequence below) — the same effect with less teacher-talk, when pupils can read a solution and pull its structure out for themselves.
- **Critique a flawed / erroneous example** ("spot the mistake", a non-example) — when the lesson turns on a strong, predictable misconception (confront it head-on rather than hope it never forms — see §5); when you want pupils *processing* the rule rather than watching it; and when they *own* the outcome (their own plan, their own piece), where a full model hands them a template to copy but a broken one shows the rules in play without giving the answer. It also anchors less-sure pupils with a concrete case to react to. Evidence: studying and explaining incorrect worked examples, and contrasting correct with incorrect, improves learning and shifts misconceptions (Booth et al.; Große & Renkl; Durkin & Rittle-Johnson).
- **Guided practice together** (Our Turn, §3) — the supported rehearsal that bridges any of the above to independent work.
- **A single neutral warm-up** — when the move can be shown on a throwaway case without giving away the real task; one only, never a string of decoys.
- **Nothing — straight to the doing** — when pupils already hold the schema (forcing a model now triggers the expertise-reversal effect below).

These are not exclusive — model *then* critique, or critique *then* practise, are common. The discipline is to choose for *this* lesson by asking: do they have the prior schema? does the lesson turn on a known misconception? do the children own the outcome? who will stall, and what gives them an anchor? is the skill best shown live? — rather than letting the structure's usual move pick itself.

**The move and the instance.** When you model a transferable move live — finding a fact and shortening it to a note, composing a sentence, taking a reading — distinguish the *move* being taught from the specific *instance* used to demonstrate it, but normally supply both. The design provides the particular place, numbers, sentence, fact or other starting example, the intended completed outcome and the important decisions to expose, so the lesson is ready to teach. The teacher may still substitute another suitable instance during the real lesson. Keep the representation blank or partly blank when completing it is part of the model; provide a prepared completed example when inspecting the finished form is the useful teaching experience. The active part of a live-complete helper stays constructable, but the teacher is not left to invent the instance itself.

**Classroom implications.**
- When live modelling is chosen, expose the process, decisions and checks children need to see. Use a prepared completed example when inspecting the finished form is the useful teaching experience; do not substitute it when children need to see the process produced.
- Choose the number of worked or modelled examples from the learning, the time each takes, the genuinely distinct cases and the lesson time. Begin with the simplest representative example and add another only when it teaches a useful variation, case or boundary.
- Pair spoken explanation with a useful visual or representation when that combination supports the learning.
- Keep, reduce or remove support according to whether it enables the intended thinking or supplies that thinking or answer. A worked example may remain as a quality reference or comparison model when children still make the important decisions themselves; do not impose a fully worked → partly worked → blank sequence.
- Keep steps small. Rosenshine: teach in small steps, with pupil practice after each step.

**Avoid.** The **expertise reversal effect** (Kalyuga): heavy scaffolding that helps novices *hurts* pupils who already have the schema. If pupils already know it, stop modelling — move on. Telling pupils "have a go first to see what you think" before teaching — this is the minimal-guidance failure mode (see §9). Dense slides the teacher reads aloud (split attention + redundancy).

**Sources.** Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas (1998); Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006); Rosenshine (2012); Willingham, *Why Don't Students Like School?*; Sherrington, *Rosenshine's Principles in Action*.

## 3. Guided Practice and Release for Repeatable Skills

**Principle.** For a new defined procedure or answerable repeatable skill, use supported rehearsal between modelling and independent work when children need it. Content, dialogic and task-centred lessons use checks suited to their structure rather than a universal My Turn → Our Turn → Your Turn gate.

**Why it works.** New procedural schemas can be fragile, and supported rehearsal can reduce the risk of practising an incorrect method. Roughly 80% success at the intended release point is a useful planning expectation for a well-defined skill, not a result a generated resource can know, a guarantee, or a universal lesson metric. Very high success may appropriately build fluency, confidence or retrieval.

**Classroom implications.**
- For a repeatable skill, use a fresh supported attempt focused on the decisions children will later make independently.
- Plan enough modelling, guided practice and accessible progression that successful independence is plausible.
- Include a worthwhile check before release when its result can genuinely inform whether the class is ready.
- The check should be capable of revealing class understanding beyond willing volunteers, but the material does not prescribe books, whiteboards, cold calling, wait time, show call or another response routine.
- The live teacher decides from the real class response whether to proceed, add support, model another case or extend guided practice.

**Avoid.** Treating every lesson type as a My Turn → Our Turn → Your Turn sequence; claiming an exact percentage was achieved before the lesson was taught; forcing a separate hinge slide where no decision depends on it; prescribing response-management routines.

**Sources.** Rosenshine (2012); Lemov, *Teach Like a Champion* (Cold Call, Show Call, Everybody Writes); Christodoulou, *Making Good Progress*; EEF Maths KS2/3 Recommendation 3 (manipulatives and representations with clear rationale).

## 4. Independent Practice

**Principle.** Pupils consolidate learning by producing it themselves, with feedback, at a success rate that means they are practising the learning rather than their errors. Rosenshine #9. What the producing looks like differs by lesson type, and it should sit close in form to the thing the lesson actually wants, because children get better at what they practise and transfer is smaller than teachers hope.

**Why it works.** Practice that requires children to retrieve, explain, construct, apply or organise the intended learning can provide strong learning and evidence when they possess enough knowledge and the task directs attention to the right thing. Recognition, reading, copying, explanation and demonstration may still be useful steps or the correct form for the objective. Judge the thinking children actually do rather than treating a generative-looking activity as automatically deeper. Feedback or an appropriate comparison standard remains part of the learning mechanism (Agarwal, Nunes & Blunt 2021).

**Classroom implications, every lesson type.**
- For a defined procedure or answerable repeatable skill, release follows the conditional planning principle in §3. Other lesson structures use evidence suited to their own shape.
- Provide an answer, model, success criteria or another suitable comparison standard where the outcome is definite or exemplifiable. Do not prescribe whether the teacher live-marks, self-marks or examines work later.
- Unsupported blank-page recall is demanding and often produces incomplete evidence for primary children. Normally provide a useful structure such as headings, questions, a skeleton or a partly completed diagram when the aim is to reveal specific knowledge. Free recall may still be chosen deliberately when children have enough secure knowledge and open retrieval is the intended action.
- Generative forms carry conditions, and the conditions decide whether they work: a summary works when summarising has been taught and collapses into copying otherwise; a drawing task needs its contents named; imagining suits children with prior knowledge and is the wrong pick for beginners (Fiorella & Mayer 2016; Dunlosky et al. 2013). Brief the condition alongside the form.

**In rule-practice lessons** (a procedure or rule the child must execute reliably), the volume-and-ordering mechanics live in `teaching-sequence-skill-based.md`, which loads exactly when a lesson takes that shape. The maths-specific practice rules live in `subject-maths.md`.

**In content lessons** (science, history, geography, RE), practice combines remembering and reasoning, with meaningful reasoning appearing early enough to form part of the core work every child is expected to reach. Straightforward recall remains useful but should not be the only thinking children do with the content. Writing about content can help children organise, express and consolidate understanding, but it is one useful option rather than a compulsory ingredient in every lesson.

**In writing lessons**, the practice is composing, with support, using a taught and memorised process — the best-evidenced move in primary writing by a distance (strategy instruction ~1.0 across two meta-analyses: Graham et al. 2012; Kim et al. 2021). Component drills reliably improve the component and not the composition — grammar exercises were the only approach of thirteen with no significant effect on writing quality — so a lesson that teaches a component builds the bridge deliberately: the child uses it inside real composing in the same lesson or the next, because the transfer does not happen on its own.

**Avoid.** "Differentiation by outcome" where low attainers drift without a teacher. Pages of identical questions with no variation — this plateaus learning. Recall-only practice standing in for thinking in a content lesson.

**Sources.** Rosenshine (2012); Fiorella & Mayer (2016); Agarwal, Nunes & Blunt (2021); Agarwal (2019); Karpicke, Blunt, Smith & Karpicke (2014); Graham, Kiuhara & MacKay (2020); Graham, McKeown, Kiuhara & Harris (2012); Kim, Yang, Reyes & Connor (2021); Dunlosky et al. (2013); Christodoulou, *Making Good Progress*; EEF Maths KS2/3 Recs 2 & 8.

## 5. Misconceptions

**Principle.** Plan for the genuine specific misconceptions a topic reliably produces — often one to three, sometimes one, and sometimes none worth a separate teaching beat. Surface important ones deliberately during teaching rather than relying on later marking.

**Why it works.** Misconceptions are often the result of a pupil applying a sensible rule in the wrong domain (e.g. "longer number = bigger number" works for integers, fails for decimals), but some errors arise from missing knowledge, misunderstanding or momentary inattention. Left undetected, pupils practise and consolidate the wrong rule. Ofsted's Maths Research Review is explicit: common misconceptions are predictable and should be planned for, not discovered. Hattie's work on feedback: the most powerful feedback is immediate and specific to the error pattern.

**Classroom implications.**
- Identify the canonical misconceptions the topic genuinely has before designing the lesson — the count is the topic's own, not a target. (E.g. for rounding decimals: "round the whole number part then the decimal separately"; "0.35 rounds to 0.4 to 1dp, so 0.35 rounds to 1 to 0dp".)
- Show a wrong answer on a slide — "this pupil got 0.7. What did they do? Why is it wrong?" — and discuss. This is safer than letting it happen silently in books.
- Use **diagnostic questions** (Hodgen, Craig Barton style): multiple choice where each distractor maps to a specific misconception, so a wrong answer tells you *which* misconception.
- Address the misconception with a counter-example, not just a correction. "If that rule were true, then…" lets pupils see the contradiction.
- When you build a two-option discrimination — a spot-the-mistake pair, a contrast, a hinge question, a diagnostic distractor — make the two options differ on *exactly* the dimension you are teaching and be equivalent on every other. Test it by trying to defend the option you marked wrong: if a sharp pupil could win that argument, the pair is confounded — the "wrong" option is wrong on the taught axis but right on some other axis the question also invokes, so children who pick it aren't holding the misconception, they're reading a different (defensible) question. Fix it by holding everything else constant — same subject, same context — and varying only the taught feature, then phrasing the question on that feature. For persuade-vs-describe, both notes should be about the *same* attraction — "The Colosseum is a large stone amphitheatre" (describes) vs "You'll stand where real gladiators once fought!" (sells) — and the question asks "which one *sells* the place?", not "which *belongs* in a brochure?", because a plain describing line can legitimately belong in one.
- Revisit an important misconception in a later lesson only when the user supplies genuine sequence context, linked files or assessment evidence. Do not invent an unknown next lesson.

**Avoid.** Only teaching the correct method and hoping the misconception never forms. Correcting misconceptions privately one book at a time when they are in fact shared by half the class. Treating a misconception as carelessness — it is usually a mis-generalised rule.

**Sources.** Ofsted Maths Research Review (2021); Hodgen & Wiliam; Craig Barton, *How I Wish I'd Taught Maths*; EEF Maths KS2/3 Rec 1 (assessment to build on what pupils know).

## 6. Checking for Understanding

**Principle.** Design checks capable of revealing whether the intended learning is secure enough for the lesson's next decision. Do not equate a good check with a prescribed classroom response routine.

**Why it works.** Volunteer answers can overstate class understanding. A useful check gives the live teacher access to evidence from more than the keen volunteers and, before independent practice of a new procedure, can inform whether the class is ready to continue or needs more support.

**Classroom implications.**
- Create a question or task that diagnoses the learning rather than merely asking whether everyone understands.
- Where wrong options are used, map them cleanly to plausible misconceptions and protect the answer from wording or position cues.
- Before independent practice of a new procedure, include a worthwhile check when the result can genuinely affect the next move. Do not force a separate hinge-question slide in every lesson.
- State the learning action and intended evidence. The live teacher chooses how to gather and inspect responses, including hands up, cold calling, partner work, writing or another routine.
- The teacher, not the generated resource, decides from the real response whether to proceed, re-explain or add guided practice.

**Avoid.** "Does everyone understand?" as the only evidence. Relying only on volunteers. Prescribing cold call, no hands, wait time, Everybody Writes, Show Call, books, whiteboards or a fixed sampling routine.

**Sources.** Wiliam, *Embedded Formative Assessment*; Lemov, *Teach Like a Champion 3.0*; Rosenshine (2012); EEF Metacognition & Self-Regulation guidance.

## 7. Cognitive Load in Practice

**Principle.** Protect working memory ruthlessly. Every extraneous element on a slide, every redundant sentence, every split attention point steals capacity from learning.

**Why it works.** Intrinsic load (the difficulty of the content itself) cannot be reduced without simplifying what's taught. Extraneous load (how it's presented) is entirely under the teacher's control. Germane load (the effort of building schema) is what we want to maximise. Slide design is almost entirely about eliminating extraneous load.

**Classroom implications.**
- **Split-attention effect.** Put labels on the diagram, not in a separate key. Put the question and its working in the same visual field. Don't make pupils hold one thing in mind while looking at another.
- **Redundancy effect.** Do not put the teacher's script on the slide and also say it. Pupils try to read and listen simultaneously and do both badly. Slides carry the image/diagram/worked example; the teacher carries the narration.
- **Modality effect.** Visual + spoken beats visual + written for explanations. Use the slide for the diagram, your voice for the commentary.
- **Coherence.** Relevant optional context pictures are governed by `context-pictures.md` and must disappear rather than reduce text, work space, clarity or completion speed.
- **Pacing.** Break up unnecessarily long, uninterrupted explanation with useful opportunities to process or check understanding. Around five minutes is a useful guide for noticing when uninterrupted explanation may be running long, not a fixed outer limit; a story, demonstration, read-aloud or carefully developed explanation may reasonably last longer.

**Avoid.** Walls of text. Multiple representations of the same thing that pupils must integrate (split attention). Teacher reading the slide. Animations that move while you explain — the eye tracks the motion instead of the meaning.

**Sources.** Sweller, *Cognitive Load Theory* (2011); Mayer, *Multimedia Learning*; Oliver Caviglioli, *Dual Coding with Teachers*.

## 8. Purposeful Lesson Closure

**Principle.** Every lesson should end with purposeful consolidation or useful evidence, in the form that completes its structure. Same-session retrieval is one possible ending, not a universal exit-ticket requirement.

**Why it works.** Retrieval can strengthen and reveal central learning, while task-centred conclusions, dialogic reflections, review of completed work, explanation or synthesis may already provide the purposeful close. Repeating the same evidence in a compulsory extra ticket adds time without necessarily adding information.

**Classroom implications.**
- Choose the ending that best completes the lesson's actual work.
- Use a short retrieval of central learning when it adds useful consolidation or evidence.
- Do not prescribe the recording medium or a fixed question/time count.
- Use the ending to shape a linked next lesson only when the user supplies genuine sequence context or real assessment evidence.
- Without that context, do not invent class results, apply a fixed threshold or prescribe tomorrow's lesson.

**Avoid.** Adding an exit ticket that duplicates the ending already present; forcing every ending into silent written retrieval; relying on volunteers as the only evidence; inventing a next-day re-teach need from results the resource cannot know.

**Sources.** Rosenshine #10; Wiliam, *Embedded Formative Assessment*; Dunlosky et al. (2013).

## 9. Lesson Structures — When Each Is Evidence-Based

This is the most important decision the agent makes. The evidence is clear but nuanced.

### Skill-based
**Use when.** Children are learning a repeatable skill or procedure they need to perform reliably.

**Structure.** A short explanation when the skill needs it → My Turn → enough guided practice for the likely difficulty → Your Turn → optional Apply. One teaching cycle may contain closely connected, manageable variations of the same procedure; separate cycles are needed when combining them makes the procedure too complex or changes the decision rule.

**Why.** Modelling and supported practice expose the process and reduce unproductive search. A bounded pattern investigation or method comparison may precede direct teaching when it adds learning value, but novices are not left to invent the complete procedure unaided.

### Content-based
**Use when.** Children are learning a coherent body of knowledge, explanation or conceptual relationship they need to understand, recall or explain.

**Structure.** Teach one small, manageable chunk → children use or process it → teach the next distinct chunk → larger practice that draws the lesson's knowledge together. Closely connected facts may remain together only when they still form one simple, easy-to-understand chunk. Main practice is not restricted to a question count or one written form, and Apply is optional.

**Why.** Small chunks protect working memory, while immediate processing makes children work with each idea before a different one arrives. Meaningful content practice combines remembering with worthwhile thinking where the objective supports it.

### Discovery / Inquiry
**Use when.** Discovery is the best route after judging the objective, children's prerequisite knowledge and a safe, dependable phenomenon. Explicit explanation afterwards secures why the result or pattern occurred.

**Why the caveats matter.** The evidence cautions against minimal guidance, not against Discovery as a full route. Productive Discovery is bounded, focused and followed by accurate explanation. It may also appear as a smaller exploration inside Skill-based or Content-based teaching.

**Procedure boundary.** A pattern investigation or method comparison may precede direct teaching of a procedure when it genuinely helps. Novices must not be left to invent the complete method through unguided trial and error.

**Safeguard.** Make the suitability judgement first. If productive Discovery still looks doubtful after considering the prerequisites, phenomenon and objective, teach the learning directly.

**Sequence.** Follow `teaching-sequence-discovery.md`.

**Sources.** Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006); Hmelo-Silver, Duncan & Chinn (2007); Kalyuga et al., expertise reversal effect; Ofsted Science Research Review (2023); Rosenshine (2012).

### Dialogic / Scenario-based (children form and justify positions through structured talk)
**Use when.** The LO's success criterion is *the child can articulate and justify a position*, not recall a fact or perform a skill. Three conditions must hold: (a) the question is genuinely contested or interpretive — multiple defensible positions exist; (b) children have a stake or anchor to speak from — lived experience, accessible scenarios, or prior content knowledge with substance; (c) the teacher scaffolds positions and surfaces multiple views rather than delivering a fixed answer.

**Where it fits.** PSHE values lessons (friendships, online safety, healthy relationships, financial decisions, careers and aspirations). RE big-question lessons (*Is it ever right to break a promise?*, *What does it mean to belong?*). History interpretation lessons (*Was Henry VIII a good king?*, *Raiders or settlers?*). English motivation and debate lessons (*Was the Wolf telling the truth?*, *Should Macbeth have killed the king?*). Geography land-use, fair trade, and climate-decision lessons. Citizenship and P4C work. Most factual content in these subjects is still Content-based; dialogic is reserved for the lessons whose centre of gravity is the discussion itself, with children's reasoning and positions as the assessment evidence.

**Structure.** Use one or more worthwhile Stimulus → Talk beats. One discussion may carry the lesson only when it is genuinely rich and substantial. Add an honest synthesis when it helps children compare what actually emerged, then obtain proper individual evidence in the lightest form that still shows the learning. That evidence may be written, spoken, visual or practical when writing would distort the objective or create an inappropriate personal record.

**Why.** Dialogic teaching has a credible evidence base. Robin Alexander's work on dialogic teaching shows that classrooms where children's talk is purposeful, cumulative and reciprocal produce better reasoning and writing than transmission-only classrooms. Neil Mercer's *exploratory talk* research shows the same — children think more deeply when they articulate, listen, push back and revise positions. The EEF's *Dialogic Teaching* evaluation (2017) found measurable gains in English, science and maths reasoning for upper-primary children when teachers used structured talk routines. Ofsted's PSHE / Citizenship guidance emphasises that values-based learning needs structured discussion, not lecture.

**Why the caveats matter.** Dialogic teaching needs enough knowledge, a concrete stimulus, a worthwhile central question, safe distance where needed and a structure that lets each child form and evidence a view. Lesson-specific follow-up questions may help a teacher deepen the actual discussion, but sentence stems, push-back questions and synthesis are conditional teaching tools, not compulsory scripts or classroom-management routines. Correct factual errors, safeguarding issues and harmful claims rather than treating them as equally defensible opinions.

**Red flags.** Pure transmission of values dressed up as a discussion ("here are the seven kinds of influence — copy them down"). Discussion with no Synthesise (the consolidation is the load-bearing step). Sentence-stem talk used as a substitute for thinking — children parrot stems without committing to a position. Dialogic used for content that genuinely has a right answer (e.g. how the digestive system works, how to multiply two-digit numbers) — that's Content-based or Skill-based. Reflect tasks worded so vaguely that children can fill them with anything and pass — the Reflect should require a *position* and a *reason*, not just a feeling.

**Default.** Most foundation-subject lessons in primary school are still Content-based — a body of knowledge children need to understand and recall. Dialogic is the right structure when the LO is genuinely about position-taking and reasoning, and the lesson's evidence of learning is what children write or argue afterwards, not what they can recite.

### Task-Centred

**Use when.** One sustained real task is the lesson's centre of gravity, children largely hold the knowledge or skill needed to begin, and the completed task is the main evidence of learning.

**Why.** Authentic application can integrate knowledge, decision-making and performance when the task receives enough protected time. A short enabling input may prepare children without turning the lesson into a sequence of disconnected exercises.

**Boundary.** Use Skill-based when repeated performances are needed to acquire a method. Use Content-based when substantial new knowledge must be taught. Use Discovery when the phenomenon must be investigated before its explanation is secured.

**Sources.** Robin Alexander, *A Dialogic Teaching Companion* (2020); Neil Mercer, *Words and Minds* (2000) and the *exploratory talk* tradition; EEF, *Dialogic Teaching* evaluation report (2017); Ofsted PSHE / Citizenship subject reviews.

## Cross-Cutting Principles

- **High success is conditional, not one universal metric.** For a defined repeatable skill, roughly 80% success at release is a useful planning expectation; it does not govern every lesson type, generated resources cannot know the real result, and success above 95% is not automatically unproductive.
- **Curriculum coherence uses real context.** Link retrieval, misconceptions and later lessons when the brief, unit or assessment evidence supplies the connection. Do not invent a previous or next lesson to make a sequence look complete.
- **Dual coding** in explanations; **variation theory** in practice questions.
- **Memory is the residue of thought.** Consider what the activity will actually make pupils think about. Enjoyable, creative or active work can be strong when its thinking remains directed at the intended learning; unrelated elements that compete with it remain weak.
- **Prior knowledge changes support, but expected prior teaching is not proof of mastery.** Produce one coherent lesson with essential foundations visible; the live teacher adapts pace and support.
