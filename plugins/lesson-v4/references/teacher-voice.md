# Teacher Voice Guide

## Purpose

Use this guide when writing pupil-facing teaching resources in the teacher's voice: lesson slides, worksheets, questions, worked examples, model answers, success criteria, sentence stems and task instructions.

This is a **runtime voice guide**, not the calibration evidence archive. Apply these rules as defaults, but do not let "voice" override good teaching, factual accuracy, age-appropriateness, safeguarding, clarity or the specific purpose of the resource.

A sentence can sound like the teacher and still be wrong for the task if it:
- gives away the answer;
- uses language pupils may not understand;
- adds unnecessary cognitive load;
- belongs in speaker notes rather than on the slide;
- weakens the pedagogy.

## How to read this file

This guide owns **how the voice sounds**. What the words must **achieve** is owned by `preferences.md` → Written Voice (House Style) and applies at full strength alongside this file: understanding over decodability, density and paragraph breaks, protecting the answer, the printed-surface habits, and the em dash rule.

When authoring anything a child reads or hears, read sections 1-4, 15 and 17. Read the numbered section for the kind of thing you are writing at the moment you write it: a model answer opens §8, a worked example §9, success criteria §10, a misconception warning §11, a comparison prompt §12, a practical lesson §13. Read §16's calibrated examples only when wording remains uncertain after that.

The voice is the same across the primary years: age changes the support, not the humanity. KS1 normally needs more familiar vocabulary, more concrete meaning and stronger reading-access support. KS2 can carry richer vocabulary and more complex thought. Neither phase has a sentence-length target: do not make KS1 robotic by chopping every explanation into tiny sentences, and do not make KS2 abstract or academic merely because the children are older. Year 3 may need either treatment according to the material and the class. Written Voice's access rules (Below resources, Greater Depth) still apply at full strength.

# 1. Core voice

Write in a way that is:

- **Natural and conversational, but professionally credible.**
- **Direct rather than performative.**
- **Clear without sounding textbook-like.**
- **Economical when the idea is already simple.**
- **Warm without becoming sugary or over-encouraging.**
- **Confident without sounding formal for the sake of it.**

Natural contractions are welcome:
- `there's`
- `can't`
- `didn't`
- `won't`
- `doesn't`

Professional does **not** mean grammatically formal.

Prefer:
> The bulb won't light because there's a gap in the circuit.

Over:
> The bulb will not illuminate because the electrical current is unable to pass through the incomplete circuit.

Do not deliberately make wording casual. Colloquial language should still pass a simple test:

> **Would this sound comfortable if the headteacher were watching?**

# 2. Slides and speaker notes are different registers

## Pupil-facing slides

Slides are the **tighter, curated version** of the voice.

They should usually:
- get to the point quickly;
- carry only the explanation pupils need to see;
- avoid reproducing every sentence the teacher might say aloud;
- leave room for visuals, examples, discussion and questioning;
- avoid long stretches of teacher-talk prose.

Prefer on a slide:
> Which fractions are equivalent?

Rather than:
> Have a look at the fractions below and see if you can work out which ones are equivalent.

The second version can sound completely natural when spoken, but that does not mean it belongs on the slide.

## Speaker notes / spoken teaching

Speaker notes can contain:
- fuller conversational framing;
- more natural teacher-talk;
- rhetorical questions;
- additional explanation;
- pauses, emphasis and informal linking.

For example:
> So, why did the Romans actually want Britain? Well, there were a few reasons...

This can sound authentically like the teacher when spoken even if the slide itself should be tighter.

### Fuller means more spoken, not more polished

Speaker notes are not longer slide copy. They are the words as said, so the test is to read them aloud: if you cannot hear the teacher saying it to this class, sentence by sentence, the note is authored prose in the wrong register, however grammatical it is.

The tell is a chain of tidy directives, each carrying a trailing qualifier clause:

> Select materials that complete the circuit. Your explanation needs to show how each choice affects the flow, rather than simply naming the component. Finish by stating how the circuit could be extended without losing brightness.

A teacher gives one instruction at a time and turns the caveat into a question:

> Choose the materials that complete the circuit. For each one, say what it does. Then think - could we add another bulb and keep it bright?

### Default relationship

**Short/direct can become the slide. Fuller/conversational can expand it in the notes. Do not normally reverse this relationship.**

# 3. Sentence rhythm: avoid the AI sound

Good individual sentences can still sound generated when placed together.

Avoid:
- several adjacent sentences with the same basic shape;
- suspiciously similar sentence lengths;
- facts presented with identical rhythm;
- mechanically tidy transitions;
- prose that feels as if every sentence has been polished to the same level.

Example of a pattern that can become robotic:

> Rainforests are home to lots of plants and animals. They also take in carbon dioxide from the air. They also help control the Earth's climate.

The problem is not the vocabulary. It is the repeated construction and rhythm.

Prefer **natural variation**:

> Rainforests are important because many different plants and animals depend on them. They also take in carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, which helps regulate the Earth's climate.

A more sophisticated word is not automatically "too AI". Natural flow matters more than artificial simplification.

### Important

Do not force variation for its own sake. The goal is **natural rhythm**, not a checklist of sentence types.

# 4. Humour and personality

Humour is a genuine part of the voice, but it is **opportunity-sensitive, not quota-based**.

## When humour may be expected

If the content naturally hands you an easy playful hook, notice it.

Examples:
- quirky animals;
- bizarre facts;
- popcorn popping;
- amusing historical situations;
- deliberately "breaking" a circuit after making it work.

The hook is not always the content being amusing. The lesson's own misconception is often the easiest opportunity: take the wrong rule seriously for a moment and let its absurdity show.

Good:
> One apple isn't a magic "balanced diet" button!

The joke lands on the wrong idea, so it teaches while it amuses - and a wrong rule is present in most lessons, which is why a lesson with no quirky content is not a lesson with no opportunities.

Good:
> The Romans didn't travel all the way to Britain for the weather!

Good:
> You've made it work. Now break it.  
> *Not literally!*

Good:
> It doesn't need to be a masterpiece - it just needs to show the circuit clearly!

## When humour is optional

Ordinary teaching content can include a small light moment if it fits naturally.

## When to keep it straight

Procedural content often works best without humour:
- calculation steps;
- routine methods;
- important safety instructions;
- explanations where a joke adds nothing.

## Sensitive or serious lessons

Serious does **not** have to mean solemn.

A warm, normal, matter-of-fact lesson can still contain tiny harmless moments of lightness if they make the room feel human.

The safest lightness in a sensitive lesson usually targets the wrong idea being corrected: a gently exaggerated misconception makes fun of an idea, and nobody in the room is an idea. The issue itself is never the joke.

However:
- never make the actual sensitive issue the joke;
- never weaken safeguarding;
- never use humour in a way that trivialises a pupil's concern or experience.

### Core rule

**Do not ask "Have I used humour yet?" Ask "Has the content given me an easy, appropriate opportunity?"**

# 5. Explanations and definitions

## Prefer direct explanation

When a straightforward explanation works, use it.

Good:
> An electrical appliance is something that uses electricity to work.

Good:
> Evaporation is when a liquid changes into a gas.

Do not make a simple definition sound academic merely because it is a vocabulary box.

A slightly fuller shape can be better when it gives the idea before the term:

> When a liquid gets warmer, some of it can change into a gas. This is called **evaporation**.

## Style varies by the job of the sentence

An opening can carry a little more character or narrative framing when it helps children build a mental model:

> Every river has a journey. It starts high up and gradually makes its way towards the sea.

A key-term definition that follows stays plain:

> The place where a river begins is called its **source**.

"Concise" is not a universal rule for every line; the sentence's function decides how much character it can carry.

## Use useful subject vocabulary

Do not replace precise, familiar curriculum vocabulary simply to sound simpler.

For example, in Maths:
> Partition 24 into 20 and 4.

can be better than:
> Split 24 into 20 and 4.

if `partition` is the expected mathematical language.

## Adjust language to conceptual difficulty

When the underlying idea is abstract or difficult, lighter language can reduce unnecessary cognitive load.

Example:
> Claudius had something to prove.

may be more useful than:
> Claudius wanted to strengthen his political position as emperor.

when the history itself is already conceptually demanding.

This is not a ban on sophisticated vocabulary. Use precise subject language where it earns its place.

## Extra wording must earn its place

Do not add a second sentence simply because explanations "should" be thorough.

A longer version is better only when the extra wording adds:
- a useful example;
- a misconception correction;
- a method reminder;
- genuinely helpful scaffolding;
- an important connection.

# 6. Questions and pupil instructions

There is no single required question stem.

All of these can fit:
- `How do you know...?`
- `Can you explain why...?`
- `Which one...? Explain your answer.`
- `Is Sam correct? Explain your answer.`
- `What is the same and what is different...?`

Do not standardise every question into one repeated pattern.

## Keep instructions economical

Default to the shortest wording that gives pupils everything they need.

Good:
> Explain two reasons why the Romans invaded Britain.

Good:
> Answer the questions in full sentences.

But a longer version can be better when a useful reminder is needed:

> Work through questions 1-6. Remember to partition each number before multiplying.

The method reminder earns the extra words.

## Do not leak the answer

Question wording must preserve the thinking pupils are meant to do.

The commonest leak is a question that presupposes the verdict. `What has Sam got wrong?`, `What is Sam forgetting?`, `What has Sam missed?` all announce that Sam's claim fails before the child has judged it; the verb changes, the leak is the same. The test: could this question be asked, word for word, if the claim were actually right? `Is Sam correct? Explain your answer.` works either way. `What is Sam forgetting?` only works if Sam is wrong, so it has done the judging for the child.

Bad:
> What has Sam got wrong?

if pupils are first supposed to decide whether Sam is wrong.

Better:
> Is Sam correct? Explain your answer.

The presupposition is legitimate only when the error's existence is given and the task is finding, explaining or correcting it - `find the mistake in this working` - because there the judgement was never the thinking being asked for.

## Use pupil-clear language

A phrase can sound natural to the teacher but still be unclear to pupils.

For example:
> Which answer would you choose as the best model?

may be less clear than:
> Which is the stronger answer? Explain why.

Always check the pupil's likely interpretation, not only whether the wording sounds like the teacher.

# 7. Scaffolding

## Scaffold the barrier, not the answer

Adjust support based on:
- how new the learning is;
- conceptual difficulty;
- pupil familiarity;
- independence expected;
- the specific thing preventing pupils from succeeding.

Heavy scaffolding can support:
- structure;
- organisation;
- steps;
- method;
- prompts;
- sentence construction.

It should **not** supply the substantive answer pupils are meant to generate.

### Examples

First experience of a difficult explanation:
- heavy scaffold may be appropriate.

Retrieval three weeks later:
- minimal scaffold.

Pupils know the content but struggle to organise writing:
- scaffold the organisation, not the facts.

A new mathematical method:
- light or heavy method support may be useful depending on the class.

### Sentence stems

Where possible, stems should grow naturally from the wording of the question.

Question:
> Why won't this circuit work?

Natural stem:
> The circuit will not work because...

A stem can be perfectly grammatical but still feel wrong if it sounds disconnected from the actual question.

# 8. Model answers and exemplars

A model answer is a **separate register**.

Do not simply write the teacher's spoken voice as if a pupil said it.

Aim for:
- a strong, attainable answer for the year group;
- enough subject knowledge to model success;
- natural pupil-plausible phrasing;
- useful subject vocabulary;
- clear sentence structure;
- natural rhythm.

Avoid:
- adult prose disguised as pupil writing;
- excessive polish;
- formal connective chains such as `Firstly... Furthermore... Finally...` unless genuinely appropriate;
- mechanically balanced sentences;
- unnecessary first-person hedging such as `I think` when answering a known-content question.

Good:
> The Romans wanted to invade Britain for several reasons. Britain had useful resources, including metals and farmland. The Romans also wanted to make their empire bigger. Claudius wanted a successful invasion because it would make him look like a strong and powerful leader.

This can be detailed without becoming adult-sounding.

## Model-answer anti-AI check

Before finalising, ask:
- Do consecutive sentences have suspiciously similar length?
- Do multiple facts use the same construction?
- Does the answer sound "complete" in a way a real strong answer from this year group would not?
- Have I made the vocabulary unnecessarily casual just to sound human?

Human-sounding does **not** mean dumbing down the vocabulary.

When an accuracy boundary shapes the answer - a claim that must stay cautious - translate the caution into the child's own words rather than importing the guidance document's phrasing. A child writes `could be part of a balanced diet`; `could contribute to a balanced diet` is the adult document showing through, and it reads as adult polish however correct it is.

# 9. Worked examples

Worked-example narration should usually be **scan-friendly and economical**.

Good:
> Partition 24 into 20 and 4.

> Multiply the tens first: 3 × 20 = 60.

> Now multiply the ones: 3 × 4 = 12.

> Finally, add the two answers: 60 + 12 = 72.

Avoid narrating every thought:
> We have dealt with the tens, so now we need to move on to the ones before combining our two answers to find the final answer.

If the worked steps are already visible, the finished model can be very compact:

> 3 × 24 = 72

or:

> 3 × 24 = 72 because 60 + 12 = 72.

Do not restate the entire procedure in prose unless it serves a clear teaching purpose.

# 10. Success criteria

Default style:
- short;
- imperative;
- specific;
- actionable.

Prefer:
- `Describe what can be seen and heard.`
- `Use expanded noun phrases.`
- `Choose powerful verbs.`
- `Check capital letters, full stops and commas.`

Do not default to:
> I can use expanded noun phrases.

Criteria are read and used by the child, so they are written in the child's own words. A criterion can be short, imperative, specific and actionable and still fail by being planning language:

> Judge balance using the pattern across a day or week.

is a curriculum document's sentence. The child's step is:

> Think about what someone eats across a whole day or week.

Extra wording is useful when the criterion itself needs scaffolding.

For a struggling class:
> Try starting some of your sentences in different ways, for example with a fronted adverbial.

That extra explanation earns its place.

# 11. Misconceptions and "Watch out!" language

`Watch out!` can fit when it flags a genuine trap.

Good:
> **Watch out!** The water hasn't disappeared - it has evaporated into the air as water vapour.

Use it because there is a misconception to catch, not as a decorative recurring label.

A useful pattern:
1. name or correct the mistaken idea;
2. give one short accurate explanation.

Do not over-explain the warning.

---

# 12. Comparison and critique prompts

Natural comparison prompts include:

> What is the same and what is different about these two circuits?

> Compare the two circuits. What similarities and differences can you spot?

> Which is the stronger answer? Explain why.

> What could we add to make this a stronger answer?

Again, the pedagogical check matters:
- do not tell pupils a claim is wrong before they have judged it, including by presupposition (`What is she forgetting?` has already judged - see §6);
- do not use teacher-facing terminology pupils may not understand;
- preserve the actual thinking.

---

# 13. Practical / activity-led lessons

Voice is not only the wording on the slide.

Sometimes the most authentic resource is one where **the slide gets out of the way and lets pupils do something**.

For a practical lesson:
- use short challenges;
- give only the teaching needed before the next action;
- let pupils notice patterns;
- pause for brief teaching;
- send them back to the task.

Example structure:
> Challenge → brief teaching → try it → quick check → improve it → record it.

Good practical slide:
> **Can you make the bulb light?**

Good follow-up:
> **You've made it work. Now break it.**  
> *Not literally!*

Avoid turning an activity-led lesson into:
> explanation → explanation → explanation → worksheet.

# 14. Tone by lesson type

## Easy factual lesson

Keep slides especially lean.

Use:
- visuals;
- classification;
- quick questions;
- retrieval;
- examples.

Do not inflate a simple LO into paragraphs of content.

## Tricky conceptual lesson

Use:
- plainer language;
- examples;
- comparisons;
- careful scaffolding;
- slightly more explanation where it genuinely reduces cognitive load.

## Sensitive / PSHE-style lesson

Aim for:
- warm;
- normal;
- clear;
- matter-of-fact;
- respectful.

Avoid:
- clinical safeguarding-leaflet language;
- over-serious solemnity;
- childish euphemisms;
- humour that targets the issue itself.

## Practical lesson

Let the instructions and activity carry the lesson. Teach in short bursts.

# 15. Things to avoid

Avoid these unless the context clearly justifies them:

- generic AI transitions;
- repetitive sentence patterns;
- several equally polished sentences in a row;
- unnecessary rhetorical flourishes;
- formal vocabulary merely to sound academic;
- slogan-like wording used for effect rather than teaching;
- invented compound modifiers a teacher wouldn't say (`a varied-looking lunch` for `a lunch with lots of different foods`);
- excessive teacher-talk written directly onto slides;
- forced humour;
- forced "fun" labels;
- explaining the same idea twice without adding value;
- scaffolds that give away the answer;
- first-person model-answer openings such as `I think...` when unnecessary;
- adult-polished pupil exemplars;
- making every slide follow the same sentence formula;
- em dashes and en dashes in anything a child or parent reads: write a comma, brackets, a colon, a full stop or a spaced hyphen ( - ) instead (the full rule lives in Written Voice).

Specific rejected/weak signals from calibration include:
- `electricity can't just jump over a gap` - too informal for the intended resource;
- `pop a comma` - too colloquial;
- `Gap in the circuit = bulb off` - slogan-like compression;
- `Prove it.` as a generic challenge - too slogan-like / underspecified.

# 16. Calibrated examples

## A. Pupil-facing explanation

> A bulb will only light if electricity has a complete path to travel around. If there's a gap anywhere in the circuit, the bulb won't light.

Why it fits:
- direct;
- contractions;
- accurate;
- no unnecessary framing.

---

## B. Light personality on a slide

> The clue is in the name! A **fronted** adverbial has been moved to the front of the sentence.

Why it fits:
- brief personality;
- directly connected to the concept;
- still professionally credible.

---

## C. Humour from the content

> The Romans didn't travel all the way to Britain for the weather!

Why it fits:
- easy natural opportunity;
- relevant;
- short;
- does not replace the actual explanation.

---

## D. Model answer

**Question:** Why are rainforests important?

> Rainforests are important for two main reasons. They provide habitats for many species and absorb carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, helping to regulate the Earth's climate.

Why it fits:
- a strong, attainable model for the year group;
- subject vocabulary is not artificially simplified;
- varied construction;
- does not sound like a teacher speech.

---

## E. Success criteria

**LO: Write a setting description**

- Describe what can be seen and heard.
- Use expanded noun phrases.
- Use powerful verbs to describe movement.
- Check capital letters, full stops and commas.

Why it fits:
- imperative;
- specific;
- economical.

---

## F. Scaffolded task

> Write three facts about the rainforest. Think about its climate, plants and animals.

Why it fits:
- support is present because pupils may need help starting;
- it provides categories, not answers.

---

## G. Practical lesson instruction

> **Can you make the bulb light?**

Then, once successful:

> **You've made it work. Now break it.**  
> *Not literally!*

Why it fits:
- the slide gives pupils something to do;
- humour grows naturally out of the activity;
- explanation can follow their experience.

---

# 17. Final pre-flight check

Before finalising pupil-facing material, quickly check:

1. **Does this sound natural, or suspiciously polished?**
2. **Could I remove words without losing useful teaching?**
3. **Does the sentence belong on the slide, or would I actually say it aloud? A script or note must pass as speech: hear the teacher saying it, sentence by sentence.**
4. **Have I kept useful subject vocabulary where it helps?**
5. **If I've added scaffolding, what barrier is it solving?**
6. **Have I accidentally given away the answer - or presupposed the verdict the child is meant to reach?**
7. **Is the wording clear to the pupil, not merely clear to a teacher?**
8. **If humour is present, did the content genuinely invite it?**
9. **If an obvious playful opportunity exists, have I missed it?**
10. **Across several sentences, does the rhythm feel human rather than mechanically even?**

---

## Maintenance

Treat this guide as the default runtime specification.

Do **not** keep expanding it every time one sentence is corrected.

Only change the guide when real resource work reveals:
- a repeated miss;
- a genuinely new register;
- a contradiction in the current guidance;
- a preference that survives more than one context.

Keep detailed calibration examples, rejected alternatives and testing history in the teacher's separate evidence document rather than adding them all here.
