# Stick-in Sheets Pedagogy

This reference carries the thinking behind the Stick-in Sheets. The `stick-in-sheets-designer` agent reads it before working. It exists because the teacher's school has slow, difficult printing and does not routinely glue worksheets into books - the lesson's slides carry the learning on their own, and a child's exercise book evidences the thinking in the child's own hand. The one deliberate exception is a *stick-in piece* for a visual a child cannot reproduce by hand - printed sparingly and glued in. Holding the pack to the few moments that genuinely need it is what keeps it worth the paper.

## The write-on moments - a visual the child marks, not copies

Most writing moments leave an **answer** in the book while the question that prompted it stays on the board: a child reading "35 + 40 =" writes "75", and the book holds the answer. A few moments are different in kind - the question *is* a picture the child marks, sorts, labels or draws on: a Venn to sort shapes into, an angle to name, a row of triangles to classify, a diagram whose parts the child labels (the parts of a plant, the features of a river), a box to draw an angle in. A child cannot reproduce that picture by hand in a squared book, so copying it off the board breaks down exactly where the learning happens. These are the **write-on moments**, and they are the one case where a printed piece glued into the book serves the book-first goal better than the child's own copying.

Apply one question to each moment: *does the child write onto a figure they could not redraw by hand?* If they do, it is a write-on moment. If they are recording an answer the board already carries - an arithmetic result, a fluency list, a reasoning sentence - it stays in the book in the child's own hand, never on a printed piece. Holding the pieces to the few moments that genuinely need them is what keeps them worth the paper, and it is why a pure-arithmetic lesson produces none at all.

**A recording table's furniture is a figure.** When a moment asks children to record into a multi-column table or grid - a classification table with a decision, source and explanation per device, a results table with headed columns - the headers and ruled columns are structure the child would have to rule and copy before any thinking starts, and by the time thirty children have drawn four columns the lesson has spent its practice time on furniture. That moment passes the write-on test: the table is the figure, and the child's decisions go into printed cells. The boundary stays sharp on both sides: a moment whose answers are prose sentences or a simple numbered list needs no table and earns no piece, and a table the worksheet already prints is already in the child's hands, so only a table task recorded in books earns one.

Each write-on moment is written to `stick-in-sheets.json`, which a builder renders into one printable PDF - the *Stick-in Sheets* - that the teacher prints, cuts along the guides, and hands out for children to write on and glue in at that point in the lesson. When no moment is a write-on moment, the spec still carries an empty list and no pack is produced - the absence is the signal that nothing needed printing.

**Sizing sits between two limits.** Each piece is large enough that a child can write or draw the mark legibly *and* the skill itself survives - an acute angle must still read as acute, not shrink into a sliver - and small enough to fit the A4 book with a margin to glue it down and room for the handwriting around it, the LO above and a reasoning sentence below. The builder's book-fitting defaults already sit between these limits, so omit `widthMm` unless a specific piece genuinely needs a different size; where the two limits conflict, the writable size wins and the surrounding handwriting carries to the next page.

## The read-from moments - a source the child reads, not marks

A second, rarer kind of moment earns a printed piece for the opposite reason. Sometimes the question is not a figure the child marks but a source the child has to *read detail off*: the cells of a Victorian timetable, a line of handwriting, small figures in a photograph, place names on a map. The board can show the whole thing; it cannot show that detail large enough from the back of the room, and a class asked to "look closely" at a picture two inches tall is being asked to guess. The teacher's own fix was to take the picture out of the PowerPoint and print it. That is a **read-from moment**, and the piece for it is a `source-copy`: the same published picture the slide shows, printed at exercise-book width with a caption naming it, and nothing to write on.

The test is two questions, both of which must be yes: *do children have to read fine detail off this source to do the task?* and *is that detail genuinely too small to see on the board?* A 400-pixel photograph of an 1897 classroom under "look closely at the classroom" earns one. A portrait children glance at to see who it shows does not, because the whole scene reads from the board. Nor does a source whose adapted words already sit on the slide at reading size; the copy is for the detail the board cannot carry, never a second copy of what it can. One copy per source per moment, and a source read at two moments is still one piece: the child glued it in the first time.

## Finding the moments - the walk

Read validated `lesson-design.json` in lesson order:

1. inspect `starter`;
2. inspect each `teachingSequence` source unit in array order;
3. inspect `ending.beat` when `ending.included` is true.

For each source unit, use its exact `kind`, `pupilInstruction`, optional `taskStructure`, `content`, `representationRefs` and representation interaction to determine what pupils do. Apply the existing write-on test unchanged: the moment earns a printed piece only when pupils write, draw, sort, label or mark directly onto a figure they could not reasonably reproduce by hand.

Teacher-modelled source units still normally produce no pupil piece. `interaction: "pupil-writes-on"` is strong evidence that a visual should be checked, but it does not itself override the existing write-on test.

`lesson.json`, when present, remains the binding authority for the exact final figure to copy field-for-field. `lesson-design.json` determines which pedagogical moment is write-on.

These never produce a piece: vocabulary cards, success-criteria panels, the on-board sentence-stem frame (the empty scaffold children write *from*), answer/review slides, and any teacher-modelled My/Our Turn.

On the same walk, ask the read-from test of every source unit whose `photoRefs` carry a picture the task reads detail off, and give the moment a `source-copy` when both its questions are yes.

Walk the whole design before emitting anything, so the pack's pieces come out in lesson order and none is missed.

## Faithfulness

Re-present what the lesson designed; do not invent new pedagogy, new questions or new figures. The lesson design tells you *which* moments are write-on and carries the pedagogy; the slide fixes the exact figure to print (the spec section below explains why). You are deciding which moments earn a printed piece, not redesigning the lesson.

## The stick-in-sheets spec

`stick-in-sheets.json` is the contract with the Stick-in Sheets builder. Keep to the shape below - the builder draws only the visuals it knows, and rejects one it does not.

```json
{
  "meta": { "lesson": "<topic name>", "yearGroup": "Year 4", "subject": "Maths" },
  "items": [
    { "visual": "angle-row", "tag": "Angles", "label": "Name each angle",
      "spec": { "figures": [ {"degrees": 35, "rotation": 20}, {"degrees": 90, "rightAngle": true}, {"degrees": 130} ], "writeOnLabels": true } },
    { "visual": "venn", "tag": "Sort", "label": "Sort the shapes",
      "spec": { "label1": "has a right angle", "label2": "has 4 equal sides" } }
  ]
}
```

Each item names one write-on moment with four things: a `visual`, the `spec` that visual draws from, a short `label` (what the child is doing, shown as the page caption the teacher reads while cutting), and a `tag` - the piece's question handle, covered next.

**Give each piece a `tag`, because a cut-out is separated from everything around it.** The teacher prints the pack, cuts along the dashed guides, sorts the pieces into piles, and hands each child a piece to glue in at the right moment. Once a piece is scissored free, the only thing that says which question it is is what is printed *on the piece itself* - a caption at the top of the page goes in the bin with the offcuts. So the builder stamps the `tag` on every copy: make it the short handle the board already uses for that moment - the Your Turn question's label, "Apply", an Our Turn piece's name - so the cut-out, the board the child is working alongside, and the book all name the question the same way, and a child holding a piece can find its place on the board at a glance.

**Make the tag unique across the whole pack.** A pack often gathers moments the board labels the same way: one set of Your Turns numbered (1), (2), (3), then a second set that restarts at (1). Stamp both and two different piles read "1", which is the very confusion the tag exists to remove. Qualify each so it stands alone - "Straight 1" and "Diagonal 1", or run the whole pack straight through - whichever matches how the lesson names the moments on its slides. Keep it to a word or two so it sits comfortably above a small tile; the full instruction stays on the board, where the child reads the task, and only the handle rides on the piece. A pack with a single piece needs no tag - every copy is the same question, so there is nothing to tell apart.

The supported visuals:

- **`venn`** - a blank labelled Venn to sort into. `spec`: `{ "label1", "label2" }` - the two circle criteria; leave the regions empty.
- **`carroll`** - a blank labelled 2×2 Carroll grid. `spec`: `{ "rowLabel", "rowNotLabel", "colLabel", "colNotLabel" }`.
- **`angle-row`** - a strip of angles to name or classify, a write-on line under each. `spec`: `{ "figures": [ { "degrees", "rotation"?, "rightAngle"? } … ], "writeOnLabels": true }`. List every angle the lesson uses; the builder keeps the figures a uniform size and wraps a long strip onto more than one row.
- **`triangle-row`** - a strip of triangles to classify, a write-on line under each. `spec`: `{ "figures": [ { "kind": "scalene" | "isosceles" | "equilateral" | "right" } … ], "writeOnLabels": true }`.
- **`draw-box-row`** - a row of empty boxes for the child to draw in (e.g. "draw each angle"). `spec`: `{ "boxes": [ { "caption": "a)" } … ] }`.
- **`number-line`** - one to three aligned number lines for a child to mark, estimate or label, with a handwriting band under each. It is the same number line the board, the worksheet and the wall draw, from the same fields, so **copy the task slide's `numberline` object field for field**: `start`, `end`, `interval`, `labels`, `arrow` or `arrows`, `jumps`, `highlight`, `caption`, `showTicks`, or `lines: [...]` for a stack. The piece is the child's copy, so any `answer` dot on the slide is left off by the build; copy the task slide, not its check slide, and every number the task slide prints appears on the piece while nothing it leaves blank does. `showTicks: false` leaves only the end ticks, which is the estimation form. Default print width 130mm; a dense scale wants a larger `widthMm` on the item so a child can mark one tick without hitting its neighbour. Do not use it when children only read a line and answer somewhere else. Leave jumps off where placing them is the child's work. The older index form (`"start": "0", "end": "1000", "intervals": 10`, `tickLabels` with `given: true`, `arrows` by `index`, `startBlank`/`endBlank`) still draws, for pieces written before 13 September 2026; there, a label without `given: true` never prints.
- **`reflection-grid`** - a dot lattice with a dashed mirror line and a shape on one side, for the child to draw the reflection on by hand ("reflect this shape in the mirror line"). `spec`: `{ "cols", "rows", "mirror": { "orientation", "at" }, "shape": [[x,y], …] }` - `orientation` is `"vertical"`, `"horizontal"`, `"diagonal-up"` (slope +1) or `"diagonal-down"` (slope −1), `at` its position/intercept in squares (whole numbers keep the answer on dots); `shape` the starting shape's vertices (x across, y up). Leave `showReflection` OFF - the child draws the reflected half; this is the question form. The builder sizes it large enough to plot on and fits a book.
- **`geoboard`** - one square of dotty paper, glued in because a child cannot rule an evenly spaced peg grid by hand. `spec`: `{ "cols", "rows", "shapes"? }` - pegs sit at every whole position `0..cols` across and `0..rows` up, x across from the left and y up from the bottom. Two genuine forms, and the lesson's moment decides which: leave `shapes` OFF for *draw your own shape on the dots*, or supply `shapes` (each `[[x,y], …]`, the vertices in order) for *count the sides and corners of this one*. Both are real write-on tasks, so neither is the printed answer to the other. Copy `cols`/`rows` and the vertices field for field from the slide's `geoboard` so the glued board matches the one the class counted on. The builder sizes it large enough that a child can rule a straight side between two pegs.
- **`geoboard-row`** - a strip of dotty boards, each carrying one shape with its own write-on line beneath: count this one, name it, then the next. `spec`: `{ "figures": [ { "cols", "rows", "shapes" } … ], "writeOnLabels": true }` - one entry per shape, each the same spec a single `geoboard` takes. This is the form for a *Your Turn* that works through several shapes, because it keeps the whole set on one cut-out rather than handing a child four separate scraps to keep track of. The builder gives it a taller figure box than the angle and triangle strips get, because a dotty shape is counted a corner at a time and pegs crowded together defeat the counting, and it wraps a long strip onto more than one row.
- **`grid-map`** - a schematic river-town map on a numbered four-figure grid, glued in for the child to read human/physical features and four-figure grid references off and annotate. `spec`: `{ "eastings": [2+ consecutive numbers], "northings": [2+ consecutive numbers], "river": [[easting, northing], …], "roads"?: [[[e,n], …], …], "features": [ { "name", "square": [easting, northing], "type": "physical" | "human", "icon"? } … ], "highlightSquare"?: [easting, northing] }`. The numbers sit on the grid lines at the corners (read along the bottom, then up the side); each feature is drawn in the cell whose bottom-left corner is its `square`, every marker and label in one neutral ink so the human/physical answer is the child's to decide. Copy the spec field for field from the slide's `grid-map` so the glued map matches the board. The builder sizes it large enough to read the numbers and write a reference beside a feature.
- **`coordinate-grid`** - a numbered first-quadrant coordinate grid, glued in for a grid the child cannot rule accurately by hand in a squared book. `spec`: `{ "cols", "rows", "points"? }` - the grid runs `0..cols` across the bottom and `0..rows` up the side, numbered on the axes, origin at (0,0). The lesson's question form decides `points`: for a *plot-the-points* moment leave `points` off, so the child plots onto a blank grid; for a *complete-the-polygon* moment (the points are given and the child draws the sides between them) supply `points: [{ "x", "y", "label" }]` - the same labelled points the board shows - so the glued grid carries the figure the child works on, which is exactly the thing they cannot rule by hand. Leave `join` off in both cases: the joined shape is the answer the board reveals, not something printed on the child's piece. Copy `cols`/`rows` (and any `points`) from the slide's `coordinate-grid` (its `max`/`xMax`/`yMax`, and its plotted points) so the glued grid matches the board. The builder sizes it large enough to read the axis numbers and mark on.
- **`translation-shape`** - a numbered coordinate grid carrying a whole shape for the child to translate, glued in for a grid they cannot rule accurately by hand in a squared book. `spec`: `{ "cols", "rows", "points", "translate": { "dx", "dy" } }` - `points` is the original shape's vertices (`[[x,y], …]` or `[{ "x", "y" }, …]`, x across and y up), `translate` the `{ dx, dy }` slide in squares (positive right/up). Leave `showImage` OFF - the child plots and joins the translated image themselves on the clear grid; the image and arrow are the answer the board reveals, not something printed on the child's piece. Copy `cols`/`rows`, `points` and `translate` field for field from the slide's `translation-shape` so the glued grid matches the board. The builder sizes it large enough to read the axis numbers and plot on.
- **`rainforest-layers`** - a cross section of a tropical rainforest (four stacked bands, top to bottom: emergent, canopy, understorey, forest floor), glued in for the child to name each layer. `spec`: `{ "heights"?: true, "light"?: true }` - and nothing else is needed, because the piece is drawn in its **write-on** form automatically: the same bands and trees the board shows, with the layer names removed and a ruled line beside each band for the child to write on. The band tint carries the light gradient (brightest at the top, near dark at the floor), which is what the child reads to work out which layer is which, so it survives the small printed size. Add `light` when the sun and thinning light arrows are part of what the child is explaining. `heights` has no effect here: the write-on form drops the heights along with the layer names, because a height printed beside an empty line narrows the answer to one layer. A slide's `notes` (the short phrase per layer) is deliberately dropped here: the child is recalling the layer names, and a phrase saying what a layer is like sitting beside an empty line hands them the answer. Copy those flags from the slide's `rainforest-layers` so the glued piece matches the board. The builder sizes it large enough to write "understorey" on a line.
- **`map`** - the real shipped world map, glued in for the child to write the continent and ocean names onto. `spec`: `{ "continentMarkers": [ { "marker", "at": [x, y] } x7 ], "oceanMarkers": [ { "marker", "at": [x, y], "repeatAt"? } x5 ], "seaInitialSpaces": [[x, y], [x, y], [x, y]] }` - seven numbered continent markers, five lettered ocean markers with `repeatAt` on the Pacific so it reads as one ocean at both edges, and three dashed write-on boxes on water for sea initials; every position is a fraction of the map image. The Equator, compass and joined-edge cues print by default. The stick-in builder always forces the write-on world form, even if a labelled teaching map was copied from the slide, so the child's map stays a genuine retrieval task. The base is the same asset the board and the worksheet use, so the map in the book and the map on the board are one map rather than two drawings of the world.
- **`geographical-description-frame`** - a blank three-part frame headed **Biome**, **Location**, and **Features from evidence**, glued in so the child can write a complete geographical description without copying the frame. `spec`: `{ "responseLines"?: { "biome"?: 1-6, "location"?: 1-6, "features"?: 1-6 }, "responseHeights"?: { "biome"?: number, "location"?: number, "features"?: number } }`. The default prompts ask for biome meaning plus an example, continent plus more than one country, and two features linked to map or photograph evidence. The builder always forces task mode and removes optional answer/reveal content, so every pupil response space stays blank. This is the one piece whose response space you set rather than the builder, so count each part's lines from what its prompt asks for at the width it prints: `two features linked to evidence` is two written things, and a single line under it is the page telling the child to write less than the task asked.
- **`table`** - a write-on recording table with printed headers and given item names, blank response cells the child records decisions into (classify each device, record each result), glued in because ruling a headed multi-column grid in a squared book costs the practice time the table exists to hold. `spec`: `{ "headers": [...], "rows": [[...], ...] }` - copy both field for field from the slide's `table` so the glued grid matches the board. The piece always renders the QUESTION form: any cell carrying the `||` reveal marker is blanked automatically, so copying the task slide's blank table or the check slide's completed one yields the same write-on piece. Response cells print at handwriting height whatever the printed text needs, so the child has room for a Year 4 phrase per cell.
- **`label-diagram`**: a diagram or photo with a dot on each part and a leader line out to a blank line the child writes the part's name on (label the parts of a plant, the features of a river). `spec`: `{ "image", "callouts": [ { "anchor": [x%, y%], "label_at"?: [x%, y%] } … ] }`. `image` is the picture's path, the same one the slide's `label-diagram` shows so the piece matches the board; each `anchor` is the point on the picture a label belongs to, as percentages of width and height; `label_at` optionally places the label, otherwise it routes to the nearest margin. Leave the labels blank (no `given`): the blank line is the question form the child completes; set `given: true` on a callout only for a part you want printed for them. The builder sizes it large enough to read the picture and write a word on each line.
- **`place-value-chart`**, **`part-whole-model`**, **`pyramid`**, **`mult-grid`**, **`base-ten-blocks`**, **`counter-group`**, **`place-value-mini`** - the place value family, the same drawings the board, the worksheet and the wall use, printed in ink. Copy the slide's object exactly as it is (`spec` is the slide's fields). A write-on chart, model, pyramid or grid leaves the cells or circles the child fills blank (`""`, or `{ "blank": true }` in a part-whole node); a piece is refused, not tiled, when its spec cannot draw. Remember the test a piece has to pass: a chart a child can rule in a squared book in a minute is usually better drawn by hand.
- **`source-copy`** - the read-from piece: a printed copy of one source the child reads detail off (a document, a timetable, a photograph, a map), captioned, with nothing to write on. `spec`: `{ "imagePath", "caption", "widthMm"? }`. Copy `imagePath` and the caption from the slide that shows the source, so the paper in the child's hand and the board name the same evidence the same way. The builder prints it at exercise-book width (165mm) and keeps the picture's own proportions; a copy that would run taller than the page is refused with the height named rather than cropped, so a tall document takes a smaller `widthMm` or a crop the slide already made. **Know what that width costs before you take the default.** At 165mm one copy fills a landscape row on its own, so a child needing two of them takes two pages and a class set of two photographs is sixty-four sheets, each about a third empty. The same two at `widthMm: 130` sit side by side, so each child's pair comes off ONE page and the class set halves to thirty-two - which also keeps a child's set together, the thing the pack is laid out to do. Readable detail still decides: a dense document, a timetable, a page of handwriting keeps the full width and is worth the paper. A photograph a child is looking at for clothing, footwear or what someone is holding usually is not, so set the width the detail needs rather than letting the default set it.

Take each figure from the rendered slide that shows it, copied exactly. The lesson's slide spec (`lesson.json`) carries the precise figure the child sees on the board: the same Venn with its labels, the same reflection grid with its shape and mirror line, the same row of triangles. The stick-in piece is that identical task placed in the child's hands, so read the write-on moment's visual from the slide and reproduce its spec field for field, and the printed piece and the board become one activity. This matters most where a source unit's `content` only describes the figure in words (an "irregular 5 to 6 vertex shape", "a Venn sorting by two properties"): the slide is where that description became one concrete figure, so the slide is the binding version. Re-deriving the figure from the source-unit prose instead lets the sheet and the board settle on two different shapes, and a child reflecting the printed shape then completes a different figure from the one the board reveals, which is exactly the break a glued-in piece is meant to prevent. `lesson-design.json` still tells you *which* source units are write-on and carries the pedagogy; the slide fixes the exact figure to print. When a run skipped slides and no `lesson.json` exists, fall back to the source unit's `content` and `representationRefs`, since there is then no board for the piece to match. A `widthMm` may sit on an item to set a specific printed size, but omit it by default and let the builder use its book-fitting size. If a write-on moment needs a visual not in this list, leave it out and flag it in your final report rather than forcing a poor fit.
