# Stick-in Sheets Pedagogy

This reference carries the thinking behind the Stick-in Sheets. The `stick-in-sheets-designer` agent reads it before working. It exists because the teacher's school has slow, difficult printing and does not routinely glue worksheets into books - the lesson's slides carry the learning on their own, and a child's exercise book evidences the thinking in the child's own hand. The one deliberate exception is a *stick-in piece* for a visual a child cannot reproduce by hand - printed sparingly and glued in. Holding the pack to the few moments that genuinely need it is what keeps it worth the paper.

## The write-on moments - a visual the child marks, not copies

Most writing moments leave an **answer** in the book while the question that prompted it stays on the board: a child reading "35 + 40 =" writes "75", and the book holds the answer. A few moments are different in kind - the question *is* a picture the child marks, sorts, labels or draws on: a Venn to sort shapes into, an angle to name, a row of triangles to classify, a diagram whose parts the child labels (the parts of a plant, the features of a river), a box to draw an angle in. A child cannot reproduce that picture by hand in a squared book, so copying it off the board breaks down exactly where the learning happens. These are the **write-on moments**, and they are the one case where a printed piece glued into the book serves the book-first goal better than the child's own copying.

Apply one question to each moment: *does the child write onto a figure they could not redraw by hand?* If they do, it is a write-on moment. If they are recording an answer the board already carries - an arithmetic result, a fluency list, a reasoning sentence - it stays in the book in the child's own hand, never on a printed piece. Holding the pieces to the few moments that genuinely need them is what keeps them worth the paper, and it is why a pure-arithmetic lesson produces none at all. This question decides whether a moment earns a piece. Once it has one, the child works on that sheet and everything its task asks them to write goes on it (`Every answer has its place on the sheet`, below).

**A recording table's furniture is a figure.** When a moment asks children to record into a multi-column table or grid - a classification table with a decision, source and explanation per device, a results table with headed columns - the headers and ruled columns are structure the child would have to rule and copy before any thinking starts, and by the time thirty children have drawn four columns the lesson has spent its practice time on furniture. That moment passes the write-on test: the table is the figure, and the child's decisions go into printed cells. The boundary stays sharp on both sides: a moment whose answers are prose sentences or a simple numbered list needs no table and earns no piece, and a table the worksheet already prints is already in the child's hands, so only a table task recorded in books earns one.

Each write-on moment is written to `stick-in-sheets.json`, which a builder renders into one printable PDF - the *Stick-in Sheets* - that the teacher prints, cuts along the guides, and hands out for children to write on and glue in at that point in the lesson. When no moment is a write-on moment, the spec still carries an empty list and no pack is produced - the absence is the signal that nothing needed printing.

**Sizing sits between two limits.** Each piece is large enough that a child can write or draw the mark legibly *and* the skill itself survives - an acute angle must still read as acute, not shrink into a sliver - and small enough to fit the A4 book with a margin to glue it down and room for the handwriting around it, the LO above and a reasoning sentence below. The builder's book-fitting defaults already sit between these limits, so omit `widthMm` unless a specific piece genuinely needs a different size; where the two limits conflict, the writable size wins and the surrounding handwriting carries to the next page.

## The read-from moments - a source the child reads, not marks

A second, rarer kind of moment earns a printed piece for the opposite reason. Sometimes the question is not a figure the child marks but a source the child has to *read detail off*: the cells of a Victorian timetable, a line of handwriting, small figures in a photograph, place names on a map. The board can show the whole thing; it cannot show that detail large enough from the back of the room, and a class asked to "look closely" at a picture two inches tall is being asked to guess. The teacher's own fix was to take the picture out of the PowerPoint and print it. That is a **read-from moment**, and the piece for it is a `source-copy`: the same published picture the slide shows, printed at exercise-book width with a caption naming it, and nothing to write on.

**A source made of words is read from in a different way, and it earns its own piece.** A picture is read from because the detail is small; an account, a letter, a diary entry, a recipe, a set of rules or a poem is read from because the task asks children to *quote* it, and a child copying a quotation off the board gets it wrong, gets it slowly, or writes their own version of it. So the test for a text source is one question: does the task ask children to take the source's own words into their answer? When it does, the piece is a `source-text` and it carries the words at reading width with the title and attribution attached. A Year 4 history lesson asked children to fill a record about Patience Kershaw "using her own words" from an account that only the board carried, in the same lesson whose worksheet printed a second account for exactly that reason (17 September 2026); the two beats made the same demand and only one of them was resourced.

Default it to `per: "pair"`. Nobody writes on it, so one between two is usually right and halves the paper; `per: "child"` is for the task a child works entirely alone, and the worksheet is usually the better home for that anyway. A source whose words are already on the slide at reading size and which children only refer to, without quoting, needs no piece.

The test is two questions, both of which must be yes: *do children have to read fine detail off this source to do the task?* and *is that detail genuinely too small to see on the board?* A 400-pixel photograph of an 1897 classroom under "look closely at the classroom" earns one. A portrait children glance at to see who it shows does not, because the whole scene reads from the board. Nor does a source whose adapted words already sit on the slide at reading size; the copy is for the detail the board cannot carry, never a second copy of what it can. One copy per source per moment, and a source read at two moments is still one piece: the child glued it in the first time.

## The handling moments - items the child moves, not marks

A handling moment prints in one of two forms, and the Lesson Designer's `handling.kind` has already chosen which: `cards`, cut out and moved under heading cards, described below; or `sheet`, every item on one landscape page with its picture as large as the page allows and a box in its corner, printed one per child, pair or group and never cut, the key or the places (`Write 1 to 5`) printed at the top. A sheet is a task where a mark on each item is the whole decision, like putting a story's pictures in order; cards are for a task where moving things round and changing your mind is how children decide. Copy the form into the kit's `form`; the builder, the key and the checks are the same for both.

The third kind of moment, and the one that is not glued in. When the lesson design says a sort is done with printed cards (`taskStructure.handling.kind` is `cards`), children sit at tables with a set of cards and a pair of heading cards and move the cards under the headings; the board shows the same task for the teacher to point at, and the answer is heard and seen when the teacher goes round. The Lesson Designer makes that choice, not you, because it is a decision about where the substantial work sits and what a calm move to tables buys: a Year 4 class spent a history lesson on the carpet drawing two columns on whiteboards for sorts they could have done with cards at tables in half the time (15 September 2026). Your job is to print the kit faithfully.

Three things make a kit faithful, and the builder refuses one that is not. The cards are the unit's own sort items and the headings its groups, word for word and id for id, so the kit and the board are one activity. The pupil pages never carry the key: the cards print in an order that is not the key's order, all the same size and shape, with the kit's short `tag` stamped small on every card so a stray card still says which activity it belongs to, and never which heading. And the key, the acceptance note and the preparation line go to a separate teacher text file beside the pack, copied from the unit's structured answer, its `acceptanceCondition` and its `handling.where`. A write-in grid is not a substitute: the whole point is that the child moves the card and can change their mind.

A card kit is not a sort on every slide, and it is not a bonus. The design chooses it for the beat where handling earns its time (usually the main work, or the beat that prepares it), and one kit per lesson is the ordinary case. A sort the class does from the board on whiteboards has no `handling` block and gets no kit.

## The lesson's printed levels come first

A design whose beats carry `levels` has already decided what prints: the lesson designer chose, beat by beat, the sheet that changes what children do or record, and the teacher picks it or the board on the day. Build one printed piece for every beat whose `levels.printed` is set, in lesson order, in the form it names, and nothing for a beat it leaves `null` (a `figure` piece is one sheet, which holds every figure that beat's task needs):

- `sort`: a `card-set` from the beat's sort, `form` taken from its `handling` (`sheet` prints every item on one page for children to mark, and a word sort into a few groups becomes a table they tick; give it `reference`, the rule or fact children decide by as the board states it, printed at the top as the clue); `cards` prints cards to cut and move.
- `source`: a `source-text` for words, a `source-copy` for a picture.
- `figure`: the figure the slide shows, copied field for field, as a whole page under its task.
- `task`: a `task-sheet` with `task`, the `material` children work on (`speaker` and `text` for a named child's claim, `text` for a case or a worked answer, `imagePath` and `caption` for a picture), any smaller `prompts`, and lines to answer on filling the page.

Every piece names its beat in `sourceUnitId` and carries `task`, the job as the board gives it, printed at the top like a mini worksheet. Each prints as its own file (`Activity 1 - Sarah's words`), a whole page each, one between two unless `per` says otherwise, two to a page when the piece is still big enough to use (a ten frame two to a page has boxes a counter fits in); nothing is cut unless cutting is the task (`layout: "slips"` keeps the older several-to-a-page copies for a piece glued into books because gluing it in is the job).

**One task is one sheet.** When a beat's task needs more than one figure (a tally chart and the bar chart drawn from it, three photographs to name features on, three pairs of bars each with its own sum), write one item per figure, all naming that beat in `sourceUnitId`, and give `task` once, on the first. The builder prints them together as one sheet and one file, named for the first item's `label`, the way round that prints the figures largest. A later item's own `task` prints above its figure as that figure's step (`(2) Draw a bar for each minibeast.`), so give a later item a `task` only when it is one. When the teacher hands out figures from two beats as one sheet, give every one of those items the same `sheet` name, which becomes the file's name. A sheet of four photographs written as four separate pieces cost 64 pages where 16 did the job, and each child juggled four sheets for one task (7 October 2026).

**Every answer has its place on the sheet.** Once a beat has a printed piece, the whole task is done on it and nothing for that task goes in the book: a child sent from a grid on paper to a book and back loses their place in both, and the teacher asked for one sheet that holds the task (10 October 2026). Read the task for each thing it asks the child to write, and give each one its place:

- **On the figure itself**, when the answer is a mark the figure already has room for (shade it, plot on it, fill its cells, a row figure's own line). Set `"write": "on the figure"`, which records that you looked.
- **A box in a sum**: an entry of `write` with `?` or `[ ]` where the answer goes (`"(1) 1/2 = ?/10"`) prints beside its figure with a box there.
- **A short rule for a number or a word inside given words**: type two underscores where each goes (`"J' ( __ , __ )"` for a coordinate, brackets and comma given).
- **A ruled line**: any other entry (`"(1) 1/4 ="`) prints with a line running on from the words, for an answer of several words.

`write` is a list with one entry per answer, in the board's words. A sum the child completes belongs there, not in the figure's own labels, where it prints small with nowhere to put the answer. List only what the task asks for: a line nobody was asked to write on tells the child there is more to do.

When the task is in numbered parts (`(1) Plot triangle JKL: ... (2) Plot rectangle PQRS: ...`), keep the board's wording and its numbers, and start each entry of `write` with the number of the part it answers (`"(2) P' ( __ , __ )"`). The builder then prints each part as its own block with its answers under it, beside the figure when the figure is about as tall as it is wide, so the task never prints as one paragraph.

Words a child reads to do one figure of a row go under that figure, as its `caption` (the time to show on a blank clock face), not in the task line: four times listed in one sentence left a Year 2 child matching each to its clock by a small letter (7 October 2026). The task line then says the job once (`Draw the hands to show each time.`).

`resource-opportunities.py stick-in-kits` prints `STICK_IN_WRITING_PLACE:` for a sheet whose task asks for writing and names none of these; the designer runs it before returning.

**The page sets the size.** On a whole page the builder prints each figure as large as its space allows, growing a drawing's boxes and words together where that is what fills the page, so `widthMm` does nothing there. It sets the size of a `slips` piece only. Coins and a ruler stay life size wherever they print. `resource-opportunities.py stick-in-kits` refuses a pack missing a printed level the design set. The walk below is for a design with no `levels`, made before 1 October 2026.

## Finding the moments - the walk

Read validated `lesson-design.json` in lesson order:

1. inspect `starter`;
2. inspect each `teachingSequence` source unit in array order;
3. inspect `ending.beat` when `ending.included` is true.

For each source unit, use its exact `kind`, `pupilInstruction`, optional `taskStructure`, `content`, `representationRefs` and representation interaction to determine what pupils do. Apply the existing write-on test unchanged: the moment earns a printed piece only when pupils write, draw, sort, label or mark directly onto a figure they could not reasonably reproduce by hand.

Teacher-modelled source units still normally produce no pupil piece. `interaction: "pupil-writes-on"` is strong evidence that a visual should be checked, but it does not itself override the existing write-on test.

`lesson.json`, when present, remains the binding authority for the exact final figure to copy field-for-field. `lesson-design.json` determines which pedagogical moment is write-on.

These never produce a piece: vocabulary cards, success-criteria panels, the on-board sentence-stem frame (the empty scaffold children write *from*), answer/review slides, and any teacher-modelled My/Our Turn.

On the same walk, ask the read-from test of every source unit whose `photoRefs` carry a picture the task reads detail off, and give the moment a `source-copy` when both its questions are yes. Ask the text question of every source unit whose teaching carries an account, an extract or a passage: when the task asks children to use its own words, the moment takes a `source-text`.

**The size of the beat decides, not the wording.** A quick marking move is whiteboard work: children read the line off the board and write the phrase, and printing a class set for thirty seconds of thinking is paper nobody needed. What has to arrive fully resourced is the beat that is a main activity in miniature, where children need two things at once - something to work into and something to work from - and keep what they write. That beat is the one the check looks at: `resource-opportunities.py stick-in-kits` prints `STICK_IN_SOURCE_FAULT` when a Do beat hands children a representation to complete, asks them to use the source's own words, and no source is printed for them to work from. A quick beat that says `underline` is left alone.

Walk the whole design before emitting anything, so the pack's pieces come out in lesson order and none is missed.

## Faithfulness

Re-present what the lesson designed; do not invent new pedagogy, new questions or new figures. The lesson design tells you *which* moments are write-on and carries the pedagogy; the slide fixes the exact figure to print (the spec section below explains why). You are deciding which moments earn a printed piece, not redesigning the lesson.

## The stick-in-sheets spec

`stick-in-sheets.json` is the contract with the Stick-in Sheets builder. Keep to the shape below - the builder draws only the visuals it knows, and rejects one it does not.

```json
{
  "meta": { "lesson": "<topic name>", "yearGroup": "Year 4", "subject": "Maths" },
  "items": [
    { "visual": "angle-row", "tag": "Angles", "label": "Name each angle",
      "spec": { "figures": [ {"degrees": 35, "rotation": 20}, {"degrees": 90, "rightAngle": true}, {"degrees": 130} ], "writeOnLabels": true } },
    { "visual": "venn", "tag": "Sort", "label": "Sort the shapes",
      "spec": { "label1": "has a right angle", "label2": "has 4 equal sides" } }
  ]
}
```

Each item names one write-on moment with four things: a `visual`, the `spec` that visual draws from, a short `label` (what the child is doing, shown as the page caption the teacher reads while cutting), and a `tag` - the piece's question handle, covered next.

**Give each piece a `tag`, because a cut-out is separated from everything around it.** The teacher prints the pack, cuts along the dashed guides, sorts the pieces into piles, and hands each child a piece to glue in at the right moment. Once a piece is scissored free, the only thing that says which question it is is what is printed *on the piece itself* - a caption at the top of the page goes in the bin with the offcuts. So the builder stamps the `tag` on every copy: make it the short handle the board already uses for that moment - the Your Turn question's label, "Apply", an Our Turn piece's name - so the cut-out, the board the child is working alongside, and the book all name the question the same way, and a child holding a piece can find its place on the board at a glance.

**Make the tag unique across the whole pack.** A pack often gathers moments the board labels the same way: one set of Your Turns numbered (1), (2), (3), then a second set that restarts at (1). Stamp both and two different piles read "1", which is the very confusion the tag exists to remove. Qualify each so it stands alone - "Straight 1" and "Diagonal 1", or run the whole pack straight through - whichever matches how the lesson names the moments on its slides. Keep it to a word or two so it sits comfortably above a small tile; the full instruction stays on the board, where the child reads the task, and only the handle rides on the piece. A pack with a single piece needs no tag - every copy is the same question, so there is nothing to tell apart.

The supported visuals:

- **`venn`** - a blank labelled Venn to sort into. `spec`: `{ "label1", "label2" }` - the two circle criteria; leave the regions empty.
- **`carroll`** - a blank labelled 2×2 Carroll grid. `spec`: `{ "rowLabel", "rowNotLabel", "colLabel", "colNotLabel" }`.
- **`angle-row`** - a strip of angles to name or classify, a write-on line under each. `spec`: `{ "figures": [ { "degrees", "rotation"?, "rightAngle"? } … ], "writeOnLabels": true }`. List every angle the lesson uses; the builder keeps the figures a uniform size and wraps a long strip onto more than one row.
- **`triangle-row`** - a strip of triangles to classify, a write-on line under each. `spec`: `{ "figures": [ { "kind": "scalene" | "isosceles" | "equilateral" | "right" } … ], "writeOnLabels": true }`.
- **`draw-box-row`** - a row of empty boxes for the child to draw in (e.g. "draw each angle"). `spec`: `{ "boxes": [ { "caption": "a)" } … ] }`.
- **`number-line`** - one to three aligned number lines for a child to mark, estimate or label, with a handwriting band under each. It is the same number line the board, the worksheet and the wall draw, from the same fields, so **copy the task slide's `numberline` object field for field**: `start`, `end`, `interval`, `labels`, `arrow` or `arrows`, `jumps`, `highlight`, `caption`, `showTicks`, or `lines: [...]` for a stack. The piece is the child's copy, so any `answer` dot on the slide is left off by the build; copy the task slide, not its check slide, and every number the task slide prints appears on the piece while nothing it leaves blank does. `showTicks: false` leaves only the end ticks, which is the estimation form. Default print width 130mm; a dense scale wants a larger `widthMm` on the item so a child can mark one tick without hitting its neighbour. Do not use it when children only read a line and answer somewhere else. Leave jumps off where placing them is the child's work. The older index form (`"start": "0", "end": "1000", "intervals": 10`, `tickLabels` with `given: true`, `arrows` by `index`, `startBlank`/`endBlank`) still draws, for pieces written before 13 September 2026; there, a label without `given: true` never prints.
- **`shaded-fraction`** - a shape cut into equal parts for a child to shade, or to read the shaded fraction off: the same drawing as the slide's `shaded-fraction`, so **copy the slide's object**: `parts`, `shaded` (0 for a blank shape the child shades), `shape` (`bar`, `grid` or `circle`), `rows`, or `bars` for a stack to compare. It prints in grey on the photocopy. Default print width 110mm. Do not use it when the child only answers a question about a shape shown on the board.
- **`fraction-wall`** - the slide's fraction wall glued in so a child can rule across it and compare rows. `spec`: `{ "fractions": [1, 2, 4, 8] }`, copied from the slide. Default print width 130mm, which keeps a twelfths row nameable.
- **`money`** - a row of the real coin and note pictures to count, total or sort, copied from the slide's `money` object: `{ "items": ["£1", "50p", "20p", "|", "10p"] }`, with `"|"` for a gap between two groups. Coins print at life size and to scale with each other. Use it when a child writes on or beside the coins (circle the coins that make 75p); a total written in the book under a slide's coins does not need a piece.
- **The measuring scales and thinking diagrams** - `dial-scale`, `measuring-jug`, `ruler`, `timeline`, `process-chain`, `classification-key`, `concept-map`, `fishbone`, `continuum-line`, `source-pathway` and `number-network`. Each is the same drawing the board, the worksheet and the wall draw, from the same fields, printed in ink at a size a child can write on, so **copy the task slide's object field for field** as the `spec` and leave out only `type`. Glue one in when the child works ON the drawing and cannot draw it well enough by hand: marks a level on an empty jug (leave `value` off), reads a dial, measures against a `ruler` (it prints at true size, so a real ruler lines up with it), places dates on a timeline, fills the empty boxes of a chain (`null` boxes) or the blank circles of a network (`value: null`), follows a key, or marks a position on a continuum. A copy carrying the finished answer is a reference, not a write-on moment, so blank what the child is to write. A `label` in the spec is the board's caption and does not print on the piece; the item's own `label` names the moment.
- **`annotated-text`** - a passage the child marks up by hand: a poem, a model paragraph, a sentence or a source extract set in the middle with room around it, because a child cannot copy a whole passage into a book to annotate it. It is the same drawing the board and the wall draw, so **copy the slide's object** and change only what makes it the child's task: keep the marks the board gave and turn the ones the child adds into write-on lines (`"note": ""`), or drop the marks and set `"space": "annotate"` for wide empty margins and roomy lines. Default print width 180mm. Do not use it for a passage the child only reads; that is sheet text.
- **`reflection-grid`** - a dot lattice with a dashed mirror line and a shape on one side, for the child to draw the reflection on by hand ("reflect this shape in the mirror line"). `spec`: `{ "cols", "rows", "mirror": { "orientation", "at" }, "shape": [[x,y], …] }` - `orientation` is `"vertical"`, `"horizontal"`, `"diagonal-up"` (slope +1) or `"diagonal-down"` (slope −1), `at` its position/intercept in squares (whole numbers keep the answer on dots); `shape` the starting shape's vertices (x across, y up). Leave `showReflection` OFF - the child draws the reflected half; this is the question form. The builder sizes it large enough to plot on and fits a book.
- **`geoboard`** - one square of dotty paper, glued in because a child cannot rule an evenly spaced peg grid by hand. `spec`: `{ "cols", "rows", "shapes"? }` - pegs sit at every whole position `0..cols` across and `0..rows` up, x across from the left and y up from the bottom. Two genuine forms, and the lesson's moment decides which: leave `shapes` OFF for *draw your own shape on the dots*, or supply `shapes` (each `[[x,y], …]`, the vertices in order) for *count the sides and corners of this one*. Both are real write-on tasks, so neither is the printed answer to the other. Copy `cols`/`rows` and the vertices field for field from the slide's `geoboard` so the glued board matches the one the class counted on. The builder sizes it large enough that a child can rule a straight side between two pegs.
- **`geoboard-row`** - a strip of dotty boards, each carrying one shape with its own write-on line beneath: count this one, name it, then the next. `spec`: `{ "figures": [ { "cols", "rows", "shapes" } … ], "writeOnLabels": true }` - one entry per shape, each the same spec a single `geoboard` takes. This is the form for a *Your Turn* that works through several shapes, because it keeps the whole set on one cut-out rather than handing a child four separate scraps to keep track of. The builder gives it a taller figure box than the angle and triangle strips get, because a dotty shape is counted a corner at a time and pegs crowded together defeat the counting, and it wraps a long strip onto more than one row.
- **`clock`** - one clock face, the same clock the board draws from the same fields, so **copy the slide's `clock` object**: `time`, `hands`, `colourCoded`, `minuteRing`. A blank face (`"hands": false`) is the draw-the-hands task; a face with hands is read and the time written beside it. Default print width 60mm, so the five-minute ticks are far enough apart to aim a hand at. For more than one face, use `clock-row`, which gives each face its own time or its own line.
- **`clock-row`** - a strip of clock faces, each the same fields a single `clock` takes. Two forms, and the task decides which. Read the time: faces with hands and a write-on line beneath each, `{ "figures": [ { "time": "3:45" }, { "time": "4:15" } ], "writeOnLabels": true }`. Draw the hands: blank faces with the time to show printed under each as its `caption`, `{ "figures": [ { "hands": false, "caption": "quarter past 4" }, { "hands": false, "caption": "quarter to 7" } ] }`. Any row figure (`angle-row`, `triangle-row`, `geoboard-row`) takes a `caption` the same way, printed in place of that figure's line. On a whole page up to five figures print side by side.
- **`turn-diagram`** - one turn to name, the same turn diagram the board draws: copy the slide's `quarters` or `amount`, `direction` and `countMarks`.
- **`triangle-square`** - the SATs part-whole puzzle to complete in the book, copied from the slide's `triangles` and `square`. Exactly one shape must be `""`, the one the child writes in; a piece with no blank or two blanks is refused, because it is not a question.
- **`polygon`** - one or more shapes to measure or to draw the lines of symmetry on, the same shapes the board draws from the same fields (`shapes` with `name`, `sideLabels`, `angleLabels`, `candidate`). The child's copy never carries `symmetryLines`, `verdict` or `fold`, so copying the answer slide still gives the question.
- **`translation-grid`** - a numbered grid with a marker moved from one position to another, copied from the slide's `max`, `from`, `to` and labels. Leave the arrow off (`"showArrow": false`) when working out the direction of the move is the child's task.
- **`area-grid`** - a squared grid of labelled patches for the child to count, copied from the slide's `cols`, `rows`, `rects` and `unitLabel`. Default print width 110mm, so a square is big enough to count.
- **`comparison-slot`** - the ring a child writes `<`, `>` or `=` into, with the two values either side (`left`, `right`), the same ring the board draws. The piece never prints the slide's `answer`.
- **`grid-map`** - a schematic river-town map on a numbered four-figure grid, glued in for the child to read human/physical features and four-figure grid references off and annotate. `spec`: `{ "eastings": [2+ consecutive numbers], "northings": [2+ consecutive numbers], "river": [[easting, northing], …], "roads"?: [[[e,n], …], …], "features": [ { "name", "square": [easting, northing], "type": "physical" | "human", "icon"? } … ], "highlightSquare"?: [easting, northing] }`. The numbers sit on the grid lines at the corners (read along the bottom, then up the side); each feature is drawn in the cell whose bottom-left corner is its `square`, every marker and label in one neutral ink so the human/physical answer is the child's to decide. Copy the spec field for field from the slide's `grid-map` so the glued map matches the board. The builder sizes it large enough to read the numbers and write a reference beside a feature.
- **`coordinate-grid`** - a numbered first-quadrant coordinate grid, glued in for a grid the child cannot rule accurately by hand in a squared book. `spec`: `{ "cols", "rows", "points"? }` - the grid runs `0..cols` across the bottom and `0..rows` up the side, numbered on the axes, origin at (0,0). The lesson's question form decides `points`: for a *plot-the-points* moment leave `points` off, so the child plots onto a blank grid; for a *complete-the-polygon* moment (the points are given and the child draws the sides between them) supply `points: [{ "x", "y", "label" }]` - the same labelled points the board shows - so the glued grid carries the figure the child works on, which is exactly the thing they cannot rule by hand. Leave `join` off in both cases: the joined shape is the answer the board reveals, not something printed on the child's piece. Copy `cols`/`rows` (and any `points`) from the slide's `coordinate-grid` (its `max`/`xMax`/`yMax`, and its plotted points) so the glued grid matches the board. The builder sizes it large enough to read the axis numbers and mark on.
- **`translation-shape`** - a numbered coordinate grid carrying a whole shape for the child to translate, glued in for a grid they cannot rule accurately by hand in a squared book. `spec`: `{ "cols", "rows", "points", "translate": { "dx", "dy" } }` - `points` is the original shape's vertices (`[[x,y], …]` or `[{ "x", "y" }, …]`, x across and y up), `translate` the `{ dx, dy }` slide in squares (positive right/up). Leave `showImage` OFF - the child plots and joins the translated image themselves on the clear grid; the image and arrow are the answer the board reveals, not something printed on the child's piece. Copy `cols`/`rows`, `points` and `translate` field for field from the slide's `translation-shape` so the glued grid matches the board. The builder sizes it large enough to read the axis numbers and plot on.
- **`rainforest-layers`** - a cross section of a tropical rainforest (four stacked bands, top to bottom: emergent, canopy, understorey, forest floor), glued in for the child to name each layer. `spec`: `{ "heights"?: true, "light"?: true }` - and nothing else is needed, because the piece is drawn in its **write-on** form automatically: the same bands and trees the board shows, with the layer names removed and a ruled line beside each band for the child to write on. The band tint carries the light gradient (brightest at the top, near dark at the floor), which is what the child reads to work out which layer is which, so it survives the small printed size. Add `light` when the sun and thinning light arrows are part of what the child is explaining. `heights` has no effect here: the write-on form drops the heights along with the layer names, because a height printed beside an empty line narrows the answer to one layer. A slide's `notes` (the short phrase per layer) is deliberately dropped here: the child is recalling the layer names, and a phrase saying what a layer is like sitting beside an empty line hands them the answer. Copy those flags from the slide's `rainforest-layers` so the glued piece matches the board. The builder sizes it large enough to write "understorey" on a line.
- **`map`** - the real shipped world map, glued in for the child to write the continent and ocean names onto. `spec`: `{ "continentMarkers": [ { "marker", "at": [x, y] } x7 ], "oceanMarkers": [ { "marker", "at": [x, y], "repeatAt"? } x5 ], "seaInitialSpaces": [[x, y], [x, y], [x, y]] }` - seven numbered continent markers, five lettered ocean markers with `repeatAt` on the Pacific so it reads as one ocean at both edges, and three dashed write-on boxes on water for sea initials; every position is a fraction of the map image. The Equator, compass and joined-edge cues print by default. An ordinary world map copied from a slide is still turned into this write-on form, so the child's map stays a genuine retrieval task. Two other maps print as the slide drew them, because the child reads the answer off their evidence rather than finding it printed: a world map with shaded areas and named sites (circle E or F), and South America's `presentation: "regional-layers"` map with its country names and shaded Amazon rainforest, for a read-from question such as which other country has some Amazon rainforest. Copy that object from the slide; `worksheetMode: "regional-marking"` removes the shading when the child is the one shading it. It is the one shared map the board, the worksheet and the wall draw too, laid out at the width the piece prints (150mm unless `widthMm` says otherwise) so its markers and write-on boxes are sized for a Year 4 pencil, in ink because the pack is photocopied; the map in the book and the map on the board are one map rather than two drawings of the world. Markers are printed where you put them, so two positions that would print on top of each other are refused by name (`MAP_MARKERS_OVERLAP`) and the piece is skipped with that reason: move one, or give the piece a wider `widthMm`.
- **`geographical-description-frame`** - a blank three-part frame headed **Biome**, **Location**, and **Features from evidence**, glued in so the child can write a complete geographical description without copying the frame. `spec`: `{ "responseLines"?: { "biome"?: 1-6, "location"?: 1-6, "features"?: 1-6 }, "responseHeights"?: { "biome"?: number, "location"?: number, "features"?: number } }`. The default prompts ask for biome meaning plus an example, continent plus more than one country, and two features linked to map or photograph evidence. The builder always forces task mode and removes optional answer/reveal content, so every pupil response space stays blank. This is the one piece whose response space you set rather than the builder, so count each part's lines from what its prompt asks for at the width it prints: `two features linked to evidence` is two written things, and a single line under it is the page telling the child to write less than the task asked.
- **`table`** - a write-on recording table with printed headers and given item names, blank response cells the child records decisions into (classify each device, record each result), glued in because ruling a headed multi-column grid in a squared book costs the practice time the table exists to hold. `spec`: `{ "headers": [...], "rows": [[...], ...] }` - copy both field for field from the slide's `table` so the glued grid matches the board. The piece always renders the QUESTION form: any cell carrying the `||` reveal marker is blanked automatically, so copying the task slide's blank table or the check slide's completed one yields the same write-on piece. Response cells print at handwriting height whatever the printed text needs, so the child has room for a Year 4 phrase per cell.
- **`label-diagram`**: a diagram or photo with a dot on each part and a leader line out to a blank line the child writes the part's name on (label the parts of a plant, the features of a river). `spec`: `{ "image", "callouts": [ { "anchor": [x%, y%], "label_at"?: [x%, y%] } … ] }`. `image` is the picture's path, the same one the slide's `label-diagram` shows so the piece matches the board; each `anchor` is the point on the picture a label belongs to, as percentages of width and height; `label_at` optionally places the label, otherwise it routes to the nearest margin. Leave the labels blank (no `given`): the blank line is the question form the child completes; set `given: true` on a callout only for a part you want printed for them. The builder sizes it large enough to read the picture and write a word on each line. A picture the board marks with letters or numbers for children to name (every callout `given`, each label a single letter or number, as the slide has it) is copied the same way, callouts as the slide wrote them: on a page each letter prints in a circle on the thing it marks, with a ruled line for it beneath the picture, so the picture keeps the whole of its space and needs no typed underscores or `label_at`.
- **The board's other pictures**: `line-pair`, `bar-model`, `tally-chart`, `pictogram`, `blank-surface`, `balanced-pattern-plate`, `circuit-diagram`, `circuit-symbol-bank`, `parachute-forces`, `bar-chart` and `line-graph`. Each is the one drawing the board, the worksheet and the wall use, so **copy the slide's object field for field** into `spec` (the `type` is the item's `visual`). The pack can print every one; whether a moment earns one is still the write-on test above. The ones that pass it most often: a `tally-chart` with `blank: true` to tally into, a `bar-model` with a `?` part to fill, a `blank-surface` to draw jumps or partitions on, and a chart or graph glued beside the questions a child answers in their book when the board could not show its scale from the back of the room (then it is a read-from piece, and copy the task slide, never its answer slide). The builder prints each at the smallest width its own words stay readable at; a `widthMm` on the item overrides it.
- **`digit-cards`** - a number drawn large as digit cards with the working of a digit check marked on it: the child circles the digit a step looks at, or finishes the working the board started. Copy the slide's object and change only what makes it the child's task: a `label`, `note` or `working` line the child writes becomes `""` (a ruled line), and a mark the child makes comes off. Default print width 110mm. Do not use it for a number the child only reads; that is sheet text.
- **`place-value-chart`**, **`part-whole-model`**, **`pyramid`**, **`mult-grid`**, **`base-ten-blocks`**, **`counter-group`**, **`place-value-mini`** - the place value family, the same drawings the board, the worksheet and the wall use, printed in ink. Copy the slide's object exactly as it is (`spec` is the slide's fields). A write-on chart, model, pyramid or grid leaves the cells or circles the child fills blank (`""`, or `{ "blank": true }` in a part-whole node); a piece is refused, not tiled, when its spec cannot draw. Remember the test a piece has to pass: a chart a child can rule in a squared book in a minute is usually better drawn by hand.
- **`source-copy`** - the read-from piece: a printed copy of one source the child reads detail off (a document, a timetable, a photograph, a map), captioned, with nothing to write on. When a published picture is itself the thing a child marks and no drawing in this list can make it (a blank coordinate grid the board shows as a picture), it is a figure like any other: give it `task` and `write`. `spec`: `{ "imagePath", "caption", "widthMm"? }`. Copy `imagePath` and the caption from the slide that shows the source, so the paper in the child's hand and the board name the same evidence the same way. The builder prints it at exercise-book width (165mm) and keeps the picture's own proportions; a copy that would run taller than the page is refused with the height named rather than cropped, so a tall document takes a smaller `widthMm` or a crop the slide already made. **Know what that width costs before you take the default.** At 165mm one copy fills a landscape row on its own, so a child needing two of them takes two pages and a class set of two photographs is sixty-four sheets, each about a third empty. The same two at `widthMm: 130` sit side by side, so each child's pair comes off ONE page and the class set halves to thirty-two - which also keeps a child's set together, the thing the pack is laid out to do. Readable detail still decides: a dense document, a timetable, a page of handwriting keeps the full width and is worth the paper. A photograph a child is looking at for clothing, footwear or what someone is holding usually is not, so set the width the detail needs rather than letting the default set it.

- **`source-text`** - the read-from piece for a source made of words: an account, a letter, a diary entry, a recipe, a set of rules, a poem. `spec`: `{ "title", "text", "attribution"?, "per"?, "widthMm"? }`. `title` says what the source is, because once the slip is cut free of the page it is the only thing naming what the child is holding; `text` is the words exactly as the child reads them, and its own line breaks are kept, so a witness answer per line, a verse or a numbered rule prints the way it reads. `attribution` is who said it, to whom and when, printed small underneath. `per` is `"pair"` by default, which prints one between two, or `"child"` for one each. Nothing is written on it: it is what a pair reads while they each write somewhere else, which is why it is not part of any child's glued-in set and prints on its own pages with cut guides. The default width is 130mm, narrower than a picture's, because prose past about seventy characters a line is harder to read and two copies of that width sit side by side; a copy too tall for one page is refused with the height named rather than cut short, since a source with its last paragraph missing is a different source.

- **`card-set`** - the handling kit: a set of cards and heading cards for one sort the design does with printed cards at tables; its key is in the slide notes for the same sort, and no answers file is printed. Unlike every piece above, it is copied from `lesson-design.json`, not from the slide, because the identity that matters is the unit's own `taskStructure` and structured answer. `spec`: `{ "sourceUnitId", "form": "cards" | "sheet", "instruction", "headings": [{ "id", "label" }], "cards": [{ "id", "label", "detail"?, "photoRef"?, "imagePath"? }], "sets": { "per": "child" | "pair" | "group", "groupCount": null | n }, "teacher": { "where", "answer": [{ "cardId", "headingId" }], "alsoAccept": null | "..." } }`. Copy `headings` from the unit's `groups`, `cards` from its `items` (id, label and any detail, which prints under the label; for an item with a `photoRef`, that `photoRef` and the `imagePath` the photo contract gives it, the same published picture the board shows, which prints above the label), `instruction` from its `pupilInstruction`, `answer` from its `answer.structure.placements` (itemRef to cardId, groupRef to headingId), `alsoAccept` from its `answer.acceptanceCondition`, and `form`, `sets` and `where` from its `handling` (`form` is its `kind`). Set the item's `tag` to the short handle the board uses for that beat and its `label` to the beat's heading. The builder prints as many sets as `per` needs (the class size for `child`, half of it rounded up for `pair`, `groupCount` for `group`), shuffles the print order away from the key, stamps the tag on every card, continues a set too tall for one page onto the next page with its caption saying so, and refuses a kit whose key leaves a card unplaced or names a heading the set does not have, a kit with no tag, a card too tall for a page, and a picture card whose picture file cannot be found, because a card printed without the picture children were meant to look at is a different task that still looks complete. Every card in a set is printed the same size, so a set with any picture card makes every card tall enough for a picture and size gives nothing away. The orchestrator's `stick-in-kits` check then confirms the kit matches the unit before the build runs.

Take each figure from the rendered slide that shows it, copied exactly. The lesson's slide spec (`lesson.json`) carries the precise figure the child sees on the board: the same Venn with its labels, the same reflection grid with its shape and mirror line, the same row of triangles. The stick-in piece is that identical task placed in the child's hands, so read the write-on moment's visual from the slide and reproduce its spec field for field, and the printed piece and the board become one activity. This matters most where a source unit's `content` only describes the figure in words (an "irregular 5 to 6 vertex shape", "a Venn sorting by two properties"): the slide is where that description became one concrete figure, so the slide is the binding version. Re-deriving the figure from the source-unit prose instead lets the sheet and the board settle on two different shapes, and a child reflecting the printed shape then completes a different figure from the one the board reveals, which is exactly the break a glued-in piece is meant to prevent. `lesson-design.json` still tells you *which* source units are write-on and carries the pedagogy; the slide fixes the exact figure to print. When a run skipped slides and no `lesson.json` exists, fall back to the source unit's `content` and `representationRefs`, since there is then no board for the piece to match. A `widthMm` may sit on a `slips` item to set a specific printed size, but omit it by default and let the builder use its book-fitting size; on a whole page the page sets the size. If a write-on moment needs a visual not in this list, leave it out and flag it in your final report rather than forcing a poor fit.
