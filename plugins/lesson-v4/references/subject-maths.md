# Subject Discipline: Maths

Read this at the start of the run when the lesson is Maths, before the structure is chosen. The adaptation-designer reads it too, before making the Greater Depth and Below resource decisions.

This file refines the general guidance for Maths. The Lesson Designer uses the class-lesson sections together with the general teaching rules. The Adaptation Designer also reads the Greater Depth section. A mathematical requirement does not disappear because a general example was written from another subject, and a general access or support boundary does not disappear because the lesson is Maths.

Maths lessons here rarely fail on accuracy, and they do not fail through rote success either. Rote fluency is a foundation this school deliberately builds, not a weakness to design around. The failure is what happens after the fluency is built: children who can already do it easily just get more examples. The fluency gets built and never spent. Wasted time. The gate runs both ways: for a genuinely new repeatable method, children normally need reasonable execution before later selection or reasoning, unless that thinking is inseparable from understanding the mathematics itself, and once they are secure, staying is the failure.

## The shape of a maths lesson

Choose the maths lesson shape from the actual mathematical objective. The two-part shape — first secure fluency or execution, then use it in reasoning or problem solving — remains a strong available option and should be used when that sequence best serves the mathematical learning.

For a genuinely new repeatable method, fluency-first remains a strong default: normally secure reasonable execution before asking children to select when or why to use it. Reasoning may begin earlier when it is inseparable from understanding the mathematics itself, and a legitimate fluency lesson may end with sustained accurate execution.

Give reasoning or problem solving its own enabling teaching when it introduces a genuinely new decision, representation, reading demand or way of thinking. Do not create a second whole-class teaching act merely because a task has been labelled "problem solving".

And the trap on the other side of it, kept word for word:

> Jumping to word problems before fluency is secure — the linguistic load crowds out the mathematical load.

**Secure the new idea on numbers small enough to see, then scale to the ones the objective names.** A child meeting rounding for the first time on 3,462 is holding the new idea and four-digit place value at the same time, and the place value is the part that fails. On 43, between 40 and 50, there is one new thing in the room: which end is it nearer. Once that is secure, and once halfway is secure on 45, the same line takes 3,462 and the only thing that has changed is the scale. The same move is worth making wherever a new idea arrives on numbers big enough to hide it: a fraction of 8 before a fraction of 96, a 2-digit exchange before a 4-digit one. Two limits. The small case is the way in, not the lesson, so it takes two or three minutes and the objective's own numbers arrive quickly and carry every bit of the practice and the assessment. And it is not always available: where the difficulty genuinely is the size of the number, or where the small case behaves differently from the general one, teach the real case and scaffold it instead.

**A shortcut is taught in the lesson that gives it meaning, derived from the representation, once the representation has done its work.** Most repeatable methods have a quick version that children will meet and should end up using: the digit to the right for rounding, adding a zero for multiplying by ten, the exchange written as a small one. Both ways of handling it fail. Offered early, it replaces the representation rather than summarising it, because a child who can get every answer by hunting one digit has no reason to keep drawing a line, and the understanding the line carried goes with it. Withheld entirely and deferred to a later lesson, the class leaves drawing a number line for every question, and the next lesson has to reopen the meaning anyway to attach the shortcut to something.

So the shortcut belongs in the same lesson, at the end, and it is asked as a question about the representation rather than announced as a second method: `We looked at the ones digit for the tens, the tens digit for the hundreds, the hundreds digit for the thousands. Why is it always the digit to the right?` Because that digit is what tells you which side of halfway the number sits. That is the shortcut and its meaning in one sentence, and a child who forgets it can rebuild it from the line.

In the source units that beat is a `prepare` unit with `mode: pattern-investigation`, placed after the cycle whose representation it summarises, and its `activity` is the question itself. It is not a My Turn, because nothing is being modelled: the class is being asked to look back at work they have already done and say what was the same every time. Writing it as a My Turn puts it under the cycle rule, so it then owes an Our Turn and a Your Turn of its own, and the lesson either guides and practises a shortcut it only meant to name or grows a cycle it never needed (a Year 4 rounding deck did the second, 12 September 2026).

The representation has done its work when children have used it to overturn a wrong answer and have met the case the shortcut alone gets wrong. Until both have happened, the shortcut is the thing they will remember instead. Two limits. A convention with nothing underneath it to derive (a number landing exactly halfway rounds up) is stated as an agreement when the class hits it, not derived from anything, because there is nothing to derive. And a method whose short form simply is the method has no second version to hold back.

**Live grouping and release belong to the teacher.** The generated lesson provides one coherent mathematical sequence, enough planned guidance and suitable support. The teacher decides live grouping, carpet work, response surfaces, pace and when different children move on. The familiar security-based grouping routine remains available to the teacher; the resource does not prescribe it.

## Fluency, reasoning and problem solving are three different demands

Naming which one a task makes decides what a good version of it looks like.

**Fluency** is executing the taught method reliably: short questions, same skill, difficulty building across the set. Executing is the doing; a fluency beat's `thinking` line names the decision inside the method at that step (`which column decides the next number?`, `do I exchange here, and why?`), never `complete the calculations`, because the decision is what the child has to hold and the calculation is only where it shows.

**Reasoning** is judging the maths itself: is this right, is it always, sometimes or never true, convince me, spot the mistake, this person says this, do you agree, why or why not.

**Problem solving** is one-step and two-step word problems, open-ended questions, or problems where the child has to use the fluency at some point to solve them.

**Spending the fluency, once it is secure**, looks like: the same work in a different context, modelled examples gone wrong for the child to catch, marking a set of answers to judge which are right, then on into reasoning and problem solving. It does not look like more of the same examples.

**Where one method runs across several cases, practise each case in a short block before mixing them.** Three place values to round to, three denominators, two operations in one method: a set that mixes them from the first question asks a child to work out which case they are in and then run a method they learned twenty minutes ago, and when the method is that new the choosing crowds out the running. A few on the nearest 10, a few on the nearest 100, a few on the nearest 1,000, then the mixed ones. The mixing is not dropped, because choosing the case is where the learning actually shows and a child who only ever meets the cases in labelled blocks has not been asked the real question: it comes second, in the same session, and the same-number-three-ways item belongs there. The limit is an objective whose difficulty is the choosing (`choose the most efficient method`), where blocking would remove the lesson.

**Each of the three is a beat, and this design decides where it goes.** Fluency lives in the cycles' Your Turns, one per modelled move. Reasoning and problem solving are `practise` units, placed where the lesson has enough behind them to carry them: usually after the cycles, and between them where one concept is the whole of what the problem needs. Neither is a wing bolted on at the end out of habit, and neither is skipped by default because the cycles filled the time. A lesson whose objective supports worthwhile reasoning designs a real shape for it; a lesson whose objective genuinely does not says so (`preferences.md` → Reasoning Is Every Child's Entitlement).

## The worksheet's sections in maths

The section labels are maths's own words, the ones already on the board at school, and each names one of the demands defined above. Whenever fluency shares a sheet with deeper work, fluency comes first: children need reliable execution before they spend it. Three to six substantial standalone questions is often enough; it is not a cap on the number of brief calculations, reads or classifications a grouped activity may contain. Count how many times the child performs the taught mathematical action, not how many question numbers print on the sheet.

Each generated sheet picks the sections its evidence genuinely needs rather than filling every slot or meeting a quota:

- **Below:** Fluency, Reasoning or Problem Solving.
- **Expected:** Fluency, Reasoning or Problem Solving.
- **Greater Depth:** Fluency, Reasoning, Problem Solving or Going Deeper.

Problem Solving is available to Below when it is pedagogically sound and independently accessible; it is not a compulsory Below section.

**Going Deeper belongs to the Greater Depth resource alone.** It labels authentic same-objective depth selected by the adaptation designer with this file and `reasoning-prompts.md`. It may use one rich task or several concise prompts and does not require a fixed item count. A hard applying question on Expected remains Problem Solving.

This is stated here because it is a maths rule, not a general one: other subjects shape their sheets around whatever the lesson's thinking needs, and no other subject is required to have a fluency block.

## Making practice generative

On repeatable number work, prefer the form where the child produces the maths over the form where they complete it. The four moves, kept word for word from where they were refined:

Before choosing one of those moves, design the examples as a set rather than a
bag of questions. Decide which mathematical feature stays invariant, which
feature changes next, and where a boundary or misconception should surface.
When one starting value supports several related transformations, calculations
or representations, keep the related results together if comparing them is part
of the learning. A central value with several operations around it, or several
representations of one value in a row, can create many useful attempts while
making the relationship visible. This does not make a table the default:
separate questions are better when each item needs its own method, working or
choice and adjacent answers would cue the move. The test is whether seeing the
results together teaches the intended structure or merely saves paper.

> - **Generate values when that serves the mathematics.** Child-generated numbers are one available activity when they preserve the target skill and add useful practice or engagement. Use deliberately selected values when exact numbers are needed to expose a boundary, misconception, invariant or controlled variation. Do not make generation the default.

> - **Keep, reduce or remove support for a mathematical reason.** Fading is an important available pattern, not a compulsory sequence. A frame or representation may remain constant, change or disappear according to whether it enables the target thinking or supplies it. Greater Depth may retain purposeful support when it enables deeper reasoning. Below has a light preference towards useful structural support without requiring it on every question.
> - **Let the pupil build the representation when constructing it is the skill.** When deciding where the jump or partition goes is itself the learning, leave that construction to the pupil unless the adaptation specifically decides that a pre-drawn structure is needed for access. Keep a pre-drawn representation when reading or interpreting it is the target. Using the Below resource does not by itself decide the configuration.
> - **Scaffold a taught mental strategy as a fill-in method frame.** A strategy the lesson teaches can be practised as a printed equation or stem the child completes, for example `= ___ + ___ =` for redistribution, or `First add ___ + ___. Then adjust ___ - ___.` for adjusting, with the blanks growing across the set as above. The `method-frame` helper renders this on the sheet and on the board, and it takes the blanks per line, so the same frame can be given fully worked, part-given or all blank across the set.

## Greater Depth in maths

Read by the adaptation-designer.

Greater Depth stays on the class objective and within the year group's curriculum. First decide whether Expected already provides sufficient mathematical depth. When a separate Greater Depth resource is needed, increase authentic mathematical demand through one or more of these moves:

- stronger relational thinking or generalisation;
- choosing, translating or comparing representations;
- selecting and justifying an efficient method;
- systematic search with a complete argument;
- a richer constraint, boundary case or carefully chosen unfamiliar application.

Fresh same-objective practice may be included when it improves case coverage, but harder numbers, extra quantity or a changed surface representation do not by themselves create Greater Depth. Use harder in-year numbers, missing values, exchanges, less familiar cases or controlled variation only when the class-taught method still applies.

Preserve the class practice architecture when it carries an important invariant or progression. Change it when another arrangement is needed to expose the deeper mathematical relationship. Choose the amount from the mathematical action, case coverage, likely pace, response cost and available page space. Do not impose a maximum standalone-question count or a fixed Fluency, Reasoning, Problem Solving or Going Deeper quota.

Keep any representation, vocabulary or scaffold that enables deeper mathematical thinking without supplying it. Remove support only when consulting it would perform the thinking being assessed. Never accelerate into later curriculum content.

## Vocabulary in maths

The general rule holds: the words that earn a card are the ones children reason with. In maths those are the words a child uses to explain their method and their thinking: exchange, partition, difference, regroup, factor. Topic labels a child never reasons with do not earn a card.

Why it matters here specifically: the strongest children can often just do it and cannot explain why. The vocabulary is what lets them explain, so the card is doing its job when a child who got every question right uses its word to say what they did and why it works.

## Real-world hooks

Where a genuine, accurate link exists, a lesson or a new concept may open with a short hook: a real fact and an image that make a child want the answer, with the same context carried through the examples and practice. The hook is motivation and context, never a preview of the method, and the prerequisite starter still does its own job beside it. It is a judgement rather than a fixture: reach for it when the link is real and the maths lives inside the fact, and leave it out when the fact is scenery bolted onto the sum. A contrived hook teaches children that the real world is decoration, so no hook is better than one that has to be forced.

A real fact has to stand up. A curriculum-stable fact is safe; a volatile one (a current population, a "latest" figure, a quantity the lesson leans its maths on) is stated as a robustly rounded claim that stays true, or is marked for the teacher to verify. The general rule for facts in any subject is in the lesson designer's Starter section; this section is the maths-specific case of opening on one.

## The positions this file takes

Every subject has arguments inside it, and this file takes the teacher's side on three so a lesson never wobbles between philosophies.

**A maths skill lesson opens with the model.** The Skill-based route allows a bounded first attempt at the target before any modelling; maths does not use it. The attempt earns its place only where failure is visible to the child, and a wrong written method fails silently — the child's answer looks as plausible as a right one, so the attempt rehearses the error instead of creating the question the teaching answers. My Turn → Our Turn → Your Turn stays the maths rhythm, with the Our Turn omitted only under the route's own omission test.

**The room may split by security, but the resource does not prescribe that grouping.** Some schools keep every child on the same task and differentiate only by depth. This room may use a carpet group, independent practice, and greater depth extending; the live teacher decides whether to use that routine and when different children move on.

**Fluency may be built in blocks before later reasoning when that shape fits.** There is research that pushes mixing question types early, and it was read and weighed. For a genuinely new repeatable method, fluency-first remains a strong option: build the fluency deliberately, often in blocks, and normally secure reasonable execution before later selection or reasoning; reasoning may begin earlier when it is inseparable from understanding the mathematics itself. The failure to design against is not the blocking; it is building the fluency and never spending it.
