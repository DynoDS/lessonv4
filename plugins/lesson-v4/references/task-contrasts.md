# Task contrasts: what a task actually asks a child to know

Read this when choosing the main task or a Do beat the lesson will rely on as evidence, and when reviewing one. It exists because a task can be clear, varied, hands-on and well worded and still be completable without the learning the lesson claims to have taught. Each contrast below puts a plausible weak task beside a stronger one for a stated purpose, names what each actually requires a child to know or do, and then names the case where the simpler task is exactly right. The contrast is never "sorting bad, explaining good". A sort can be the stronger task and a sentence the weaker one.

None of these is a recipe. A lesson is never asked to match an example's names, order or materials; it is asked to be able to answer the two questions every contrast turns on: **could a child who missed the teaching do this well from the board and everyday sense?** and **could a child who only remembers the last slide do it well without using the idea on anything?** The seven contrasts are about the first question. The section after them, `One story, one process, one set of meanings`, is about the second.

## The contrasts

### History: the reason behind a choice

**Purpose.** Children explain why Tudor families sent children to work, including into hard, unpaid apprenticeships.

**Weak.** Sort six cards into `Bad things about being an apprentice` and `Good things about being an apprentice`: `up before sunrise`, `hot meals every day`, `only Sundays off`, `a strict master`, `one less mouth to feed at home`, `his own shop one day`. Requires: reading the card and knowing that early mornings are unpleasant. A child who slept through the lesson sorts all six correctly. The follow-up (`Did the bad or the good things matter more to a Tudor family? Why?`) is where the thinking was meant to be, and it arrives as a discussion after a sort that already felt like the work.

**Stronger, same purpose.** `A shoemaker promised to teach Will how to make shoes. Will got meals and a bed, but all he ever did was sweep the floor. Will's family were still unhappy. What was Will going to miss out on when he grew up?` Requires: the taught link between being trained now and earning a living later, which the everyday reading (`he was fed, so it was fine`) gets wrong. Naming the missing training is not the thinking, because the case says it outright; the consequence is. The answer can be short, one completed `because`, and a changed condition checks the same understanding from the other side: a family who could already feed him might still have wanted the training, which does not make food and a bed worthless.

**Where the simpler task is right.** Straight after the lesson teaches that a Tudor child's work gave the family something it needed, a quick match of jobs the slide did not show (`carried water`, `minded the pigs`) to what each gave the family is the right check: it needs that idea and nothing more. The good/bad sort of the deal just taught is not the lesson's evidence, and a lesson that leans on it has taught less than it looks.

### Science: a prediction that the relationship decides

**Purpose.** Children predict from the taught relationship (a longer wire, more resistance, a dimmer bulb).

**Weak.** Two circuits drawn side by side, one with a visibly thicker, brighter bulb symbol. `Which bulb will be brighter?` Requires: looking at the drawing. The picture answers the question the relationship was meant to answer.

**Stronger, same purpose.** Two circuits drawn identically except for wire length, bulbs unlit. `Which bulb will be brighter, and what in the circuit tells you?` Requires: the taught relationship, because nothing else on the board differs. A changed condition is the same strength when it stays inside the relationship that was taught: `We make the long wire even longer. What happens to the bulb now?` Adding a second cell brings in a second relationship (more cells, a brighter bulb), so it is a fair question only once that has been taught as well; it is not the same thinking with a new variable.

**Where the simpler task is right.** A clear diagram and a short one-line prediction are enough; do not add a paragraph, a table or an extra variable to make it look rigorous. And a diagram that supplies the information the prediction needs (the wire lengths) is not answer leakage; it is the question.

### Geography: reading a map versus using one

**Purpose.** Children apply a taught relationship (settlements grew where rivers gave water, transport and flat land).

**Weak.** A map with four labelled towns and four labelled rivers. `Name a town that is next to a river.` Requires: reading two labels. It claims to test the relationship and tests the key.

**Stronger, same purpose.** A new, unlabelled map with a river, a hill area and three marked sites. `Which site would you have chosen to build a village, and what on the map made you choose it?` Requires: the taught relationship applied to a map the lesson never showed, and the reason stated in the map's own features. Supplying the new map is the task, not a giveaway.

**Where the simpler task is right.** Reading labels off a map is right when reading a map is what is being taught, and a quick label-reading check after a new map skill, on a map the lesson has not shown, is an honest check.

### Maths: practice that repeats, and support that decides

**Purpose.** Children round three-digit numbers to the nearest hundred.

**Weak.** The check the lesson relies on as evidence of each child's decision draws every number line with the target hundred already highlighted. Requires: following the highlight. The support supplies the very decision being assessed.

**Stronger, same purpose.** The same method on fresh numbers in no pattern, with the number line drawn and the halfway mark unmarked, then one where the ones digit is 5. Requires: finding the halfway point and deciding which side the number sits, every time.

**Where the simpler task is right.** An ordered run such as 349, 351, 449, 451 is good teaching: set side by side, it shows the class where the boundary sits and what one hundred more does. It is weaker only as evidence, when the answers can be read from position rather than decided, so the questions the lesson treats as each child's decision come in no pattern. New numbers using the same taught method is exactly what practice is; repetition of a known move is the point, and a reference (the steps, a worked example beside the questions) stays where it enables the method without making the decision. Do not replace fluency with novelty to look harder.

### English: a count is not the quality

**Purpose.** Children write a setting description that makes a reader feel the place.

**Weak.** `Write three sentences about the forest. Include at least two adjectives in each.` Requires: counting adjectives. `The big green forest had tall dark trees` meets it. The sentence may still give the place some feeling, but the task never asked the writer to choose words for that, so what a child writes cannot show whether they did.

**Stronger, same purpose.** One model sentence beside a flat one, the difference named (`the flat one tells you the forest is scary; the strong one shows you the branches catching your sleeve`). Then: `Write two sentences that make the reader feel the forest without saying scary, cold or dark.` Requires: choosing detail that does the work, which is the taught quality.

**Where the simpler task is right.** A short grammar drill (`add a fronted adverbial to each of these sentences`) is legitimate practice of a move, and a one-sentence example can be the whole task; not every English task becomes discussion or a paragraph.

### RE and PSHE: an opinion is not evidence of understanding, and reflection can be the point

**Purpose (RE).** Children understand why a carol matters to a Christian, and that the same act can carry different meanings for different people.

**Weak.** `Do you like singing carols? Why?` after a slide showing a family singing. Requires: an opinion. A child who learned nothing about belief answers it well. A child who says `yes, because of Jesus` may understand more than that and may be repeating the board; the answer is too short to tell, and the question gives no way to find out.

**Stronger, same purpose.** `Layla sings this carol for two reasons; Ethan sings it for one. What is the difference, and could you tell it from watching them?` Requires: the two taught meanings and the taught idea that belief cannot be read from the act.

**Where the simpler task is right.** When the objective is personal reflection (`what does this festival mean to me`), the child's own meaning placed beside the taught ones is the intended outcome, and asking for it is right. A supplied view is material to interpret, never a view to agree with. In PSHE the same holds: a scenario with a practical choice (`Sam's friend wants to share a photo of you; what could Sam say?`) is stronger than `be kind` because it asks for the taught move, and privacy and the right to pass stay protected whatever the task.

### A short sequence: work that builds, and work that only looks connected

**Purpose.** A run of three or four beats in which what children produce or come to understand is what the next beat works on.

**Weak, varied-looking.** Science, electricity: match component names to pictures, a true or false quiz about conductors, draw any circuit that works, then explain what you learned. Four formats, a fresh picture on each slide, and every beat could run in any order, because none uses what the last one produced. The variety is in the channel only.

**Weak, inflated.** A history task made to look more historical by adding rules the lesson never taught (a law about apprenticeships, a guild's entry rule) and asking children to judge a case against them. The task looks harder and now tests untaught content, which in the case this came from was also unverified.

**Stronger, same topic.** Science: teach that a lamp lights only when there is a complete path through conducting parts. Children mark on one fresh broken circuit where the path fails, and the teacher confirms the fault before anyone builds on it. They draw the repair, then test it or read a supplied result, and the last question asks why the repaired circuit works, using the break they marked. Each beat's outcome is the material the next beat works on, and nothing new was added to make it look harder.

**Where the simpler task is right.** Not every beat depends on the one before. Two sources that separately feed a later comparison, vocabulary or setup beside the spine, and practice that repeats a method on fresh numbers are right as they are; a sequence does not need a named character, a chain between every pair of slides, or each later beat to be harder because it is later.

## One story, one process, one set of meanings

**Purpose.** Children use what they have just been taught, in a lesson that has no obvious second case: one person's story, one process from start to end, one object and what each part of it means.

This is where a Do most easily turns into the Teach said back, and not through carelessness. A rule can be tried on ten cases; a story happened once. So the task that comes to mind is to order the story, complete the sentence or pick the better explanation, and the right answer to each is the slide before. The way out is in how this teacher teaches. A Teach gives the takeaway, the because, the example and what it is not (`teaching-sequence-content-based.md` → `How this teacher explains`). Saying the takeaway back is rehearsal. A use moves one of the other three, and the pairs below are this plugin's own designs: each say-it-back task beside a use task written for the same teaching.

**Start from a real case, not from a change.** Every task of this kind that has worked began with something a class could meet, or something a child might say: sweetcorn skin in poo, an astronaut swallowing, a stick that has fallen out of a Christingle, a boy who chose a blue ribbon, a girl who thinks a grape is small enough already. So look there first: in a kitchen, a playground, a church, a street, a museum case; or put the wrong idea a real child holds into a named child's mouth. Then the taught idea is what explains the case, and the child is thinking about digestion, not decoding a puzzle. The shapes a real case takes:

**A real case the lesson did not show** (a new example).
- Says it back: `Nutrients are inside the small intestine. Blood is outside its wall. Rewrite these sentences to explain how nutrients reach the body.` The model answer was the Teach board, 16 words of 17.
- Uses it: `Sometimes people see bits of sweetcorn skin in their poo. Why does that happen?` Requires: that only what is broken down small enough passes through the wall.
- Also: an airport prayer room with no mihrab and an arrow on the wall (`What is the arrow for?`); an astronaut swallowing where nothing falls down.

**A named child who says what a real child might think** (it works what it is not).
- Says it back: two accounts of the stomach, where the better one ends with the Teach's own clause.
- Uses it: `Leo says a grape is small enough to swallow whole, so its nutrients can go straight into his blood. Is Leo right?` Requires: that small enough to swallow is not small enough to cross the wall.
- Also: `Sam used a blue ribbon because blue is his favourite colour. His gran says something is missing now. What does she mean?`
- When the teacher asks for children to judge which answer is better, the judging is the task and the answers have to be new. Says it back: `Which explanation helps us understand the stomach's job?` where the better one is the Teach's own two sentences and the other only says where food goes; a child picks the one that sounds like the slide. Uses it: two named children explain a part the Teach named and did not model (the mouth, the large intestine), both true, one saying only where food goes and one saying what happens and why; or `Rewrite Theo's four facts so they explain`. The child applies `an explanation says what happens and why` to sentences they have not seen.

**A new person or object the idea has to sort** (a new example, decided by the rule).
- Says it back: five boxes from the story of the 1842 law to put back in the order just told.
- Uses it: `Patience, a girl aged 17. John, a boy aged 7. William, a boy aged 14. Did the new law stop each of them working underground?` Requires: what the law actually covered, which the third child tests.
- Also: a child's shoe found in the soldiers' barracks (`What does it tell us about who lived at the fort?`).

**Something that really happens, and changes one thing** (it works the because). A stick falls out; someone makes theirs with sweets; a hospital has kind nurses and nobody cleans it. The change is one a child could see happen. Children of this age tend to answer a what-if from a general rule they already hold (small laboratory studies, Rafetseder and colleagues; a caution, not a classroom finding), so an invented one mostly tests the general rule.
- Says it back: `The ribbon is red to remind Christians of ___.`
- Uses it: `One stick falls out of this Christingle. You still have plenty of food. Add more food to the three sticks, or put the fourth stick back?` Requires: that the four sticks are the four seasons, so more food cannot stand in for one.

**A prediction before the telling** (the because, before it is given).
- Says it back: `How does a sound get to your ear? The air passes the ___ along.`
- Uses it: `Rice on cling film stretched over a bowl. I bang a tray beside it and nothing touches the bowl. Will the rice jump?` Then the banged tray answers it, and the next Teach starts from what they saw.

**The test for every one of these: name the wrong answer first.** It is for every question whose job is to find out who has understood: a quick check, a Do, and a checking question inside a Teach. It is not for a starter that recalls earlier learning on purpose, a question that only directs attention, a vocabulary card, or the final task, which follows a good answer to that very question. Before you word the question, write what a child who understood would answer, what a child who did not would answer, and what that second child is thinking. The two answers have to differ, and the second has to be one a real child in this class would give. This is how questions that show understanding are built (Wiliam on hinge questions: the right response for the right reason; Barton: it cannot be answered correctly while holding the misconception), and both faults fail it the same way. In a say-it-back the child who did not understand copies the right answer; in an obvious what-if that child reaches it by common sense; either way the two children say the same thing and the teacher learns nothing. When no real child would give the wrong answer, look at what was taught: it may be something nothing can reason out (below).

**Then put the wrong answer where it can pull.** Naming it in your notes is half of the test. A wrong idea a child never meets in the question pulls nobody: everyone gets it right and the teacher learns nothing. So it has to be in front of the class: said by a named child, offered as an option a child would really choose, or the natural reading of the situation.
- Left in the notes: `Is the grape digested in the mouth? Decide yes or no.` The design's note says a child might think small enough to swallow is small enough for the blood. Nothing on the page says so, and `digested in the mouth` sounds wrong to everyone.
- In a child's mouth: `Leo says a grape is small enough to swallow whole, so its nutrients can go straight into his blood. Is Leo right?` The same wrong idea, said aloud, sounding sensible.

Last, read the finished question from the surface, as a child who was not listening: could they get it right from the words on the page alone? A card that carries a give-away word, the only option that makes sense, a closing hint that says where to look, a question that states part of its own answer (`What has been building up?`), a claim that is plainly false (`Is this accurate?`). If so, the question has not been asked yet (`teacher-voice.md` §6, `Do not leak the answer`). Each subject file lists the wrong answers real children give in that subject.

**The same move, with nothing to decide.** A design that had these moves changed one thing in each task and wrote two of them like this:
- Nothing tempts the wrong answer: `We use a bigger orange for a Christingle. Does that mean the world has grown? Decide yes or no.` and `This candle burns right down and goes out. Does that mean Christians believe Jesus has run out of hope to give?` Requires: nothing. No child says yes, so the child who understood and the child who did not give the same answer.
- Something does: `One stick falls out. You still have plenty of food. Add more food to the three sticks, or put the fourth stick back?` More food looks generous and sensible, and a child who takes the sticks for holders chooses it. Both options are tempting, and only the meaning of the four sticks decides between them.

**An invented what-if, where a real case was wanted.** A second design read `change one thing` as `imagine something strange` and wrote, for digestion:
- A puzzle to decode: `Imagine churning stopped, but digestive juices still reached the food. Which answer explains what would change? Underline the words that explain what the juices can still do.` and `Imagine the wall here is blocked, but the tube is still open. Choose route A or B for the tiny nutrient and the food that hasn't been digested. What would the blood miss out on here?` Each names a wrong answer, so each passes the test on paper. Neither is a question a teacher asks a class of eight year olds: a stomach that stops churning and a wall that blocks while the tube stays open cannot happen, each holds three things to do, and the reading is harder than the science.
- A real case: `Leo says a grape is small enough to swallow whole, so its nutrients can go straight into his blood. Is Leo right?` and `Sometimes people see bits of sweetcorn skin in their poo. Why does that happen?` One thing to decide, about something a child has seen or could say.

A task is one ask, with its reason where the reason is the evidence, and the reading is the easy part (`teacher-voice.md` §6 says how, for every question). A choice, then an underlining job, then a second question is three tasks.

A bare yes or no is the form this goes wrong in most, because one side is usually obvious. It earns its place when the class would really split and the reason is what the teacher listens for (`Another hospital had very kind nurses, and nobody cleaned it. Would the soldiers stop getting ill?`, where the Lady with the Lamp pulls children to yes). Otherwise offer two things that both look right, and ask for the reason, because a two-way choice alone is half guessable. The stem `Does that mean...?` signals no before the child has thought. And a `what if` or an odd one out with no wrong answer is a talk starter, good for opening a topic and hearing ideas; it does not sort a class after teaching.

In RE the question asks what the object or the practice means to the people who use it, and names them (`many Christians`, `the church`). It does not ask children to rule on whether a belief is true or could run out, does not stretch a symbol further than believers take it, and never invents a belief nobody holds so that children can knock it down (`subject-re.md`: what believers believe, never whether it is true; Ofsted's RE review asks for questions about people and their uses of a tradition, and warns that inaccurate pictures of a belief leave misconceptions). The candle question fails on all three. A question may ask what believers promise, do or mean; it does not ask the class to rule that a named believer has failed at it, because some child in the room has a relative who does exactly that.

**When nothing can be reasoned out.** Some of what a lesson teaches has no reasoning behind it for a child to get wrong: what a pure symbol stands for (the orange, the red ribbon), a name, a date, where a place is, the order of a story. There is a gap or a muddle, never a wrong idea, so a what-if about it has nothing to decide (the bigger orange). The check for this content is recall, and recall comes in forms that ask more or less of a child. The order below is this plugin's own working guide; the Ofsted subject reviews back retrieval and none of them ranks these forms.

1. Fill the gap in the sentence on the board. Copying.
2. Fill the gap with the board covered.
3. The whole thing from a bare cue, board covered: `What does the orange remind Christians of?`
4. Both directions across everything taught so far: `Which part reminds Christians of the four seasons?`
5. A new arrangement that needs every meaning: `Here are three Christingles. Each has a part missing: no candle, three sticks, no ribbon. What is each one no longer reminding Christians of?` A blank map. Shuffled events with one that never happened.
6. Beside what it sits next to: `What is north of Brazil?` `Did the Romans come before or after the Vikings?`
7. Later: the same question in next lesson's starter.

Forms 1 to 3 are the say-it-back, and a lesson holds one (form 1 is not worth that one). Form 4 is the say-it-back when it asks only about the thing just taught. Forms 4 to 6, ranging over more than the last slide, are uses: the child has to keep several things apart, the slide did not show the arrangement, and a child who half knows gives a different answer from one who knows (two meanings swapped, a place put on the wrong side). Form 7 belongs to a later lesson. Where that line falls is this plugin's reasoning, not a finding.

**This is not all of RE, or of any subject.** Three things look alike and are checked differently (the split between a symbol and an object with a job is this plugin's own, drawn from its tasks):
- What a pure symbol stands for: recall, in the forms above.
- An object with a job (a mihrab shows the direction of Makkah, a prayer mat keeps the place of prayer clean): use it somewhere new, as the airport prayer room does. The real wrong answer is the name without the job: `it is a decoration`.
- The belief behind a story or a practice: children do hold wrong ideas here, the everyday reason in place of the belief (`because a baby was born`, `so it looks nice`; Ofsted's 2024 RE report found pupils calling Christmas `God's birthday`). Ask why the people who do it do it, on a part of the story or the practice the Teach has not explained: `At a Christingle service the lights are switched off before the candles are lit. Why do you think the church does that?` `Sam used a blue ribbon because blue is his favourite colour. His gran says something is missing now. What does she mean?`

**Where the simpler task is right.** A name, a term, a date or a definition the next beat stands on is worth recalling with the answer off the board, and so is a sentence rehearsed aloud before it is written. A lesson holds one such Do at most (`output-template.md` → `use`, the teacher's ruling), so it is something to spend: put it where a secure fact matters most to what comes next, and make every other Do a use.

**Two ways a use goes wrong.** It has to be answerable from what was taught: a task that needs a law, a process or a fact the lesson never gave is unfair, not harder, and a trick is not a near miss. And a changed name is not a new case: the guitar for the bell, with the same chain to recite, is the same task. The test of a use is that the taught idea decides the answer and the last slide's words do not.
