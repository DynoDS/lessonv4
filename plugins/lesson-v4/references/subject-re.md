# Subject Discipline — Religious Education (RE)

Read this at the start of an RE lesson, before the route is chosen. This file governs the RE-specific teaching boundaries below.

## Teach knowledge before dependent judgement

Teach enough accurate knowledge about the religion or worldview before asking children to make a reflection, comparison or judgement that depends on it. Uninformed opinion is not the intended outcome. When the learning is an idea rather than a fact about one tradition (the same practice carries different meanings; belief and practice are not the same thing), name it in `concepts` and meet it on more than one practice or person.

Use **Content-based** teaching to build and connect knowledge. Use **Dialogic** only when the question is genuinely open after children have the knowledge needed to reason.

## Keep religious stories inside their meaning

Teach a religious story with its tradition, source, context, authority and meaning where those matter to the objective. Do not flatten it into a generic moral such as “be kind” when the religious meaning is part of the learning.

## Identify whose voice the evidence represents

Be clear whether a statement represents an individual, a group, a tradition or a widely authoritative teaching. Do not turn one person’s practice, quotation or experience into a claim about every member of a religion. Qualify the claim only as far as the evidence supports.

## Check the tradition's own rules before asking for a picture of it

RE is full of things a camera can be pointed at - a church, a mosque, a festival meal, a sacred text, a person praying - and the referent test in `preferences.md` → Lesson Designer visual-need boundary applies here as in any subject. What is specific to RE is that some traditions place limits on what may be depicted, and those limits are the tradition's, not a matter of taste. Christian art depicts Jesus freely, so a nativity, a crucifix or a Bible illustration is ordinary teaching material. Islam does not depict Muhammad, and no lesson may request a picture of him or of any prophet; teach through the mosque, the Qur'an, calligraphy, the practice or the community instead. Where you are unsure whether a tradition depicts a figure, request the place, object, text or practice rather than the figure, which teaches the same thing and cannot offend the people being taught about.

## Say what the tradition claims, as precisely as a child can hold it

A festival's date marks when a tradition celebrates an event, not a known date of the event: Christians celebrate Jesus' birth on Christmas Day, and the Bible gives no date, so `the day Christians believe Jesus was born` teaches a claim the tradition does not make. Write `the day they celebrate his birth`, and a crib figure added that morning `shows the birth in the story`, not a birth that has not yet happened. A belief names what a tradition holds about who or what something is, not merely that an event occurred: the Christian belief in this lesson is that Jesus is God's Son, and `believes Jesus was born` is a different and weaker claim that a non-Christian could share. Define belief for children as something a person accepts as true; building certainty or proof into the definition (`sure it is true even when nobody can prove it`) teaches a caricature and makes the word harder to use.

## Do not ask children to pretend to worship

Children may observe, discuss and understand religious practices. A teacher may demonstrate a posture, object or sequence when that helps children understand what happens. Do not ask children to perform worship as though the belief or practice were their own.

## Use evidence that directly shows the RE learning

Choose the evidence form that most directly shows the intended understanding: written, spoken, visual or practical. Flexibility must not weaken the evidence. There must still be enough individual evidence to show that children understood the intended knowledge, interpretation, comparison or reasoned view rather than relying only on confident speakers.

An objective phrased as a personal reflection (`what Christmas means to me`, `what I think about...`) names the product. The RE learning behind it is still knowledge and understanding: what the practice means within the tradition, and that the same practice carries different meanings for different people. Design the reflection so it draws on that learning: a child places their own meaning beside the taught ones, says what theirs shares with them and what it does not, and can say whether more than one of those meanings could be true of one person; or separates what they do from what it means to them using the distinction the lesson taught. Like-or-unlike as a two-way choice invites the sort the lesson exists to undo. A reflection a child could have written before the lesson shows the product and none of the RE. A short check of the taught meaning is the fallback only when the reflection honestly cannot carry it (`preferences.md` → What a Lesson Is For).

The choices that protect a child are in the words the class hears and reads, not in a flag to the teacher: that the example can come from school, friends or home; that the time can mean little or bring mixed feelings and still be explained well; and that a private spoken answer can replace writing. A script that says `think about December in your house` three times and offers the alternatives only in a teacher note has withdrawn them from the child who needed them, whatever the flag says.
