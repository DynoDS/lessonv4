# Explanation tasks: turning knowledge into reasoning

Read this when the lesson's main task asks children to **explain, justify, account for, compare with a reason, or say why or how** something happens - in any subject. It does not apply to a task whose product is a set of answers, a labelled diagram, a made thing or a performance, unless the child also has to say why.

It exists because a task can be well taught, well launched and well resourced and still come back as four true sentences in a row. That is not a wording problem and it is not fixed by asking for more detail.

Daniel's own research summary (18 September 2026), drawing on the EEF's oral-language, primary-science and literacy guidance, the Year 5 dialogic-teaching trial and the IES writing practice guide, is the source for what follows.

## A fact tells us something; an explanation connects facts to show why or how

This is the distinction the whole file turns on, and it is worth the class hearing in those words.

> **Weak:** "Jack eats toffees. There are germs in his mouth. Germs make acid. His tooth hurts."

Four true statements. The child appears to know four relevant things. What is missing is the reasoning between them: `fact → fact → fact → fact`.

> **Strong:** "Jack sucks toffees all the way home, so sugar is left on his teeth. The germs in the plaque feed on that sugar and make acid. The acid eats the enamel away, so a hole starts. The hole reaches the pulp, where the nerves are, so it hurts."

`sugar → bacteria → acid → enamel → cavity → pain`. The difference is not length.

| A weak answer | A strong one |
|---|---|
| Mentions relevant facts | Connects them |
| Uses some topic words | Uses them accurately and where they are needed |
| Tells us what | Explains how or why |
| Leaves the reader to infer the link | States the link |
| Several short unrelated sentences | Sentences that work as one chain |
| Stops once a fact is stated | Keeps asking "what happens because of that?" |

**Each subject reasons differently, and this file does not turn them all into science.** Science wants mechanism, cause and effect, prediction, evidence. History wants causes, consequences, evidence, significance, sometimes competing explanations. Geography wants process, interaction, spatial relationship. Maths wants justification: why a procedure works, why an answer must be true. English wants interpretation supported by the text. Design and art want choices justified against criteria. The shape they share is not a paragraph template: **make a claim, supply the knowledge or evidence, make the relationship explicit, develop the reasoning.**

## Three kinds of language, and the lesson usually supplies only one

- **Subject vocabulary** supplies the knowledge: `plaque`, `enamel`, `acid`; `monarchy`, `rebellion`; `numerator`. This is the lesson's `vocabulary`, and it is already handled.
- **Reasoning vocabulary** connects it: `because`, `so`, `therefore`, `causes`, `leads to`, `as a result`, `this means`. This is `content.reasoningWords`, and without it a child can hold every taught noun and still list them.
- **Discussion vocabulary** lets children test each other's reasoning: `I agree because…`, `I would add…`, `what is your evidence?`, `can you explain why?` This lives in `content.rehearsal.partnerAsks` and in the teacher's script.

The verbs carry more than the connectives do. A child who writes `the acid causes the enamel to lose minerals` has more explanatory control than one who knows the nouns `acid` and `enamel`: **causes, produces, increases, decreases, prevents, leads to, changes, results in, supports, suggests, demonstrates.**

## Choosing `reasoningWords`

Pick the few this task's explanation actually needs, at this year group. Not a bank.

| Year | What the explanation is doing | Useful language |
|---|---|---|
| Reception | Connecting one observation to one reason | because, so, first, then |
| 1 | One explicit link, using a taught word | because, so, when |
| 2 | A short chain rather than a list | because, so, this means, as a result |
| 3 | A process in order, several links made explicit | causes, because, therefore, when… then…, leads to |
| 4 | A mechanism: several stages connected coherently | as a result, causes, which means, therefore |
| 5 | Justifying: why this evidence supports this conclusion | this shows, this suggests, because, therefore, one reason… another reason… |
| 6 | Several causes weighed, qualified where the discipline needs it | consequently, however, although, this was significant because, the strongest evidence is… |

**The progression is not a march towards fancier conjunctions.** `Consequently` is not better than `so`. A Year 6 child who puts `consequently` between two ideas that are not causally connected is reasoning worse than a Year 2 child using `so` correctly. The real progression is: one link → several links → a complete chain → a precise chain → a justified chain → selective or qualified reasoning.

**The important word is never `because`. It is what has to follow it.** "Teeth decay because sugar" contains the connective and almost no explanation. "Teeth can decay because bacteria use sugars and produce acids that damage the enamel" is explanatory because there is a real causal proposition on both sides of the word.

**Fade them.** The stages are: the teacher models the move → a complete frame → a choice of starters → a prompt only (`make the causal link clearer`) → nothing, with the vocabulary still available. Choosing fewer words as a class gets more secure is how that happens here. The same laminated set from Year 3 to Year 6 teaches children to fill a gap while leaving them unable to decide what relationship their ideas actually have, and the evidence for sentence stems as a standalone intervention is much weaker than for dialogue, vocabulary teaching, modelling and revision. A stem is a handrail, not the staircase.

## `rehearsal`: say it, be asked one question, say it better, then write

Children articulating an explanation aloud before writing it is one of the strongest recommendations in the literacy evidence, and the reason is mechanical: composition and transcription compete for the same attention, so talking lets a child build the idea before spelling and handwriting take their share.

The second half is what does the work. A first explanation is not a performance to be judged and forgotten; it is something that gets more accurate and more connected through one question. So:

- `sayIt` is what each child says to their partner, in the child's own words: `Tell your partner how Sam's tooth decayed. Start with the sugar.`
- `partnerAsks` is the one question back: `Why does that matter?` or `What happens between the acid and the pain?`

Then they write what they have just rehearsed. The writing is the second attempt, not the first.

**Use `null` when it would not help**: a set of short answers, a task whose product is not extended prose, or a task where a child's unaided first formulation is the evidence the lesson wants. Partner talk is not compulsory before all writing (`do-beats.md` §2.1); what is true is that an extended explanation is the case where it earns its place.

## The missing link: what to say instead of "add more detail"

The most useful feedback move during and after the task is to find the **first place a reader has to ask "but why?"** and ask about that gap specifically.

> "Bacteria make acid. The tooth hurts."

"Add more detail" tells the child nothing. **"What happens between the acid being made and the tooth hurting?"** makes them supply the missing mechanism.

Put those questions in `speakerNotes.lookFor` on the task beat, naming the links most likely to be skipped and the question to ask at each: `Look for: the acid named as something the germs make - if a child jumps from sugar to the hole, ask what the germs did with the sugar.` That is diagnostic in a way that asking for length is not.

## When the launch shows the model

Two things the launch pair has to do, both owned by `preferences.md` → Slide Philosophy:

**Do not ask "which is better?"** The class answers that correctly from length alone and learns nothing. Ask instead for the exact words where the strong one explains something the weak one only states, or which link the strong one has made explicit, or whether the weak one can be improved without making it much longer.

**Model the invisible thinking in the script.** A perfect paragraph shown silently teaches less than a teacher thinking aloud: "I know sugar is involved and I know germs are involved. But if I write 'sugar is on the tooth, germs make acid', my reader has to guess the connection. I need to say what the germs do with the sugar."

## Sentence combining is a reasoning lesson

When a child has the knowledge but writes it as separate sentences, the obstacle is often sentence construction rather than understanding. Give the facts and ask for the relationship:

> Germs live in plaque. Germs use sugar. Germs produce acid.
> → "The germs in the plaque use the sugar and produce acid."

That is a vocabulary lesson, a sentence lesson and a reasoning lesson at once, and it is a good Do beat before an explanation task.
