# Teaching Sequence — Skill-Based (MT / OT / YT)

Use this file when the lesson-designer has chosen **Skill-based** teaching for a repeatable skill or procedure. My Turn → Our Turn → Your Turn is the core rhythm. Add a short explanation before My Turn when the actual skill needs one; otherwise move straight into modelling.

The structure choice itself is made in the main agent's Structure Decision section. By the time this file is read, the structure decision and the boundary tests have already been applied — what follows is execution detail for the chosen rhythm.

A short pattern investigation or method comparison may come before direct modelling when it genuinely helps children notice the structure. So may a **bounded first attempt** at the target itself — a few minutes with the real equipment or task before any model: *Can you make the bulb light?* Use it only when the attempt is safe, cheap and quick to reset, the goal is self-evident, and success or failure is visible to the child without the teacher judging it. The attempt teaches nothing by itself and is not discovery; its job is to give the full modelling that follows immediately something real to explain, so the child whose bulb stayed dark hears "the loop must be complete" as the answer to a question they already have. Do not use it where a wrong attempt looks fine to the child or quietly rehearses an error — a circuit fails visibly, a written calculation method fails silently — and `subject-maths.md` owns the maths position (maths lessons open with the model). Either way it remains part of the Skill-based route: the procedure is then explicitly explained, modelled and secured rather than left for novices to invent through unguided trial and error.

**A bounded attempt only works while the answer is still the child's to find.** Audit everything the class meets before it - the starter, the vocabulary cards, every visible reference and photograph - and keep the successful arrangement, rule or answer the attempt exists to expose out of all of it. A starter photograph of the working circuit, or a `complete / incomplete` card taught two minutes earlier, turns *Can you make the lamp light?* into copying from the board, and the modelling that follows then answers a question nobody has. Before the attempt, show only what children need to start - the loose equipment, the task itself; the term for what they found is taught after the attempt has given it meaning. This audit is part of choosing the attempt, and the reviewer checks it: an attempt whose answer is already on display is a purposeful design defect, not polish.

## Teaching Sequence Specification

For each skill being taught, design a My Turn → Our Turn → Your Turn sequence. Small variations of one procedure may share a cycle only when they are closely connected and easy enough to learn together. Split them when combining the variations creates too much complexity, even if they belong to the same broad procedure.

**My Turn** — the teacher demonstrates the whole method or skill with a specific example.

Choose the example carefully: it should make the concept clear without unnecessary complexity. The first example is a minimum-viable case — clean, unambiguous, easy to follow. If a second example is needed (to show a boundary case or common variation), it comes after the first, not instead of it.

**Match the number of examples to how long one instance takes, and give the Our Turn the same number.** Where one worked example is lengthy (a column subtraction, a multi-step conversion, a bar model built from scratch), one clean demonstration earns its minute and a second of the same case only pads the lesson. Where one instance takes seconds (finding 10 more on a place-value chart, naming a turn, reading a fraction off a bar), a second rep costs almost nothing and fluency comes from volume, so model two and have the Our Turn answer two as well: a lone example of a five-second task feels thin, and a class that has watched the move twice attempts it far more readily. The working default is therefore two for quick instances and one for lengthy ones, judged against how ready this class is likely to be and the time the lesson has; distinct-case coverage below can push it higher. It is a default, not a quota, so two examples of a move that takes two minutes each is padding.

**Model every distinct case the independent practice will require.** Some skills run the same way every time; others split into distinct cases the child must tell apart and handle differently — a division grid where the missing box can be the answer, the first number, or the second number; a conversion that reverses by direction; a sequence question whose candidates pass or fail by a rule. When the Your Turn will mix those cases, the child needs to have *seen each one worked* before meeting it alone, because a case modelled nowhere but appearing in independent practice is a case the child meets cold. So plan the modelling across My Turn and Our Turn together to span the set — each distinct case demonstrated at least once on its own worked example, with the Our Turn carrying the hardest position. Reaching an unmodelled case means another example inside the My Turn when it teaches the same move, or a second cycle when the case is genuinely a different move (the structure section below governs which); it never means a second My Turn slide stacked on the first. The failure to avoid is spending both My Turn examples on the same case while another case goes unmodelled: it looks like two worked examples, but it leaves a hole the Your Turn drops into.

**Coverage decides which examples appear; choose *which ones* so that what changes between them is what you want children to notice.** Children work out what matters in a method by seeing what varies and what holds still, so two examples that change everything at once teach less than two that change one thing. Model 24 ÷ 4 and then 36 ÷ 6 and both numbers have moved, so a child cannot tell which move mattered; model 24 ÷ 4 and then 24 ÷ 8 and the effect of the divisor is the only thing on show. The same in grammar: "Suddenly, the door opened" followed by "Suddenly, the dog barked" varies only the scenery and shows a child nothing new, while "In the morning, the door opened" holds the sentence still and reveals that the adverbial can be a phrase rather than a single word. So pick the first example as the cleanest instance of the method, with nothing incidental in it to distract from the steps, and pick each one after it to move the single feature the children need to see next.

The limit: this governs consecutive examples inside one concept, and it does not override the distinct-case coverage above, which sometimes needs a bigger jump to reach an unmodelled case. Deliberately varying a surface feature is also right when the teaching point is that the feature is irrelevant, for example changing the units or the names to show the method does not care about them. What to avoid is the accidental version, where several things move at once because the examples were written independently rather than chosen as a sequence.

When the teacher's brief names a number of modelled examples that is fewer than the number of distinct cases — "my turn, 2 questions" for a skill with three positions — coverage governs. Model each distinct case as its own worked example, and record the adjustment and its reason in design-decisions.md so the final hand-off stays anchored to the coverage decision. The brief's count is a starting shape; the cases the child will independently practise are the binding constraint, because the modelling exists to ready the child for exactly those.

**When the new learning is a *tool* applied to familiar content, keep the modelling's cases clear and let the Your Turn carry the real discrimination.** Some lessons teach a new method on new content; others teach a new *representation* — a Venn or Carroll diagram, a bar model, a number line — that children apply to content they already know (shapes they can already classify, numbers they can already compare). There the new thing is the tool, so the modelling's job is to show the tool's mechanic clearly: place one instance in each region or cell so the child sees *how* placement works, using cases obvious enough that no attention is spent on the content itself. Showing each distinct case once still holds — the child should see a "both" and a "neither" placed before meeting them alone — but a clear demonstration is all the modelling owes them. The genuine testing — the less obvious shapes, the fuller range, the discriminations a child has to reason through — belongs in the independent Your Turn, where applying the tool *is* the learning being checked. The failure to avoid is the inverse: pouring the hardest discriminations into My Turn and Our Turn and then giving a Your Turn that re-runs the same easy instances already shown, so the modelling does the thinking and the independent practice tests nothing. Decide which is new — the tool or the content — and keep the familiar one simple, so the child's effort lands on what they are actually learning, and the Your Turn earns its difficulty rather than echoing the demo.

Concretely for a sorting tool: if My Turn and Our Turn sorted shapes by one criteria pair, give the Your Turn a *different* criteria pair, so the same familiar shapes land in new regions and the child has to genuinely test each one rather than recall where it sat a moment ago. A Your Turn that re-sorts the exact shapes the teacher just placed, by the same labels, is a copy of the demo, not independent practice — the child finishing it would barely notice the slide changed. The method stays identical (that is the point of the rhythm); the *problem* is fresh. The same logic holds for any tool: a number line marked with new values, a bar model comparing new quantities — the representation is the constant, the question is new.

**Some skills are recognition, not procedure — and they are taught by establishing the set, not by modelling a method.** Naming a turn, naming a word class, sorting a shape, identifying a fraction from a bar: there is no method to step through, only an instance to match against a set of named categories. For these, the teaching before practice first *establishes and displays the whole set* — every category named and shown, with any distinction the practice depends on (clockwise vs anticlockwise, the boundary between two classes) made visible — so the child carries the set into the identification rather than meeting a category for the first time inside a "My Turn, what is this?". Where that set was taught in an earlier year it is a brisk recap that re-surfaces and shows it, not an assumption it is still secure; an embodied or concrete route is often the quickest way to re-establish it (children stand and make each turn before naming one on a diagram). The My Turn and Our Turn then identify instances *against* that displayed set, and the displayed, labelled set is the live reference the success criteria carries (`preferences.md` → Success Criteria owns that form choice and its why).

**A process must be modelled, not displayed as its own output.** `preferences.md` → Success Criteria sets the discriminator between the two kinds of skill (a method to carry out is a process; an instance to match against named categories is recognition) and one lesson often holds one of each. What the process half means for the teaching: reading or deriving an attribute is a *process*, even inside a lesson whose title sounds like classification, and it is taught by modelling the method on an example: the teacher works each criterion on the shape in front of the class, annotating it or filling a small table live as they go, so children watch the properties being *found*. It is not taught by putting the finished attributes on the slide as a labelled list. Establishing-and-displaying-the-set belongs to the recognition half — matching an instance to *named categories* (which quadrilateral, which word class) — and there it is the categories themselves you show, not the attribute-labels a child reads off an instance. The failure to avoid: presenting a process's outputs as a static list ("here are the six properties") on the very beat where children should have watched you find them — that turns a modelling beat into a reading beat, and the method is never seen in action. A blank or partly-blank `table` the teacher completes live (property down the side, "true for this shape?" beside it) is often the cleanest way to model a property-reading process, because the filling-in *is* the modelling.

The success criteria belongs on this slide as a live reference, visible while the teacher models — so children see each criterion in use as they read it. For a procedure that is the steps being demonstrated; for a recognition skill it is the labelled set being matched against.

Any visual tool children will use (a number line, a classification table, a diagram) should be on the My Turn slide. Children need to see the tool in use during modelling so they know how to use it themselves.

**Some skills transform a whole text from one form into another, and the material the child works from must be the start form, complete and unconverted.** Converting a story passage into a playscript, an active sentence into the passive, a set of notes into a paragraph, direct speech into reported speech: the skill *is* the transformation, and the gap between the two forms is the whole lesson. The child needs the start form in full, including the very features that get transformed, because spotting what to change and changing it is the work being assessed. Scaffolding the *content* is right and often necessary (a Year 4 child converting a Shakespeare scene should be handed the characters' words, not asked to invent Shakespeare), but you scaffold by supplying the start form fully, never by pre-shaping it toward the target. Hand the child a story paragraph with its dialogue written the story way, `"If I touch your hand and it is not worthy, forgive me," said Romeo`, and the conversion is genuinely theirs: they find the speaker, drop the "said" and the speech marks, write `ROMEO:`, and lift the action into a bracketed stage direction. Pre-attribute that same line in a separate box, `Romeo: "If I touch your hand..."`, and the core move is already done for them: "converting" collapses into recopying with a colon, which is the reformat-without-judgement trap (design-reviewer: "a task that only reformats the material it hands over"). The read-back test: look at the source you are about to hand over and ask whether any part of it already sits in the target form. If a child could reach the finished script by copying your source and adding punctuation, the source has leaked the answer, so rewrite it as pure start-form prose with the speech embedded the story way.

**A transformation is one integrated move on a whole passage, so model it whole and practise it whole.** The features the child produces (name-and-colon, bracketed stage directions, narrator bridges, lines down the page) are not separate concepts each needing their own Teach → Do beat; they are the checklist the child runs *while* making the single move, and they show best all at once, by converting one whole passage in front of the class. Teaching each feature as its own isolated beat (a slide drilling stage directions, then a slide drilling narrators) atomises one skill into several, multiplies the slide count, and pulls the lesson away from the thing it is for. When the lesson continues a form children met recently, re-establish the whole form briskly with one converted example that shows every feature, rather than re-teaching each feature in turn; give a short dedicated beat only to a genuinely new demand (a second narrator where yesterday there was one), and name even that inside the whole-passage model rather than as its own concept cycle. Independent practice follows the same logic: the Your Turn is one fresh passage to convert, not a numbered list of line-by-line sub-tasks. A child converting a whole paragraph is doing exactly what the LO asks; a child answering "write Romeo's line, now write Juliet's line, now add a narrator" is being walked through the very move the lesson wanted them to make unaided.

**Describe the modelled experience through the resource state.** Follow `references/modelling-formats.md` and state what later resources must provide: a prepared completed example, a blank or partly blank helper for live completion, the exact question and useful references while working is modelled elsewhere, or suitable support for a physical demonstration. Do not tell the teacher where they must write.

Children must still see the important process, choices and checks a novice would otherwise miss. A prepared finished example is valid when inspecting the finished form is the useful teaching experience, but it cannot replace seeing a process carried out when producing the answer is the learning.

Judge each genuinely different modelled move separately. Different moves in one lesson may need different resource states, but do not split one simple model into artificial mini-moves. Every helper intended for possible annotation must be large, clear and usable. Keep generic working boxes removed. In the structured hand-off, each genuinely distinct My Turn modelled move is its own `my-turn` source unit inside the same concept, so it can carry its own starting instance, modelling state, representation configuration, script and answer/model. Do not split one simple model merely because Slide Designer may later use several physical slides.

The exact disposable example remains governed by the existing move-versus-instance rule in `lesson-designer.md`. A teacher-facing suggested exemplar may still be supplied when it helps delivery.

- *When a model uses a diagram or visual helper (clock reading, shape naming, fraction-from-bar identification, graph or tally reading, or filling a diagram's own blank slots: a grid's cells, a part-whole model's circles):* follow the selected source-unit `modellingState` rather than inferring the state from the presence of a visual. A Prepared example may show the completed form when studying it is useful. A Live-complete helper leaves the part being learned blank or constructable so it can be completed during modelling. A Question and reference state keeps the exact question and useful figure or reference visible while written working happens away from the helper. No separate generic working column is added. When a Live-complete helper is selected, leave its active part for live completion; for My Turn, do not create a following answer slide. When a Prepared example is selected, the completed form may be visible from the start. The My Turn and Our Turn each take their own fresh visual, left in the configuration required by their selected resource state.

- *For a calculation, column method, partition or conversion in maths, or sentence-level and paragraph-level writing:* follow the selected source-unit `modellingState`; do not infer one merely because the work is written. When the state is Question and reference, the slide spec carries no working space and no pre-rendered working; the freed room expands the reference panel, and the teacher decides where to write. Phrase the brief in terms of the required resource state and what children need to see: *"During the My Turn, the teacher models [the column subtraction] while the class watches; the SC panel and any reference stay on the slide."* Do not ask the slide-designer to invent a working zone when the selected state does not require one.

- *Physical-demonstration support - science apparatus, an art or PE technique, or any maths or English move shown with real equipment in the teacher's hands:* the modelling happens with the equipment. The slide carries no generic working space or pre-filled answer unless the selected support genuinely requires one; it holds only the identification, setup, safety information, diagram, steps or reference that supports the demonstration. Most lessons that set this are content-based or task-centred, but a skill-based maths lesson modelled with a real protractor, ruler, or base-10 blocks can set it too.

- *Prepared example (including English writing-skill lessons when a completed model should be visible from the start):* put the complete selected example in the lesson design so the slide-designer can render it alongside any useful reference. Use this only when studying the completed form is genuinely the intended modelling experience; otherwise follow the selected resource state.

**For Question-and-reference writing, also supply a Modelled exemplar.** When the source unit's `modellingState` is `Question and reference` for sentence- or paragraph-level writing, the teacher models the writing live while children watch. Spare them the "give me a moment" pause at the start of the modelling beat by storing the modelled sentence, paragraph, or short piece in that Skill My Turn's `content.modelledExemplar`. The teacher reads it from the lesson design and uses it for the live model.

The exemplar should be the actual writing children would aspire to — pitched to the year group, demonstrating the skill being taught, short enough to model live in 2–4 minutes. For a Year 4 fronted-adverbial lesson, that's a single sentence with a clear adverbial and a comma. For a Year 5 lesson on cohesive devices, that's a 3–4 sentence paragraph using two or three of the devices the lesson teaches. Demonstrate the skill cleanly; do not load the exemplar with skills outside today's LO.

`content.modelledExemplar` appears only for Question-and-reference writing. Question-and-reference maths modelling (a calculation, column method, partition, conversion) needs no separate exemplar: that My/Our Turn source unit's `content.example` already holds the question, and the teacher works the calculation out live using the SC steps. A `Physical-demonstration support` move models an action, not a sentence, so it needs no written exemplar either. For a `Prepared example`, the completed example already plays this role.

The failure mode to avoid: writing the brief as an end-state demand — *"the slide must end up showing the completed digital time"* — without naming the resource state. A reasonable downstream agent reads that as *"the slide spec must specify the answer in a slot."* A `Prepared example` supplies the completed example explicitly; the other resource states do not thereby require a pre-filled answer slot, so the agent must not improvise one (a parenthetical, a stage direction in the question text, a paraphrased question). The fix is upstream: phrase the brief in terms of the required resource state and what children need to see during the model, and the downstream agent has a clear path that doesn't require improvisation.

The test: read your My Turn brief back and ask *what does the slide-designer do with this?* If the answer is "render the selected resource state faithfully, with the verbatim speaker-note script," good. If the answer is "specify the answer pre-filled somewhere on the slide," check whether you genuinely mean a `Prepared example` or whether another resource state is intended — and rephrase to match.

**Our Turn** — one or more fresh guided examples, with the amount chosen from the concept's likely difficulty. Children do the important thinking alongside the teacher rather than only watching. State the question, learning action and any substantive response structure that genuinely changes the task; the teacher chooses the routine response or recording method.

Keep each example close enough to My Turn to practise the same learning and different enough to require application. Focus guided questions on the decisions children will later make independently. Do not introduce an unmodelled case that changes the procedure. Provide useful task-specific teaching guidance without prescribing marking, cold calling, books or another classroom-management routine.

**When the example lives in a multi-item visual (a tally chart with rows, a pictogram with rows, a bar chart with bars, a frequency table, a set of shapes) and the work is reading a value off that chart — whether finding every row's value or answering one named row at a time — a fresh Our Turn means a fresh visual with new data, not different rows of the My Turn's chart.** Two shapes of this trap: modelling two rows in My Turn and saving the other two for the Our Turn (a fill-every-row task like finding each frequency), or reading one row in My Turn — "how many books on Tuesday?" — then a different row in the Our Turn — "how many on Wednesday?" — off the same chart. Both look like two examples but are one: the whole chart, every row, is on screen throughout the My Turn, so the row the Our Turn turns to was never new, and children read off a chart they have already watched the teacher read. It lands as the same question answered twice. So give the Our Turn its own chart with a new context and new numbers, carrying the same cases the My Turn taught plus the error-prone one (for a tally chart, a row whose remainder is four marks, which looks like a fifth bundle). Model the full first chart in My Turn so it stands as a complete worked example, then move to the fresh chart for the guided practice. (One shared dataset *across* turns is right only when the dataset is a fixed thing children read repeatedly and the *question changes to a genuinely different move* between turns — a "use this chart to answer" interpretation concept where My Turn finds a difference and Our Turn finds a total, so the child applies a new operation rather than repeating the same read on a new row. It is wrong when each turn runs the same read-off skill on a different item of the same visible chart.)

Plan enough Our Turn work for the concept's likely difficulty. If the concept looks tricky, include several fresh guided attempts rather than defaulting to one. The live teacher may skip planned guided examples when the real class is ready. Include an Our Turn unless guided participation would remove no barrier the model and success criteria have not already removed — because the learning is already secure enough, or because of the recording case below; do not use either exception to jump from one model to independent work on genuinely new or difficult learning.

**When a concept records what children have just done, one model then release is the default.** A lesson sometimes ends by having children represent their own first-hand work — drawing the circuit they built, writing up the method they just used. That recording is still its own concept with its own success criteria when it carries genuinely new learning, but it does not automatically earn a full second cycle: each child is holding the real thing they made, which is richer guided context than any fresh shared example, so model the representation once on the teacher's example and release children onto their own. Add an Our Turn there only when the representation itself carries a hard new convention (formal notation, a symbol system) that one model will predictably fail to secure.

**Your Turn** — enough meaningful independent practice for the target action, cases and response cost. A compact table or sort may contain many brief decisions, while one whole passage, investigation step or constructed representation may be one substantial performance. Do not force three to six printed questions, a fixed easy-to-hard staircase or a harder final item. The practice must still be sufficient to establish and demonstrate the learning. Follow definite-answer work with an answer slide.

Plan guided practice so successful independence is plausible. The live teacher decides from the real class whether to release, skip a planned guided example or add further support.

**Independent work means that children do the important thinking themselves.** Keep, reduce or remove each support according to the target thinking rather than the stage label.

- Keep success criteria, a word bank, representation, reference table or visual tool when it enables the intended performance without supplying the answer or making the key decision.
- Remove or reduce a support when consulting it would perform the new thinking children are meant to do independently.
- A worked example may remain when it is genuinely the most useful quality reference, comparison model or reference support and children must still generate the important answer or decision themselves.
- Do not require a full → partial → blank fade across a set or assume a support must disappear by Your Turn.

Read the independent task and ask what the child still has to decide, produce, explain or perform. That answer, not the presence or absence of a scaffold, determines whether the work is independent.

**Execution before selection is a strong default for a genuinely new repeatable method.** Normally secure reasonable execution before asking children to choose when, why or how to use the method. Do not manufacture a separate fluency phase when judgement or selection is inseparable from the skill itself. In maths, fluency-first remains a strong available lesson shape when it fits; `subject-maths.md` owns that subject-specific judgement.

## The Practice Set

Independent practice in a skill-based lesson is focused and repeated, because the goal is reliable execution: until the procedure is automated it consumes the working memory the child needs for anything richer. Order the set by variation theory — keep most features constant, change one — so the child sees what matters and what doesn't.

**Choose the practice architecture before writing the items.** Name the core
action, the amount of successful practice it needs, and the cases the child must
learn to distinguish. Then decide whether the instances should stand separately
or be organised so their relationship is visible. Keep related results together
when noticing the invariant or contrast is part of the learning; separate them
when each instance requires independent selection or working and its neighbours
would become a cue. Count the actions children perform rather than the number
of labels printed beside them. The boundary is the integrated task: transforming
one whole passage or constructing one complete representation may be enough
because the target action itself is extended. Do not multiply it into fragments
to satisfy a repetition count.

**Block while it is brand new; mix once it has a rival.** Keep the first items close to the modelled example while the pattern has nothing to be confused with yet. Once it has a competitor (the other rule, the confusable category, last week's method), mix the cases rather than running a long row of near-identical items: blocking helps a child notice what is the same within a pattern, mixing is what teaches them to tell patterns apart, and in the direct classroom tests outside maths the mixed set won at retention weeks later. Two warnings ride with this rule. Mixed practice looks worse in the lesson — more errors, children feeling less sure — and wins later, so it is not judged by how the lesson felt. And the direct evidence outside maths is thin (a small number of studies), so this is the better bet, not the proven answer. In maths, fluency ordering and practice mechanics are governed by `subject-maths.md`; this paragraph does not force one surface form.

## Writing the Success Criteria

When the success criteria for a concept is a procedure written as how-to steps, these are the mechanics of writing those steps well. Choosing the *form* of the success criteria (how-to steps, a reference table, or a labelled set the child matches an instance against) is governed by `preferences.md` → Success Criteria, which the lesson-designer reads every run; what follows is how to write the steps once that form is chosen.

**Writing how-to steps:** Each step is a short, simple verb-first action the child can carry out. One action per step. Aim strongly for 2–5 words. Use a slightly longer step only when shortening it would make the instruction cryptic or unclear; this is not permission for wordy success criteria, and the validator refuses a step past 12 words.

**The steps are the procedure in the order the child performs it.** A child reads step 1 and does it, then step 2, so the list has to be the method as hands do it, not a set of true statements about the method. Write the steps by carrying out the worked example with them: at each step, what does the child do next? A line that cannot be done at the moment it appears is out of order or is not a step. `Use tens for 10 or hundreds for 100 / For less, exchange from the left until you can remove one / Add one for more; remove one for less / Has the column reached 10? Exchange 10 for 1 / Read the number` fails that test: step 1 names a rule rather than an action, and the exchange arrives before the child has taken anything away. The same method as a child does it: `Find the tens column (or hundreds for 100). / Add one counter for more, or take one away for less. / Full column? Exchange 10 for 1. Empty column? Exchange 1 for 10. / Read the new number.` A fact the child must hold while working (`the ones digit never changes`) is sticky knowledge, not a step, and sits beside the list.

**Steps encode the decision rule, never an artefact of the worked example.** Ask of each step: would a different-but-correct performance fail it? Then the step is teaching the example, not the skill. `Connect the cell to the switch, then the switch to the lamp` fails a perfectly good circuit built in another order, and quietly swaps the taught invariant (*one complete loop*) for a ritual - the child leaves believing the order mattered. Keep order in the steps only when the order IS the procedure being taught, as in an algorithm's own sequence; the order the model happened to be built in never qualifies.

Good: "Multiply the pounds by 100."
Bad: "Multiply the whole pounds by 100 (this converts them to pence)."

Good SC step: "Work out the minute hand."
Bad SC step: "Look at the hour hand (short). It points to or just past a number — that's the hour."

The same mechanics hold outside maths. A PE throwing lesson:

Good: "Point your other arm at the target."
Bad: "Use your non-throwing arm to aim by pointing it toward where you want the ball to go (this keeps you balanced)."

**Name a familiar or just-taught action only when children genuinely know how to carry it out.** A short label may remind children of a secure sub-procedure without re-teaching it. Do not hide the lesson's new or difficult thinking inside an unexplained label; when that action is the learning, the criteria must make it runnable. Where a step needs to point at a literal piece of writing (the templated answer, the colon in a digital time, the unit), put the literal mark in the step itself: `"Write: ___ past."`, `":"`, `"Write it down."`. Children can copy a literal mark; they can't act on a description.

**When a step branches on what the question contains** — for example a conversion where the direction changes the operation — write each branch as a named lookup row using the actual content the child sees, not an abstract category label. A child scans the SC to find their input and reads off the action directly.

Good: "Hours → minutes? × 60"
Bad: "Smaller unit? Multiply by 60."

"Hours → minutes?" matches the exact words in the child's question. "Smaller unit?" asks the child to first decide which unit is smaller — a conceptual step that stalls them before they reach the answer. Name the content, not the category. The same principle applies to any branch, in any subject. A grammar lesson choosing *a* or *an*:

Good: "Next word starts with a, e, i, o or u sound? → an"
Bad: "Choose the article that matches the following sound."

The good row is a decision the child can run against the word in front of them; the bad row is a rule they must interpret before they can start.

When a task genuinely branches, use the clearest child-runnable guide: a table, flowchart, short decision list or another suitable form. Do not default to a flowchart or create a branching guide for a simple non-branching method. Whatever the form, write it as a decision the child can run, not a set of labels:

- **Phrase each branch as the question the child asks** — "Is the answer missing?", "Is the first number missing?" — rather than a bare label ("Answer missing"). A question is something the child actively answers yes/no as they scan; a label is something they have to interpret first.
- **Name the cases in one consistent scheme the child can map onto any instance.** For `first number ÷ second number = answer`, the three positions are the *answer*, the *first number*, and the *second number*. Calling one of them "the ÷ box" breaks the scheme — it names a mark on the slide instead of the slot in the calculation the child is reasoning about — so the child can no longer line each row up against their question.
- **Keep the action half parallel so only the deciding part changes down the column.** When every row ends in the same rule ("÷10 → 1 place, ÷100 → 2 places") and only the leading move differs ("Slide RIGHT" / "Slide LEFT" / "Count how far the digits moved"), the child reads one rule with a few variations instead of three separate instructions to decode.
- **Keep each cell short — a glance reference, not a sentence.** Trim anything the move already implies: a "Read the answer" tail after "Slide right" earns nothing, and length is what forces the table to shrink. The stacked, one-line-per-part look (the move, then ÷10, then ÷100 each on its own line) reads beautifully when the success criteria has a roomy zone of its own; in a narrow reference panel, forcing each part onto its own line adds height that shrinks the whole table toward unreadable, so there a short single line that wraps naturally is the better call.
- **Order the rows in a sequence the child can anticipate.** Follow the parts of the calculation in turn — for `first number ÷ second number = answer`, the rows go answer, first number, second number (or first, second, answer): a consistent walk through the structure, not a jumbled order. A predictable order lets the child run down the table by position rather than re-reading each row to work out where they are.
- **Pick out the one word that changes between rows.** The deciding word — *answer*, *first number*, *second number* — is what the child must identify before they can act, so give it visual weight (bold, and a colour used consistently for "the thing to decide") while the scaffolding around it ("Is the ___ missing?") stays plain. The eye then lands on the decision variable instead of reading every word at equal weight. The same holds for the direction word in the action half.

The aim throughout is a table the child can *run*: scan the questions top to bottom, stop at the first yes, read off the move. Correct-but-unparallel rows hold the same information and make the child do the organising the table should have done for them.

**One concept, one SC.** Each concept teaches one thing end-to-end and carries one success criteria. When that thing is a procedure, its SC is the procedure as a numbered list — short verb-first imperatives, one action per step; when it is a recognition or classification (naming a turn, sorting a word class), its SC is the labelled reference set, per `preferences.md` → Success Criteria. If you find yourself wanting to write two SCs for one concept (one for output X, one for output Y) that's the signal that you've got two procedures sharing a concept, not one — split them into two concepts at the structure level (see "Picking the splitting axis" earlier). For example, "read and write times in words and digital notation" → Concept 1 teaches the in-words procedure with its own SC; Concept 2 teaches the digital procedure with its own SC.

**The SC must not change across My Turn, Our Turn, and Your Turn.** The teacher uses it during My Turn to teach. Children refer to it during Our Turn to learn how to use it. It remains available as the same reference during Your Turn. If the wording drifts — even slightly — between those three slides, it reads as a different rule and the repetition benefit is lost. Write the steps once and reuse them verbatim across all three slides in a concept.

**When Concept 2 wraps around Concept 1's procedure, fold the key decision cues into Concept 2's SC.** A lesson where Concept 2 includes "convert" or "apply the method we just learned" as one of its steps is a wrap-around: children must run the Concept 1 procedure as a sub-step of every Concept 2 problem. If the Concept 2 SC just writes "Convert to the same unit" and nothing more, children have to remember the full Concept 1 procedure from memory while simultaneously managing the new Concept 2 thinking — the SC has stopped being a live reference and become a memory test. Fix: embed the key decision cues from Concept 1 into the relevant Concept 2 step. Not the full SC verbatim, but enough to trigger the right procedure: "Convert to the same unit → find the units → pick your fact (60, 12 or 7) → × for smaller, ÷ for larger". This keeps the SC as one self-sufficient list children can work from without needing to look anywhere else.

## Output Format Block

When writing `lesson-design.json`, append one source-unit object to `teachingSequence` for each leaf instructional beat, in final lesson order. The common source-unit fields live in `output-template.md`. Use these `kind` and `content` shapes for Skill-based teaching.

A preparation beat before My Turn, when genuinely needed, is an explicit source unit:

```json
{
  "kind": "prepare",
  "content": {
    "mode": "explanation",
    "activity": "the exact concise preparation children experience before modelling"
  }
}
```

`mode` is exactly one of:

- `explanation`
- `pattern-investigation`
- `method-comparison`
- `establish-reference`
- `criteria-teaching`
- `bounded-attempt`

Use no preparation unit when the lesson should move straight into My Turn. This preserves the existing route's optional explanation, bounded pattern/method work, recognition-set establishment and the rare case where the criteria themselves genuinely need teaching.

`bounded-attempt` carries the brief first try at the target itself, under the conditions in this file's opening section; put the exact challenge children are given in `activity`.

`explanation` carries the short teaching some skills need before the first model. Its `activity` is then the explanation as the child reads it, two or three short lines on the board (what the idea means, why it matters, what it looks like), not a description of an activity and not a slogan the script explains (`preferences.md` → Slide Philosophy, `A heading, a fact or a rule on the board is not the teaching of it`).

For each concept, use zero or more preparation units when genuinely needed, then **one or more My Turn plus Our Turn cycles**, then exactly one Your Turn. Each cycle is one My Turn source unit carrying that move's modelled examples, followed by at most one Our Turn for the same concept. Most concepts need one cycle. A concept whose independent practice needs a second modelled move takes a second cycle rather than a second My Turn beside the first, under the rule below. The Our Turn is present unless the omission test in the Our Turn section applies; omitting it is a purposeful decision, not a shortcut.

**Several examples of one move belong inside one My Turn unit; a genuinely different move earns its own cycle rather than a second My Turn beside the first.** A unit becomes its own slide, so two My Turn slides in a row means children watched two things before practising either: the second model arrives to a class still holding an unfinished first one, and the move modelled first commonly gets no guided practice at all before it turns up in independent work. A Year 4 lesson on finding 10 and 100 more or less modelled a plain number, then modelled crossing a thousand, then guided only the crossing, and the plain case went straight from one demonstration to the children's books. So put every example of the move this unit teaches in its own `content`, and when a second modelled move is genuinely needed (a boundary case with its own decision rule, a reverse exchange, a different starting point) give it a second cycle with an Our Turn between, so the sequence runs My Turn, Our Turn, My Turn, Our Turn, Your Turn and each move is used before the next is taught. `validate-lesson-design.py` refuses two My Turn units in a row for the same concept, and the slide check refuses two consecutive My Turn slides (`MODELLING_RUNS_WITHOUT_A_TURN_FOR_THE_CLASS`), which is the same board reached by splitting one unit whose examples cannot share a visual. This is a structural rule rather than a preference to weigh. A My Turn unit carries its own `content`, `modellingState`, `representationRefs`, `speakerNotes` and `answer`, and is never duplicated merely because Slide Designer may later split one move across several physical slides.

Choose a unit's examples so they share their starting value and one representation wherever the move allows, which is the same minimal-variation reasoning as above and is also what keeps the unit on one slide: `643 + 10 =` beside `643 - 100 =` holds the number still, shows both columns, and needs one chart with two answer rows, where two unrelated numbers would need two charts and force a split.

All My Turn, Our Turn and Your Turn units for the same concept use the same `conceptRef` and exact concept `successCriteriaRefs`. Preparation units use `conceptRef: null`.

My Turn:

```json
{
  "kind": "my-turn",
  "conceptRef": "concept-001",
  "content": {
    "example": "the exact question or worked problem",
    "modelledExemplar": null
  }
}
```

For sentence- or paragraph-level Question-and-reference writing, put the exact teacher-facing exemplar in `modelledExemplar`; otherwise use `null`.

Our Turn:

```json
{
  "kind": "our-turn",
  "conceptRef": "concept-001",
  "content": {
    "example": "the fresh guided example",
    "guidedQuestions": [
      "the exact first question the teacher asks",
      "the exact next question"
    ]
  }
}
```

Your Turn:

```json
{
  "kind": "your-turn",
  "conceptRef": "concept-001",
  "content": {
    "activityArchitecture": "the core action, amount, deliberate variation and whether instances stay together or stand independently",
    "task": "the exact independent work in its chosen form"
  }
}
```

Do not copy success-criteria wording into these `content` objects. Define it once under `successCriteria`, attach it to the concept, and copy the same `successCriteriaRefs` onto **every** My Turn plus the Our Turn (when present) and the one Your Turn for that concept.

Do not describe a helper again inside `content`. Define the representation once and attach the exact `representationRefs` use to each turn.

Put each turn's exact script in its source-unit `speakerNotes`. Put its answer/model/standard only in the structured `answer` object.
