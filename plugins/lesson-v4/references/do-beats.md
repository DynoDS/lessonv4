# Do Beat Formats — Reference Catalogue

A **Do beat** is a short consolidation or checking move between genuinely different taught ideas. It requires every child to use the chunk they have just been taught, and it leaves something the teacher can see: a written answer, a partner's spoken response, a visible decision, a physical position. A question put to the room is not one unless it is chosen so that every child commits to an answer that needs the idea just taught (`preferences.md` → The Teach → Do → Teach → Do Rhythm, `Questioning is not doing`, owns the line). One to three minutes is a useful estimate, not a hard limit. A spoken response, visible decision, movement, physical action, drawing or writing may be enough; every Do beat does not need a permanent written artefact. If the activity becomes substantial, treat it as main practice rather than letting a nominally quick beat swallow the lesson.

This document is a selective activity catalogue, not a second statement of the plugin's overall teaching philosophy. Response channel and cognitive demand are separate decisions. First settle the thinking children need and the actual demands or barriers the response form creates. Then choose a suitable format and consider whether an equally suitable change of form would reduce monotony, fatigue or an access barrier. Variety matters, but there is no register quota and no compulsory non-writing activity. Repetition remains appropriate when repeated performance builds the target skill.

**Register is not the same axis as demand, and a lesson needs to be chosen on both.** Register is about attention and access: switching channels keeps children awake and gives a child who fades on writing another way in. Demand is about how hard the thinking is: whether the child is surfacing something they already hold, or working something out. The two are independent, and every register below contains formats at every level of demand. Match the Pairs and Odd One Out are both sort-and-classify beats, but one asks a child to recognise four things they were just told and the other asks them to find a criterion nobody gave them. A lesson can span four registers, satisfy every rule about variety, and still ask children to do nothing harder than remember, because nothing in a register check can see that.

So pick each beat on both axes, and think about the shape across the lesson rather than beat by beat. The three bands, and what each is for:

- **Surface** (recall a fact, name a thing, match given items to given items, label from memory). Cheap, fast, and the right choice when you genuinely need to know the class caught something before you build on it. This is the band that quietly fills a lesson, because these beats are the easiest to write.
- **Work with** (apply the idea to a fresh instance, sort where the criterion has to be found, correct an error, choose between two positions and say why, rank and justify). The child holds the idea and does something to it.
- **Reason with** (explain a cause, compare two things on a criterion they select, judge a claim that is true-but-misleading, transfer the idea to a situation the lesson never showed them, generate a new example and defend it).

**A placement alone is thin evidence; a one-line why upgrades it cheaply.** A sort, match or classify beat whose only output is placements can be passed by luck or half-knowledge. Where the placement decisions carry the learning, add the short justification prompt - `How do you know?`, `Which clue told you?` - so the check shows the reasoning behind a placement, not just the placements. Skip it when the same reasoning is already demanded inside the beat or in the one that follows.

**The shape across the lesson matters more than any single beat.** Early beats after a brand-new chunk can sit at surface, and by the last beat before the main practice children should be working with or reasoning with the idea. That is not a staircase: a sequence may move up and down in demand, and a final check may return to central knowledge. If every beat in a finished sequence sits in the surface band, change some of them, and the usual fix is a harder question about the same content, not a different topic.

**Success evidence depends on the task.** Do not require roughly 80% success through every Do beat or lesson type. For a defined repeatable skill, roughly 80% at release is a planning expectation. A diagnostic question may deliberately reveal widespread misunderstanding, and many incorrect answers can be useful evidence. The live teacher responds to the actual class.

**Protect answers without destroying meaningful order.** Avoid accidental item-position patterns that let children guess answers when order carries no teaching meaning. Preserve chronology, procedural sequence, deliberate mathematical patterns and any other order that is itself part of the learning.

Do not ban mini-whiteboards or devices and do not make books or cold calling the default. Normally state the learning action. Name a device, medium or substantive response structure only when it genuinely forms part of the selected activity.

Each entry follows the same shape — what it is, register, best for, why it works, SEND access notes, source — so the agent can compare on like-for-like fields.

## How to pick

1. Settle the intended thinking or checking purpose.
2. Notice the response form's actual demands and supports, such as writing load, spoken language, public performance, reading, movement, fine-motor control, partner dependence or visual structure.
3. Choose the activity family that best serves that thinking and the supplied pupil needs. As a default, a fact suits recall or a sort, a process suits labelling, sketching or sequencing, a concept suits writing or generating a fresh example, and a value or a position suits ranking or talk.
4. Consider variety when repetition would create fatigue or monotony, but do not vary solely to meet a count.

There is no compulsory non-writing activity, fixed content-to-format route or SEND format. Familiar repetition is appropriate when it builds the target skill.

## 1. Recall (retrieval-led — fact and definition surfacing)

**Register:** recall (sometimes writing as the output channel).
**Mechanism:** retrieval practice — the act of pulling a fact from memory strengthens that memory more than re-reading it ever could (Agarwal & Bain, *Powerful Teaching*; Roediger & Karpicke testing-effect work). Use these when the prior chunk is a fact, name, date, definition, or rule that children need to lock in before building on it.

### 1.1 Free Recall
Use free recall deliberately when children have enough secure knowledge and open retrieval is genuinely the intended action. It is demanding and may produce incomplete evidence, so normally prefer a useful structure such as headings, questions, a cap or a partly completed diagram when the aim is to reveal specific knowledge.
**Best for:** secure knowledge where the openness itself is useful.
**Demands and supports:** independent retrieval and organisation; a cap or headings can reduce the starting barrier.

### 1.2 Two Things
*"Write two things you now know about X."* The cap forces selection — children must decide what is worth remembering, which is itself a metacognitive move (Agarwal, retrievalpractice.org strategies library).
**Best for:** consolidating a single Teach chunk fast. Lower stakes than Brain Dump.
**SEND access:** the cap of two protects pupils who freeze at "everything you know."

### 1.3 Whole-class oral rehearsal
**Teacher-owned response routine:** do not select this unless the teacher explicitly requests it.
Choose a deliberate oral rehearsal only when saying the exact word, definition, sequence or response together is the learning activity. State what children rehearse; the teacher chooses how individual responses are gathered.
**Best for:** precise language or a sequence that benefits from collective rehearsal.
**Demands and supports:** spoken production, listening and timing; participation management remains with the teacher.



### 1.5 Last Lesson / Last Week / Last Term
Three quick recall questions structured by time-distance (Boxer, *Teaching Secondary Science*; CogSciSci). Bakes spacing into the Do beat itself.
**Best for:** lesson 2+ in a sequence; opening Do beat after Teach 1.
**SEND access:** the predictability of the structure is the access — pupils know what kind of pull is coming.

### 1.6 Retrieval Roulette
A two-column grid: one column random-cued from this topic, one from earlier topics. Pupils answer both (Boxer & Walker; cogscisci.wordpress.com). Interleaving without effort.
**Best for:** mid-unit lessons where prior topics matter.
**SEND access:** the format is identical week to week — no novel demand on top of recall.

### 1.7 Choral Response
**Teacher-owned response routine:** do not select this unless the teacher explicitly requests it.
Whole-class rehearsal of a definition, date or sequence step where saying the exact response together is the learning activity (Lemov, *TLAC 3.0*, Technique 36, **Call and Response**). State what children rehearse; the teacher chooses how responses are gathered.
**Best for:** locking in vocabulary, sticky knowledge, key facts.
**Demands and supports:** spoken production, listening and timing; participation management remains with the teacher.

### 1.8 Quiz-Quiz-Trade (in-pairs version)
Each pupil has a small flashcard with question on the front, answer on the back. Quiz partner, swap, find new partner (Kagan cooperative learning structures). Adapt without standing-up: pairs at tables turn cards back and forth for 90 seconds.
**Best for:** vocabulary consolidation, fact pairs (capital cities, element symbols, sequence steps).
**SEND access:** every pupil gets multiple turns; one-to-one not whole-class exposure; embedded peer support.

## 2. Talk (oral processing — rehearse before writing)

**Register:** talk.
**Mechanism:** rehearsal in working memory before commit; lower-stakes formulation; oracy as the route to clearer writing (Voice 21 oracy framework; *Teaching WalkThrus* questioning techniques). Use these when the chunk is something children need to say before they can write — explanations, comparisons, opinions, why-questions.

### 2.1 Partner discussion
Use partner discussion when rehearsing, comparing or building an idea together improves the thinking. It is not compulsory before writing; children may write directly when they are ready or when independent formulation is the intended evidence.
**Best for:** explanation, comparison or position-taking where interaction improves the response.
**Demands and supports:** spoken language and partner dependence; a stem may help when it supports the reasoning without supplying the conclusion.

### 2.2 Think-Pair-Share
Use when a thinking-heavy question benefits from individual thinking time before partner rehearsal. State the question and the thinking focus; the teacher chooses how thinking time, pairing and sharing are managed.
**Best for:** thinking-heavy questions where partners need rehearsal before contributing.
**Demands and supports:** independent thinking, spoken rehearsal and listening; partner dependence; participation management remains with the teacher.



### 2.4 Sentence-Stem Talk
Children answer using a displayed stem: *"I think… because…"* / *"I agree with X because…"* / *"I'd like to build on that…"* (Voice 21 oracy framework; *Talk Moves*). The stem removes the cognitive load of how to start and frees pupils to focus on what to say.
**Best for:** discussion-heavy chunks (history significance, RE positions, PSHE values).
**SEND access:** the stem is the scaffold — pupils with language gaps get the structure for free.

### 2.5 Habits of Discussion
Teach and rehearse a small set of moves: agreeing/disagreeing politely, building on, asking a peer to say more (Lemov, *TLAC 3.0*, Technique 45). Over weeks, these become the discussion's grammar. Use for the Do beat when the chunk is opinion or interpretation.
**Best for:** ongoing — embedded into Turn-and-Talk over a term.
**SEND access:** explicit moves > implicit conversational norms; level-up for pupils who don't catch unspoken rules.

### 2.6 Rally Robin (paired listing)
*"Take turns naming features of a rainforest with your partner. One each, back and forth, until I say stop."* (Kagan.) Forces equal-airtime — both partners must speak.
**Best for:** generative recall (list as many as you can), comparison features.
**SEND access:** the strict turn structure protects quieter pupils who get talked over in open partner talk.

### 2.7 Convince Your Partner
*"You believe the Vikings were settlers. Your partner believes they were raiders. Convince them — 60 seconds each."* Forces children to articulate a position with reasons.
**Best for:** balanced-argument chunks, debates in history and PSHE.
**SEND access:** can be allocated the easier position by the teacher; partner support carries language demand.

### 2.8 30-Second Expert
One child gets 30 seconds to teach the partner what they just learnt; then swap. The "protégé effect" — teaching forces structuring of one's own knowledge (cf. Lemov, *TLAC 3.0*, **Habits of Attention**).
**Best for:** after a content-rich Teach slide; works as the bridge into written response.
**SEND access:** the listener role is itself an access route — listening with the goal of swapping is comprehensible practice.

## 3. Write (committed, assessable record)

**Register:** write.
**Mechanism:** writing forces commitment, can reveal misconceptions in the recorded response, and produces a durable record (Lemov, *TLAC 3.0*, **Everybody Writes**, Technique 38; Rosenshine Principle 6 — check for student understanding). Use these when the chunk is something pupils need to commit, not just discuss.

### 3.1 One-Sentence Summary
*"In one sentence, capture what we just learnt."* The constraint is the work — picking the load-bearing idea (Edutopia retrieval-practice strategies). Use sparingly. Do not stack four of these in one lesson.
**Best for:** end of a Teach chunk where there is a single core idea.
**SEND access:** offer a sentence stem ("The most important thing about X is…") for pupils who need the runway.

### 3.2 Stop and Jot
60 seconds, a single prompt: *"What is the difference between X and Y?"* (Lemov, *TLAC 3.0*, Technique 38 sub-variant.) Lower stakes than One-Sentence Summary; not graded for form.
**Best for:** a quick written committal mid-lesson; bridge from Turn-and-Talk to extended writing.
**SEND access:** allow bullet points or dashes; the recording matters, not the prose.

### 3.3 Tweet-Length / Six-Word Summary
*"Summarise what we just learnt in six words. No more."* The cap forces ruthless selection of what matters (Edutopia, *15 Quick Retrieval Practices*).
**Best for:** abstract or wordy chunks where pupils need to identify the core.
**SEND access:** the smallness of six words is the access — no SEND child is being asked to write a paragraph.

### 3.4 Caption This
A photo, painting, diagram, or artefact is on the board. Pupils write a caption that captures what it shows (historytech.wordpress.com; geography image-analysis tradition). Visual input → written output.
**Best for:** history primary-source images, geography photographs, art analysis.
**SEND access:** image gives pupils the content to write about; no recall demand piled on the writing demand.

### 3.5 Annotate the Image / Label the Diagram
A simple unlabelled diagram is provided; pupils add labels, arrows, and short notes from memory (geography.org.uk diagram-from-memory tradition; *Diagrams from Memory* — common in science). The drawing carries the structure; the writing fills it in.
**Best for:** science (parts of a flower, life cycles), geography (river, coast), history (parts of a castle).
**SEND access:** writing is constrained to single words at fixed locations — radically lower demand than open prose.



### 3.7 Hinge or diagnostic question
Use one carefully designed question when the result can genuinely show whether children hold the taught distinction or a predictable wrong rule. Make each wrong option plausible for the age and tied to a specific misunderstanding. State the question and answer logic; the teacher chooses how responses are gathered.
**Best for:** a point where the evidence can genuinely inform the next teaching decision.
**Demands and supports:** reading and committing to an answer; the response method is not prescribed.

### 3.8 Diagnostic Question
A short open question deliberately designed to expose a likely misconception, not just to check recall. *"Marsha says plants get their food from the soil. Is she right? Why?"* (Wiliam; Hodgen.) Surfaces thinking, not facts.
**Best for:** science misconceptions, maths concept lessons, history misreadings.
**SEND access:** the question is concrete (a named person, a claim) — easier than abstract "why does X happen?"

## 4. Visual / Draw

**Register:** visual-draw (with writing as a thin overlay).
**Mechanism:** Use drawing, diagrams, sketches or concept maps when they make children process spatial, structural or sequential learning (Caviglioli, *Dual Coding with Teachers*; Paivio's dual-coding theory; Willingham, *Why Don't Students Like School*, on memory hooks). Drawing does not automatically improve memory and is not automatically accessible: it may reduce a writing barrier for some children while adding fine-motor, drawing or spatial demands for others. State those real demands rather than a broad SEND label.

### 4.1 Quick Sketch
Pupils draw what was just described. *"Sketch the water cycle as I described it. 90 seconds. Stick figures fine."* (Caviglioli.) The sketch reveals whether the mental model is right.
**Best for:** any chunk with a spatial or sequential structure.
**SEND access:** bypasses writing entirely; reveals thinking through a different channel.

### 4.2 Labelled Diagram from Memory
Given a partially-blank diagram, pupils fill in labels (common in science; geography.org.uk). Combines visual recall with low-load writing.
**Best for:** anatomy, geography features, machine parts, anything diagrammatic.
**SEND access:** the diagram does the structural work; pupil supplies only the words.

### 4.3 Stick-Figure Sequence
Three to five small frames — pupils draw the sequence of events (story, scientific process, historical chain) with one-line captions (Caviglioli; comic-strip tradition).
**Best for:** chronology in history, processes in science, plot in literature.
**SEND access:** the frames pre-scaffold sequencing; drawing carries detail, captions stay short.

### 4.4 Sketchnote
A small box in which pupils combine icons, arrows, and a few words to capture the chunk (Caviglioli, *Dual Coding with Teachers*).
**Best for:** end-of-chunk consolidation when content is conceptual.
**SEND access:** every child can produce something; no quality threshold for entry.

### 4.5 Fishbone (Cause and Effect)
Spine = effect; ribs = causes. Pupils fill in (Caviglioli; Ishikawa).
**Best for:** "why did X happen?" history chunks; cause-effect in geography (e.g., flooding).
**SEND access:** the shape carries the logical structure — pupils only need to surface causes, not also organise them.

### 4.6 Mini Concept Map
Three or four concepts in a triangle/square; pupils draw connecting arrows and label the relationships (Caviglioli; concept-mapping research).
**Best for:** mid-unit chunks where ideas are accumulating.
**SEND access:** spatial layout reveals relationships more cleanly than prose; weaker writers thrive.

### 4.7 Frayer Model — quick form
A four-quadrant box for one key word: definition, characteristics, examples, non-examples (Frayer; *AdLit*; Wisconsin DPI). Use the four-quadrant with no separate template needed.
**Best for:** locking in a high-stakes vocabulary word mid-lesson.
**SEND access:** non-examples are powerful for pupils who form half-formed concepts — they sharpen the boundary.

### 4.8 Visual Vote
Pupils draw a small symbol in answer to a question — a tick/cross, a smiley/sad face, an arrow up/down/sideways for *increases, decreases, stays the same*.
**Best for:** binary or three-way decisions; quick check before moving on.
**SEND access:** no writing at all; pure visible commit.

## 5. Sort / Classify

**Register:** sort-classify.
**Mechanism:** Judge a sort by the thinking it requires (Marzano's classifying research; *Ambitious Science Teaching* card-sort guides). Sorting into supplied categories may test recognition; identifying categories, resolving an ambiguous placement or defending a criterion may demand substantial reasoning. Do not assign a cognitive level merely because the activity is called a sort.

Every keyed format in this section passes through the answer-scatter principle in the core notes at the top of this file: scatter the key so each item has to be decided on its own merits.

### 5.1 Card Sort (or paper-strip sort)
A small set of statements/images cut up at the table; pupils sort into pre-named categories — *true / false / not sure*, or *cause / effect*, or *short term / long term* (geography.org.uk *Card Sorting Activities*; *Ambitious Science Teaching*).
**Best for:** category-forming chunks across every content subject.
**SEND access:** reading load is on short strips, not paragraphs; movement of cards is a hands-on bridge.

### 5.2 Odd One Out
Three or four items on the board. *"Which is the odd one out — and why?"* Multiple defensible answers; the *because* is the work (theteachertoolkit.com; common in geography and science).
**Best for:** vocabulary checks, concept boundary tests, cross-unit comparison.
**SEND access:** entry is low (any answer is admissible if justified); rich for stretch pupils.

### 5.3 True / False with Justification
Six short statements. Pupils mark T/F and write a one-line reason (Edutopia retrieval list; Lemov *Hinge Question* family).
**Best for:** recall-heavy chunks where misconceptions are likely.
**SEND access:** the binary T/F is the entry; the justification is the stretch.

### 5.4 Match the Pairs
A small column of terms; a small column of definitions/effects/dates. Pupils draw lines or letter-match (common across primary subjects).
**Best for:** vocabulary, cause–effect pairings, date–event recall.
**SEND access:** the pairing is finite; no production of language, just identification.

### 5.5 Always / Sometimes / Never
Statement on the board; pupils choose *A / S / N* and justify in one line (mathematical reasoning tradition; transfers to history and science).
**Best for:** generalisation tests — *"plants need light to grow"* / *"kings ruled by force"*.
**SEND access:** the three-way choice surfaces gradation; pupils don't have to live in black/white.

### 5.6 Two Truths and a Lie
Teacher (or peer) gives three statements; pupils identify the false one.
**Best for:** recall consolidation that feels like a game.
**SEND access:** familiar party-game framing lowers anxiety; concrete decision, not open prose.

### 5.7 Concept Attainment
Teacher shows examples and non-examples one at a time; pupils try to work out the rule that separates them (Bruner concept-attainment model). Lasts 2–3 minutes if tight.
**Best for:** introducing or consolidating a definition (e.g. *what counts as a vertebrate?*).
**SEND access:** the rule emerges from concrete cases — easier than starting from abstract definition.

## 6. Rank / Position

**Register:** sort-classify (with a values/judgement edge).
**Mechanism:** Ranking, positioning and voting can be useful when committing to and comparing choices genuinely serves the learning (Facing History; Wragg's *Questioning in the Primary School*). They are not automatically harder or better than discussion. Use a clear criterion, enough relevant knowledge and safely distanced choices where personal disclosure could be uncomfortable. Used heavily in PSHE, RE, and history significance work.

### 6.1 Diamond Nine (or Diamond Four)
Nine (or four) statements/causes/factors to arrange diamond-shape: most important at top, least at bottom (theteachertoolkit; standard PSHE/RE practice).
**Best for:** history significance, RE values prioritisation, PSHE decisions.
**SEND access:** the diamond is a visible scaffold; pupils move things rather than write essays.

### 6.2 Continuum Line
Pupils place their response on a line from *agree* to *disagree* (or *true* to *false*, or *most* to *least*), with a one-line *because* underneath (Voice 21 oracy; *Barometer*, Facing History).
**Best for:** PSHE, RE positions, ethical judgements in history.
**SEND access:** the mark is the commit; the prose is small.

### 6.3 Human Bar Chart (standing version) or Seated Bar Chart
Four corners of the room labelled *strongly agree / agree / disagree / strongly disagree*; pupils move to the corner. Without the standing version, label four parts of the room and pupils point/face that direction (Facing History *Four Corners*; *Barometer*).
**Best for:** values chunks in RE and PSHE; opinion-rich history questions.
**SEND access:** physical position is itself the response — no language production required.

### 6.4 Vote with Reason
On the board: *Should the museum return the marbles? A: Yes. B: No.* Pupils choose *A* or *B* and give one reason.
**Best for:** end-of-Teach moments in PSHE, RE, historical interpretation.
**SEND access:** the choice carries the response; reason is one line.

### 6.5 Rank Three
*"Of these three causes, which mattered most?"* Pupils rank 1–2–3 and give a one-line reason for their top choice.
**Best for:** quick commit in history or science (e.g., ranking factors).
**SEND access:** three options is manageable for working memory; no production beyond the ranking.

## 7. Movement / Embodied

**Register:** movement-embodied.
**Mechanism:** Use movement, gesture, mime or physical sequencing when the action meaningfully represents the content (Sullivan, 2018; Macedonia & Knosche, 2011; *Frontiers in Psychology* embodied-learning syntheses). Do not claim automatic retention or SEND benefits. Consider mobility, sensory, anxiety and behaviour barriers and provide an equivalent seated or non-performing role where needed.

### 7.1 Freeze Frame
Pupils (in pairs or fours) create a still image with their bodies showing a moment — *the king signs the Magna Carta, the rainforest before the loggers came, Pip meeting Estella*. 60 seconds to plan, 15 to hold (Drama Primary National Strategy; dramaresource.com).
**Best for:** history, literature, RE narratives, geography scenarios.
**Demands and supports:** physical positioning, spatial awareness, collaboration; spoken language minimal during the freeze; consider mobility, sensory and anxiety barriers; seated or directing role available.

### 7.2 Thought Tracking
Mid-freeze-frame, the teacher taps one pupil's shoulder; they say one line of what their character is thinking (dramaresource.com).
**Best for:** building empathy and perspective in history, literature, RE.
**Demands and supports:** one prepared spoken line; performance demand within a group; teacher chooses how responses are gathered.

### 7.3 Conscience Alley
The class forms two lines; one pupil walks down the middle as a character facing a decision; each pupil whispers one piece of advice (dramaresource.com).
**Best for:** decision moments in history, ethical questions in RE/PSHE.
**Demands and supports:** brief whispered spoken line; listening; movement in a line; teacher chooses grouping and participation.

### 7.4 Gesture-as-Memory
Teach a short gesture with the new term (*"erosion — fingers wearing down a fist"*). Pupils do it with the teacher, then again when the term reappears (Macedonia, 2011).
**Best for:** vocabulary that needs to stick — water cycle stages, scientific processes.
**Demands and supports:** fine-motor gesture, visual and auditory encoding; repetition supports recall; not a guarantee of retention.

### 7.5 Stand If…
**Teacher-owned response routine:** do not select this unless the teacher explicitly requests it.
*"Stand if you can name a Tudor monarch."* *"Stand if you agree."* Whole class commits visibly without writing (common across primary; cf. Lemov's **Show Me** family).
**Best for:** quick poll, energy reset between Teach chunks.
**Demands and supports:** standing and physical response; binary decision; teacher chooses whether standing is appropriate and offers seated alternative.

### 7.6 Human Sequencing
Six pupils each hold a card with one event/step on it; they arrange themselves in order at the front while the class judges (Drama Primary National Strategy; common in primary).
**Best for:** chronology in history, life cycles in science, steps in geography processes.
**Demands and supports:** reading a short card, physical ordering, collaboration; class judges; consider space and participation barriers.

### 7.7 Mime It
Pupils mime a concept (*photosynthesis, weathering, evaporation, a Greek god's attribute*) for a partner to identify.
**Best for:** vocabulary stickiness with active retrieval.
**Demands and supports:** physical gesture, partner interpretation; no writing; performance demand varies; seated alternative available.

## 8. Generative

**Register:** generative.
**Mechanism:** Producing, explaining, making or teaching can support powerful learning when children have enough knowledge and the activity makes them retrieve, organise and use the intended content (Slamecka & Graf, 1978; consistent with Willingham's "memory is the residue of thought"). An active-looking or generative task is not automatically deeper than explanation, demonstration, reading or recognition; judge what children actually think about and do. Use these when the chunk has been understood and now needs to be used.

### 8.1 Write the Question
Reverse a recall task: *"I'll tell you the answer is 1066. Write me three questions where 1066 would be the answer."* Generates depth of association (Agarwal's student-generated-questions strand).
**Best for:** late in a Teach sequence when pupils have the facts.
**SEND access:** the answer is given; the cognitive load lives in the question-shaping.

### 8.2 Predict the Next Slide
*"From what we've just learnt, what do you predict will happen next? Write one prediction."* Activates prior knowledge for the upcoming Teach (Rosenshine — connecting new to prior).
**Best for:** the seam between Teach chunks; gets pupils thinking ahead.
**SEND access:** prediction has no wrong answer at the entry level — anything reasoned earns the credit.

### 8.3 Apply to a New Case
*"We've just seen how the Romans built roads. Where else would you build a road like that? Why?"* Forces transfer (Willingham — transfer requires deep structure; Boxer on responsive teaching).
**Best for:** generalisation chunks in geography, science, history.
**SEND access:** scaffold with a partner first (Turn and Talk) if pupils freeze on application.

### 8.4 Worked Example with a Missing Step
Show pupils a near-complete reasoning chain with one step missing; pupils write what fills the gap (Sweller's worked-example effect; Rosenshine Principle 5).
**Best for:** reasoning sequences in science, RE arguments, historical inference.
**SEND access:** the structure is given; only one piece of thinking is being asked for.

### 8.5 Spot the Mistake
A short paragraph or diagram with a deliberate error; pupils find it and fix it (common in maths; transfers everywhere).
**Best for:** consolidation when a misconception is anticipated.
**SEND access:** the mistake-hunt frame is engaging without raising stakes.

### 8.6 Give-an-Example
*"Give me an example — not from my slide — of an invertebrate."* (Or a renewable resource, an unfair law, a moral choice.) Tests whether the concept is portable or just memorised (Lemov, *TLAC 3.0*, **Stretch It**, Technique 9).
**Best for:** after defining a category; reveals depth of understanding fast.
**SEND access:** allow paired think first; one example is enough.

### 8.7 Headline the Lesson
*"In one headline, sum up the most important thing we've learnt so far."* (Edutopia tweet-length tradition; Agarwal *headline test*.) Forces ruthless selection plus a touch of voice.
**Best for:** end of a major Teach chunk; bridge into the lesson's main Practise.
**SEND access:** offer a stem: "How a Roman… / Why a Viking…"

## 9. Metacognitive

**Register:** metacognitive.
**Mechanism:** EEF *Metacognition and Self-Regulated Learning* guidance supports explicit, modelled and scaffolded teaching of planning, monitoring and evaluating strategies inside real subject learning (Agarwal's *Powerful Teaching* fourth lever is feedback-driven metacognition). Useful forms include explaining why an error was tempting, comparing approaches, naming a helpful strategy and identifying what remains unclear. A generic confidence vote or reflection bolt-on is not metacognitive teaching, and no lesson is required to contain one.

### 9.1 Confidence check
A confidence rating reports how secure children feel. It may support reflection, but it is not evidence of what they understand or can do. Use a genuine learning check when actual understanding matters.
**Best for:** task-specific reflection on readiness, strategy or uncertainty.
**Demands and supports:** self-report and metacognitive language; do not treat the rating as attainment evidence.

### 9.2 Muddiest Point
*"What is still muddiest about this?"* 60 seconds (Mosteller's classic; Brown University Sheridan Center).
**Best for:** after a complex Teach chunk; teacher uses the responses to drive the next chunk.
**SEND access:** explicitly invites confusion as legitimate — protective for pupils who normally mask gaps.

### 9.3 What-I-Knew vs What-I-Know
*"Before this lesson I thought X. Now I think Y."* Surfaces conceptual change explicitly (EEF metacognition guidance; reflective writing tradition).
**Best for:** lessons that target a misconception or shift a view.
**SEND access:** the contrast structure does the heavy lifting; pupils fill two slots.

### 9.4 Three-Two-One
*Three things I learned, two things I wonder, one thing I want to remember.* (Common formative-assessment formats; Sheridan Center.) Compresses recall + curiosity + selection into 90 seconds.
**Best for:** end-of-lesson consolidation; mid-lesson if you want a wider sweep.
**SEND access:** chunked across three small commits, not one large one.

### 9.5 Why Did I Get That Wrong?
After a Hinge Question, pupils write a one-line explanation of why a wrong answer was tempting — even if they got it right (adapted from Agarwal's feedback-driven metacognition). Teaches that misconceptions are common and namable.
**Best for:** after a diagnostic-question chunk.
**SEND access:** normalises errors; for SEND pupils whose self-image is fragile around being wrong, this is high-value culture-building.

### 9.6 Thumbs-Direction
**Teacher-owned response routine:** do not select this unless the teacher explicitly requests it.
Pupils show thumbs up / sideways / down to *I get it / I'm fuzzy / I'm lost* (variant of Lemov's **Show Me**, *TLAC 3.0*, Technique 8). No whiteboards needed.
**Best for:** lightning-fast mid-Teach check; whole class in 5 seconds.
**SEND access:** visible commit without writing; teach pupils that "sideways" is a respected answer.

### 9.7 Think-Aloud Reverse
The teacher has modelled thinking aloud during the Teach; in the Do, a pupil thinks aloud through the same kind of problem while the class watches (EEF *Metacognition* guidance — explicit modelling becomes pupil rehearsal). 60–90 seconds.
**Best for:** science reasoning, historical inference, RE arguments.
**SEND access:** the modelling has already been done; pupil only echoes the structure with new content.
