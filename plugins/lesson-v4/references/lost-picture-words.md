# Words that point at a picture that never came

Read this when a picture your resource names is terminally unavailable
(`unsatisfied` or `omitted`), or when your prompt says the picture stage is
`unavailable`.

The lesson was written before anyone searched for its pictures, so its wording
points at them: `Look at this sponge.`, `This photograph shows a place like it,
at night.`, `These five pictures tell the story`. When the picture is lost and
the words are kept exact, the teacher is told to point at nothing and the class
reads a caption for an empty card. Six of twenty lessons were delivered like
that on 7 October 2026. You are the only one who knows which pictures arrived,
so these words are yours to put right. Nothing else about the wording is.

## Which lane the lost picture is in

Ask what a child could no longer do without it.

**The picture was an extra.** The teaching stands on the words and the picture
only showed it: a sponge beside the meaning of `permeable`, a bronze figure
mentioned in passing, one of five story cards. Put the words right, as below.

**The task cannot be done without it.** Children were to look and decide:
`Choose an adjective that is true in the picture`, `Find Rama by his bow`.
Rewording here would quietly hand over a task with no right answer, and the
page would look finished. Leave the task and its wording exactly as they are.
An `essential: true` picture reaches the lesson designer through the
content-gap picture wave, who finds another route to the same learning. When
that wave is already spent, keep the task whole, put one plain line at the top
of that slide's speaker notes (`This task needs a picture of a tall tree in a
field at night. None was found: show one before the class starts.`) and name
the slide in your completion report.

## Putting the words right (the first lane only)

Change the sentences that point, on the pages that picture belonged to, and
leave every other sentence as it was written.

- **On the page.** A sentence whose only job was to introduce the picture goes:
  `This photograph shows a place like it, at night.` A sentence that also
  counts or names the pictures is corrected to what is there: `These five
  pictures` over four pictures and a name card becomes `These five cards`.
  A card that lost its picture stays as a card with its name on it, among the
  cards that kept theirs: the teacher chose that over a blank-topped card and
  over taking the other pictures away (10 October 2026). Only the words change.
- **In the speaker notes.** Say the thing in plain words where the script
  pointed at it: `Look at this sponge.` becomes `Think of a sponge.` Keep the
  teaching around it word for word.
- **Something to bring.** Only when the lost picture was of an ordinary thing a
  teacher could have to hand (a sponge, a raincoat, a coin), put one short line
  at the top of that slide's notes saying so: `If you have one, hold up a real
  sponge and pour a little water on it.` A night sky, a Tudor street or a
  heart inside a chest gets no such line: asking for what cannot be brought is
  only noise.

The new wording never says what the picture would have shown as evidence, and
never states an answer children were to work out.

Name every lost picture and the pages you put right in your completion report,
so the teacher knows before the lesson.

## What holds you to it

`check-repair-scope.py` reads the picture stage's own receipts. On the pages
that named a lost picture, and the other pages of the same design unit, it lets
a sentence change when it carries a pointing word (`picture`, `photograph`,
`look at`, `you can see`, `shows`). Every other sentence, every other page and
every teacher answer is held exactly as before, so a refusal there means the
change went past the words that point.
