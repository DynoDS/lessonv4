# Reasoning Prompts

The shapes a good reasoning question or task can take, the stem clauses that sharpen them, and year-group exemplars to imitate. When an objective supports worthwhile reasoning, every child should encounter it at an appropriate threshold. Do not reserve reasoning for higher attainers or early finishers, and do not bolt a token reasoning task onto a lesson where it serves no real purpose.

Two agents read this catalogue:

- the **lesson-designer**, when it designs the reasoning a whole class meets — the Apply task, the problem-solving questions on the worksheet, the stretch end of a Your Turn. Here a shape is pitched low-threshold–high-ceiling: a prompt every child can begin, and every child can get stuck on.
- the **adaptation-designer**, when it designs reasoning for Below or Greater Depth resource variants. Greater Depth uses the catalogue on the class objective at increased demand. Below may use an appropriate reasoning shape for its selected objective, with the pupil-facing wording, support and reading demand pitched accessibly.

Extending the catalogue to Below does not weaken the whole-class worthwhile-reasoning expectation above.

A reasoning shape is not the same as a Do beat. The small consolidation beats between Teach slides (`do-beats.md`) sometimes share a name with a shape here — Always/Sometimes/Never, Odd one out — but a Do beat is a 1–3-minute move to use a fact before the next chunk arrives, while a reasoning prompt is the question or task children justify their thinking on. Reach for this file when you are designing the reasoning; reach for `do-beats.md` when you are pacing a content lesson's teaching sequence.

## Reasoning prompt types

Choose the reasoning form from the important thinking in the actual objective. Error analysis, comparison, generalisation, boundary testing and other forms are options; procedural or conceptual pairings are useful tendencies, not fixed routes.

| Type | Example move |
|---|---|
| **Spot the mistake** | A named character's attempt that the child judges and explains. Excellent for procedural LOs where canonical misconceptions exist. Frame it open, so the child has to inspect the work before deciding rather than being told the verdict: "Tiny is writing a playscript and wrote his first line like this. Has Tiny done it correctly? Why or why not? How do you know?" or "What advice would you give Tiny?" These stems hold whether or not there is a fault, so the child does the judging. Keep the stem from handing the verdict over: "Where did Tiny go wrong?", "What did Tiny get wrong?" or "Fix it" tell the child a fault is there before they look, so the work collapses into locating a guaranteed error; and a sub-question whose answer is just the lesson's topic ("what is Tiny writing?") adds no thinking, because a class already doing playscripts answers it without a glance at the line. Reserve a fault-naming stem for the case where spotting one subtle, specific planted error is itself the skill ("Tiny says 0.3 + 0.4 = 0.7, so 1/3 + 1/4 = 1/7"). When the aim is to judge quality against the success criteria, keep it open. The named-character framing (White Rose, Herts for Learning) is narratively softer than a neutral wrong answer. |
| **Always, sometimes, never** | State the number set, context or conditions covered by the claim and use deliberate boundary cases. One counterexample proves `not always`; establishing `sometimes` needs at least one valid and one invalid case inside the stated domain. |
| **Prove it / Convince me** | Child shows reasoning, not just an answer. Works in every subject. |
| **Find all solutions** | Use only when the possibility space is finite, achievable for the year group and answerable from taught knowledge. Children work systematically and explain completeness; the answer resource supplies the complete set and a suitable systematic route. |
| **Variation — same structure, one feature changed** | Missing number in a different position, decimal where a whole was, negative where positive was. Makes pupils re-derive the method. |
| **Move between representations** | Moving between representations may be core learning for every child when the relationship is the objective (NCETM, Morgan). It becomes deeper when children explain important connections, select a useful representation or translate in a less familiar situation. A surface change alone is not Greater Depth. |
| **Odd one out** | Accept one well-reasoned, accurate classification when that is enough. Ask for another defensible criterion only when it usefully deepens the learning. Multiple answers count only when they are genuinely supported by the taught content. |
| **What's the same? What's different?** | Two calculations, two diagrams, two representations of the same idea. "What stays the same? What changes?" Anchor stem from Watson and Mason; works in every subject. |
| **Construct easier and harder cases** | Use when changing the case requires children to identify a real structural difficulty. Keep both versions within today's learning rather than accelerating into next-year content. |
| **Generalisation** | "What's the rule? Will it always work? Why?" |
| **Write your own** | Use when creating the question requires genuine understanding of its structure. Do not assume generation is automatically deep. |
| **Unfamiliar context** | Use only when the context creates worthwhile transfer without adding irrelevant reading, background knowledge or novelty barriers. |
| **Compare two methods / answers** | Two worked solutions or two children's claims; which is better and why. For writing and grammar, "rank these three options for impact" is the natural form. |
| **Reverse / working backwards** | Given the answer, find the missing step or number. |
| **Choose and justify the method** | The child picks the efficient strategy from those taught, then says which they used and why. Strong where a lesson teaches several mental strategies (redistribution, adjusting, compensating) and the assessed thing is the *choice*, not just the answer. The sharpening clause is the justification: "which is most efficient here, and how do you know?" |

## Stem clauses that sharpen a prompt

Explanation, justification and proof can reveal method, certainty, connections and misconceptions, but the underlying question still determines the reasoning demand. Adding `because`, `explain` or `how do you know?` does not by itself turn recall or identification into deep reasoning. A listing question becomes "...how do you know you've got them all?" and the child is suddenly working systematically. Once you've drafted a prompt, scan this list and attach whichever clause genuinely sharpens it. Most prompts take one; a few take two.

| Clause | What it does | Where to attach it |
|---|---|---|
| **"…because…"** | Forces justification, not yes/no | After any true/false or comparison |
| **"…convince me without calculating"** | Forces structural reasoning over computation | After a calculation prompt where structure is the point |
| **"…how do you know you've got them all?"** | Turns listing into systematic working | After find-all-solutions or any open enumeration |
| **"…will it always work?"** | Turns a single example into generalisation | After a worked case |
| **"…what stays the same? What changes?"** | Surfaces invariance across a varied sequence | After a variation sequence |
| **"…what if I changed the [3] to a [5]?"** | Forces re-derivation of the method | After any specific calculation |
| **"…give a non-example"** | Tests the boundary of a concept | After a definition or classification |
| **"…make me a Y[X] question and a Y[X+1] question with this answer"** | Two-tier ask — makes the difficulty driver explicit | After "write your own" |

These are not types in their own right — they are the finishing move on a prompt of any type. A "spot the mistake" with "...explain the rule Tiny broke" attached is a sharper prompt than the bare version. An "always/sometimes/never" with "...convince me with a counter-example" attached is a sharper prompt than the bare version.

**Keep connected instructions clear.** Avoid long bundles of unrelated demands, but preserve every action genuinely needed by the task. Where children must answer and justify, calculate and compare, find cases and establish completeness, or complete a multi-part test form, present the connected actions as clear ordered or labelled parts rather than forcing an artificial one-clause limit. The failure is bundling a second imperative onto the question so the two blur together: "Which of these is the bigger turn? Name both." reads as one muddled instruction — is "name both" a second question, a hint, the actual task? The reasoning is *the comparison*, so ask only that and attach a clause that makes them justify it: "Which is the bigger turn? Explain your answer." The "…explain / …because / …how do you know" clause already pulls the child to name what they are comparing as part of giving the reason, so it does the second instruction's job without a second instruction. When you catch yourself appending a "…and also do X" to a reasoning prompt, fold X into the question or replace it with the clause that makes X happen on its own.

## Year-group exemplars

These examples illustrate possibilities rather than gold-standard templates. Before using or adapting one, check mathematical or factual accuracy, year-group demand, prior teaching, the complete answer and avoidable reading or background demands.

- **Y1 — Find all solutions.** "□ + □ = 10. How many different ways? Convince me you have them all."
- **Y2 — Hidden number / property reasoning.** "I'm a two-digit number. My tens digit is double my ones digit. What might I be? Find them all."
- **Y3 — Odd one out.** "Odd one out: 432, 234, 423, 324. Argue at least two ways."
- **Y3 — What's the same? What's different?** "How many different rectangles with whole-number sides have an area of 36 cm²? What about a perimeter of 36 cm? What's the same? What's different?"
- **Y4 — Variation, multi-output.** "Use four 4s and any operations to make every number from 0 to 10."
- **Y4 — Grammar, rank for impact.** "Write three fronted adverbials for the same clause. Rank them for impact. Explain your top choice."
- **Y5 — Spot the mistake (Tiny).** "Tiny says 0.3 + 0.4 = 0.7, so 1/3 + 1/4 = 1/7. Is Tiny right? Convince me."
- **Y5 — Reading, question generation.** "Make up two questions you *can't* answer from this paragraph. What would you need to know?"
- **Y6 — Always/sometimes/never with structural depth.** "75% of 60 is the same as 60% of 75. True or false? Convince me."
- **Y6 — Ratio, true/false on a depth concept.** "If A:B is 3:5, then A is 3/5 of B. True or false? Show me with a bar model."
- **Y3 — Science, spot the flawed test.** "Sam wants to find out which material is most waterproof. He pours a cup of water on the felt and a teaspoon of water on the foil. What has Sam got wrong, and how would you fix his test?"
- **Y4 — History, judge the claim against the evidence.** "Tiny says the Romans made Britain better for everyone who lived here. Use two things we learned today — one that supports Tiny, one that doesn't. Is she right?"
- **Y4 — Geography, always/sometimes/never on a pattern.** "Rivers always get wider as they travel to the sea. Always, sometimes or never true? Use a river we studied to convince me."
- **Y5 — RE, whose voice.** "Two people we read about keep the same festival in different ways. Does that mean one of them is doing it wrong? Explain your answer using both examples."

What makes these strong is that each one tests a concept native to the year group, can be answered from class lesson content alone, and rewards reasoning rather than recall or calculation volume. Aim for the same qualities when you write a reasoning prompt.

## Cross-subject use

The reasoning shapes transfer; the detailed content and standard do not.

- Maths may use variation, counterexamples, systematic search, representation choice and justified method selection.
- English may compare effects, justify structural choices or reason from textual evidence. Until a subject-English file exists, keep the detailed reading, writing and grammar guidance below active.
- **Reading comprehension.** Depth = multi-point inference with multiple pieces of evidence; comparing author's choices across two texts; justifying a competing interpretation; evaluating effect. Pupils writing their own greater-depth question on the extract for a peer is a strong closing move.
- **Writing.** Depth = manipulating structure for effect (shift in formality, flashback, sentence-length variation for tension); emulating a studied author's structure; drafting then redrafting for precision; justifying choices. The Year 6 TAF links greater-depth writing to evidence of wide reading drawn on independently — prompt the child to emulate a specific author.
- **Grammar.** Depth = explaining the effect, not just identifying the feature. "Write three fronted adverbials for the same clause and rank them for impact." "Spot the mistake in this flawed sentence and explain the rule broken." Application in extended writing with justification.
- Science may evaluate a test, predict from a mechanism or explain an anomaly when those fit the taught objective.
- History, Geography and RE may weigh claims, sources, significance, evidence or perspectives using their subject guidance.
- PSHE, Computing, Art, DT, Music and MFL require objective-specific disciplinary judgement rather than a generic formula based on annotation, unfamiliarity or added writing.

Use the matching subject file where one exists. Where one does not, keep the prompt anchored to the knowledge, evidence and performance children actually have.

Changing context or representation is one available reasoning move only when it makes the relevant relationship, choice or transfer genuinely more demanding. A different-looking task is not automatically deeper.
